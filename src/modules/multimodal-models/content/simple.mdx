## The big picture

Picture asking a chat model a question about a photo, such as “Which digit is this?” Before the model can answer, it has a problem to solve. A language model reads text as a line of small pieces called **tokens**. A token is usually a whole word or part of a word. Inside the model, each token is stored as a list of numbers. A photo is different, because it is a grid of tiny coloured dots and not a line of words.

Text, pictures and sound are different kinds of input, and each kind is called a **modality**. A model that takes in more than one kind is called a **multimodal model**. This module shows how a picture gets into the model’s line of tokens. The usual recipe cuts the picture into small square tiles. It turns each tile into a short list of numbers. Then it lines those lists up beside the words of your question. In the most direct design, the model reads the picture’s tiles much the way it reads words.

### Where you have met it

Ideas from this module show up in several places you may know:

- A model that answers questions about a picture must turn that picture into tokens first. One design in this lab, called LLaVA, does exactly that. The lab’s own example asks “Which digit is this?” about a drawing.
- Finding the picture that best fits a phrase you type is a matching job. A design called CLIP is built for matching. It scores how well a picture and a caption go together.
- Speech, video and scanned pages can be handled the same way. You cut them into pieces and turn each piece into numbers. Going deeper shows how.
- This app has its own local assistant, which belongs to a family of models that can accept images. The copy in this app still cannot see anything, and Where it breaks explains why.

### Why it matters

Without a step like this, a language model could only read text. A photo, a chart or a screenshot would be invisible to it. The step also has a price, because a model can hold only a fixed number of tokens at once. Every tile that enters the line takes up room. A single photo can fill a large share of the space that your words also need. The way a picture is cut and passed on decides two things. One is how much detail the model gets, and the other is how much room is left.

A second problem hides underneath the first. The part that reads pictures and the part that reads words have to agree. A photo of a dog and the words “a dog” should get lists of numbers that match. A model gets its numbers through **training**, a long process in which it studies many examples. Each time, it nudges its numbers a little so that it makes fewer mistakes. The training method that builds this agreement is called **contrastive learning**. The last card in the lab shows how it scores a match.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how many tokens a picture becomes, using the picture size and the tile size, plus one optional summary token;
- how a tile becomes a list of numbers by multiplying and adding, plus a small position code;
- what three designs, called CLIP, LLaVA and Flamingo, pass on, and how much model room each one uses;
- how contrastive training rewards the labelled picture–caption matches and penalizes every other pairing in the batch;
- how each picture splits 100% among the captions, and how the temperature sharpens or flattens those shares.

## What it is

### A picture is a grid of numbers

A digital picture is made of tiny squares of colour called **pixels**. Each pixel in a colour photo holds three numbers. They say how much red, green and blue light it gives off. A photo that is 224 pixels on each side has about 50,000 pixels. With three numbers for every pixel, that comes to about 150,000 numbers.

The lab keeps things much smaller so that you can see every number. Its pad is 8 pixels on each side, which makes 64 pixels in all. Each pixel is either ink, stored as 1, or blank, stored as 0. When the lab opens, the pad holds a blocky 5 drawn with 17 inked pixels.

The language models in this course are **transformers**. A transformer reads a line of tokens, and each token is a list of numbers called a **vector**. A grid of pixels is not a line of vectors. So before a transformer can read a picture, something has to cut the picture up and convert it.

The standard way to do that comes from a design called the Vision Transformer, or **ViT**. It has three steps:

1. **Cut** the picture into small square tiles called **patches**, using the same grid of cuts whatever the picture shows.
2. **Turn** each patch into a short list of numbers, its **patch embedding**, using a fixed recipe.
3. **Add** a **position embedding**, a small code that records where the patch came from.

Each patch then becomes one token, and the position code keeps tiles from different places apart. The sections below take the three steps one at a time.

### Step 1: cut the picture into patches

Picture cutting a photo into a grid of square tiles, like a sheet of stamps. The cuts always fall in the same places. A cat, a page of writing and a blank wall are all cut alike. Each tile is called a **patch**, and each patch will become exactly one token.

To count the patches, divide the side of the picture by the side of one tile. That tells you how many tiles fit along each edge. Then square that number, because the tiles fill a square grid with the same count along both edges. The lab writes this out in its **Patch tokens** readout. For the 8×8 pad cut into 4×4 tiles, it shows (8 / 4)² = 4. Two tiles fit along each edge, so 2 × 2 = 4 tiles cover the pad.

The same rule works at real sizes. A picture 224 pixels on each side, cut into 16-pixel tiles, fits 224 / 16 = 14 tiles along each edge. That gives 14 × 14 = 196 patches, so the picture becomes 196 tokens.

Some picture readers add one extra token at the front of the line, called the **CLS token**. It brings the 224-pixel picture to 197 tokens instead of 196. The CLS token holds no pixels of its own. By the end of the reader, though, its numbers often serve as a summary of the whole picture.

Smaller tiles mean more tokens, and bigger tiles mean fewer. On the lab’s pad, 2×2 tiles give 16 tokens of 4 pixels each. At the other end, a single 8×8 tile turns the whole picture into one token.

### Step 2: turn each patch into a list of numbers

Now picture filling in the same short form for every tile, with four questions on it. The first asks how much ink the tile holds. The second asks whether that ink leans left or right. The third asks whether it leans toward the top or the bottom. The fourth asks whether it runs along one diagonal more than the other. Each answer is a number, so every tile ends up as a list of four numbers.

In the lab, those four questions are four **filters**, named **ink**, **left − right**, **top − bottom** and **╲ − ╱**. A filter is a small grid of numbers called weights, with one weight for each pixel in the tile. The **Patch embedding** card draws each filter as a little grid of + and − cells. That way, you can see which pixels count for the answer and which count against it.

To get a filter’s answer, multiply each pixel by the weight in the same spot. Then add up all the results. A blank pixel is 0, so only the inked pixels count toward the answer.

The lab numbers the patches in reading order, with #1 at the top left. The count goes left to right along each row of tiles, then moves down to the next row. Before any filter works, the lab lays the tile’s pixels out in one line. It reads row by row, the way you read a page. For a 4×4 tile, that line holds 16 numbers, and each one is a 0 or a 1. The lab calls this line **x**. The **Image to patches** card shows it as a strip labelled “patch #2 flattened, row by row: x”.

Take patch #2 at the 4×4 tile size, which is the top-right quarter of the pad. It holds the right ends of the 5’s top bar and middle bar. So 3 of its 16 pixels are inked. Two of them sit in the tile’s top row and one sits in its bottom row. All three are on the tile’s left half. Here is how each of the four filters scores the tile:

- **ink:** This filter gives every pixel a weight of 1/16, so its answer is 3 × 1/16, about 0.19. That is the share of the tile that holds ink.
- **left − right:** Each pixel on the left half gets a weight of +1/8. Each pixel on the right half gets −1/8. All three inked pixels are on the left, so the answer is 3 × 1/8 = 0.375. The lab rounds that to 0.38.
- **top − bottom:** This works the same way, with +1/8 for the top half and −1/8 for the bottom. Two inked pixels are on top and one is on the bottom. That leaves one pixel’s worth, 1/8, which the lab rounds to 0.13.
- **╲ − ╱:** This filter gives +1/4 to each pixel on one diagonal and −1/4 to each pixel on the other. One inked pixel lies on each diagonal, so the two cancel out and the answer is 0.00.

Each answer is a **weighted sum**: you multiply every pixel by its own weight and add up the results. A step built only from weighted sums like this is called a **linear map**. The **Patch embedding** card writes the full step in its heading as `z = W·x + position`. Here, W·x is the linear map, and the position code is a separate step that is added on top. In that line, x is the tile’s 16 pixels, and W is the four filters stacked one above another. That is why the card’s table calls each filter a “row of W”. The dot in W·x means “multiply each pixel by its weight and add up the results”. The card does this once for each filter, so W·x is the list of four answers.

This multiply-and-add step is called the **patch embedding**. People also use that name for the list of numbers it makes. One sign that the step is linear is that its answers add up neatly. In patch #2, the two top pixels alone give 2/8 on left − right. The bottom pixel alone gives 1/8. Together they give 3/8.

The same four filters are used on every tile in the picture. That matters, because two tiles with exactly the same pixels always get exactly the same W·x, wherever they sit.

**Where the form picture stops matching:** A person wrote the form’s four questions. A person also set this lab’s four filters so you can read every number. A real picture reader learns its filters during training instead. It starts from random weights, nudges them over many examples, and uses far more filters. A reader called ViT-B/16 uses 768 learned filters, each covering a 16×16 tile in three colours. So each of its tokens is a list of 768 numbers. Those learned filters come without names, so nobody wrote down which question each one asks.

### Step 3: add a position code

Turning tiles into numbers creates a new problem. Nothing in a tile’s four numbers says where the tile was. Set **Patch size** to 2×2 and look at patches #2 and #3. They sit side by side in the top row. Each one holds the same two inked pixels across its top, so both get exactly the same W·x. Both read 0.50 for ink and 1.00 for top − bottom, with zeros for the other two filters. A 2×2 tile has only 4 pixels, so the weights are bigger. Ink uses 1/4 and each half uses 1/2. Two inked pixels give 2 × 1/4 = 0.50 for ink. Both sit on top, so they give 2 × 1/2 = 1.00 for top − bottom.

That would be a real problem, because the step that comes later has no built-in sense of order. That step is **attention**, where each token gathers information from the other tokens. On its own, attention treats the tokens like a bag of pieces. Two identical tokens would look like the same piece twice, with no sign of which one came first.

The fix is a small code, added to each token, that depends only on the tile’s row and column. This code is the **position embedding**. The lab calls it the position code and shows it in the “+ position” column of the **Patch embedding** table. In this lab the code is a fixed pattern of small numbers. They rise and fall like a smooth wave across the rows and columns. That wave shape is called a sine wave, so the lab calls it a sine pattern. A real ViT usually learns its position codes during training instead of using a fixed pattern.

Back at 4×4, patch #2’s position code adds 0.07 to the first three answers. It subtracts 0.07 from the last one. The table on the card has one row for each filter, with columns headed “W·x”, “+ position” and “= token”. Reading across each row gives these sums:

- **ink:** The sum is 0.19 + 0.07 = 0.26.
- **left − right:** The sum is 0.38 + 0.07 = 0.45.
- **top − bottom:** The sum is 0.13 + 0.07 = 0.20.
- **╲ − ╱:** The sum is 0.00 − 0.07 = −0.07.

So patch #2 enters the model as the four numbers `[0.26, 0.45, 0.20, −0.07]`. At 2×2, the tokens for the twin patches #2 and #3 end in 0.04 and −0.04. Their first three numbers match. The position code is the only thing that tells them apart.

### What happens next: the vision encoder

In a real model, the tokens now pass through a **vision encoder**, a transformer built to read pictures. A transformer works in **layers**, and each layer is one round of processing for every token. A vision encoder stacks many layers one after another. Inside it, attention lets each tile’s token gather information from the other tiles. Each token ends up carrying something about its neighbours as well as its own pixels. A vision encoder is usually trained on its own first, often with the contrastive method described below. Only then do people connect it to a language model. This lab stops before this point, so no encoder layers run here.

### Three ways to hand a picture to a language model

A language model can hold only a fixed number of tokens at once. Picture a notebook with a fixed number of lines. Every token fills one line, and nothing more can be written once the pages run out. The notebook stops matching in one way. In a real notebook, each new line takes the same effort as the last. A model compares every token with every other token. So each extra token adds more work than the last, as Why it’s here explains. The notebook size is called the model’s **context**, or context window. This lab assumes a context of 4,096 tokens.

Three common designs join a vision encoder to a language model. They differ in what they pass on and in how much of the notebook they fill.

- **CLIP:** This design trains a picture reader and a caption reader side by side. It uses contrastive learning until a matching picture and caption get a high score together. Its picture reader first makes one vector for each tile, like the other designs. In the lab’s starting setup, each one is 1,024 numbers long. CLIP then boils all of those down into one vector for the whole picture, a step called **pooling**. It converts that vector to 768 numbers. CLIP compares that one vector with caption vectors. So CLIP is good for search but does not write sentences. None of its output enters a language model.
- **LLaVA:** This design takes the patch vectors from a CLIP picture reader. It passes each one through a small converter called a **projector**. The projector changes each vector to the length the language model uses for words. In a version called LLaVA-1.5, that goes from 1,024 numbers to 4,096. The 4,096 is the length of each token. It happens to match the 4,096-token notebook size above, but the two numbers measure different things. The results go straight into the prompt as **image tokens**. The model treats them like any other tokens, and they fill the context like any other tokens too.
- **Flamingo-style:** These models squeeze a picture’s vectors down to a fixed set of 64, whatever its size. They add extra layers to the language model so the words can read those 64 vectors. This is called **cross-attention**. Ordinary attention lets each word gather information from its own line. Cross-attention lets it gather information from a separate set of vectors outside the line.

One way to keep them apart is to ask where the picture goes. LLaVA pastes the picture into your message tile by tile. Flamingo-style keeps 64 summary vectors beside the message for the words to read. CLIP never writes a message at all, because it only rates how well a picture fits a caption.

Those choices decide the cost. A picture 336 pixels on each side, cut into 14-pixel tiles, makes 576 patch tokens. A LLaVA-style model puts all 576 of them into its 4,096-line notebook. That is about one seventh of the notebook for one picture. The other two designs keep the picture out of the line entirely.

### How contrastive learning works

CLIP’s picture reader and caption reader start out unmatched. The numbers one gives a dog photo do not link to the numbers the other gives “a dog”. Contrastive learning is how the two are trained to line up. It works on a **batch**, a group of pictures that each come with their own caption. The method runs in five steps, and the **Contrastive pairs** card lets you check each one with real numbers.

**Step 1:** Every picture and every caption becomes a vector. In the card, a person wrote these vectors by hand, with four numbers each. The four numbers stand for four topics: vehicle, animal, sky and structure. Picture A, a ferry at a dock, gets 0.9 for vehicle and 0.7 for structure. Its other two numbers are small. Caption 1, “a ferry tied up at the dock”, gets 0.8 for both vehicle and structure. It gets almost nothing for animal and sky.

**Step 2:** Every picture is scored against every caption, using a score called the **cosine similarity**. It checks whether two vectors have the same mix, meaning the same topics in roughly the same proportions. A score near 1 means nearly the same mix, and a score near 0 means almost nothing in common. Only the mix counts, not the size. For example, [1, 0, 0, 1] and [2, 0, 0, 2] score exactly 1. One list is twice as big as the other. Picture A and caption 1 both lean heavily on vehicle and structure, so they score 0.97. Caption 2, “a dog waiting on the pier”, shares little with picture A beyond structure. That pair scores 0.41.

Lay the scores out as a grid, with pictures as rows and captions as columns. The three true pairs sit on the diagonal from top left to bottom right. Every other cell holds a **negative pair**. That is a picture and a caption from the same batch that do not belong together. Three pairs make 3 × 3 = 9 cells, so 6 of them are negative pairs. The count grows fast, because a batch of 100 pairs would have 100 × 100 − 100 = 9,900 negative pairs.

**Step 3:** Each row is turned into shares. For each picture, the card asks “which caption fits this picture?” It splits 100% among the three captions and gives the biggest share to the highest score. It also asks the reverse question down each column, which is “which picture fits this caption?” The step that turns a row of scores into shares that add up to 100% is called a **softmax**. Bigger scores get bigger shares, and the wider the gap between two scores, the more lopsided their shares become. A small gap on its own gives fairly even shares. That is why the temperature, described next, stretches the gaps first.

Before the softmax, the card divides every score by a setting called the **temperature**. It is written τ, the Greek letter tau. The temperature controls how far apart the scores are when the softmax sees them. The section after these five steps shows why that matters so much.

**Step 4:** The card gives a penalty that grows as the labelled match’s share shrinks. It is zero only at 100%. The labels say which caption truly belongs to each picture. For every row and every column, the card turns the labelled match’s share into a penalty. The penalty climbs faster and faster as that share shrinks:

- **Full share:** If the labelled match gets the full 100%, the penalty is 0, because nothing could be better.
- **81% share:** If it gets 81% of the share, the penalty is about 0.21, which is still small.
- **One third:** If it gets one third, which is an even three-way split, the penalty is about 1.10.
- **5% share:** If it gets only 5% of the share, the penalty is about 3, which is large.

A **loss** is a training score where lower is better. The card builds its loss from these penalties in two directions. It averages the penalties across the three rows to get **Image → caption**. It averages them down the three columns to get **Caption → image**. The **Contrastive loss** is the average of those two. So the loss is low only when each labelled match takes a big share of its row and column. At the starting temperature of 0.20, the rows give 0.241 and the columns give 0.229. The loss is their average, 0.235.

The **Chance loss, ln 3** readout shows 1.099, the penalty for an even three-way split. It is the loss you would get if every score in a row were equal. Then nothing would tell the captions apart. The “ln” in the label is short for natural logarithm. The lab uses that math function to turn a share into a penalty. So ln 3 is the penalty for a one-third share. You need the pattern in the list above, not the function itself.

**Step 5:** During training, each small change to the two readers is chosen to lower the loss. In practice, that raises the scores of the labelled pairs and lowers the scores of all other pairings. The paper that introduced CLIP, by Radford and colleagues in 2021, describes the goal in the same terms. The goal is to raise the scores of the real pairs and lower the scores of every incorrect pairing. Over a huge number of pairs, that steady pressure puts the true pairs on top. The card in this lab trains nothing, though. It scores six fixed vectors so that you can see what the loss measures.

### How the temperature changes the shares

Dividing by the temperature changes how far apart the scores are before the softmax sees them. Dividing by 0.20 is the same as multiplying by 5. Every score and every gap becomes five times bigger. Dividing by 0.05 multiplies them by 20 instead, while dividing by 1.00 changes nothing at all. The softmax cares about the gaps between scores. Stretching the gaps hands more of the share to the leader.

Picture C, a ship under storm clouds, shows this best, because one rival caption scores close to its own. Its own caption, “dark clouds over the water”, scores 0.93. The ferry caption scores 0.74, because a ship is a vehicle too. The gap between those two scores is only about 0.18, though the rounded scores make it look like 0.19. The temperature decides how much that small gap counts.

- **At 0.20:** The gap is stretched five times, to almost 1. The labelled caption gets 70% of the row, and the ferry caption gets 28%.
- **At 0.05:** The gap is stretched twenty times, to almost 4. The labelled caption now gets 98%, and the ferry caption only 2%.
- **At 1.00:** The gap stays at about 0.18, so the two shares come out close together. The labelled caption gets 43%, and the ferry caption gets 36%.

A low temperature makes every row sharp, so the leader takes nearly everything. A high temperature makes every row flat, so the shares spread out. When the labelled caption is already the leader, sharpening helps it and the loss falls. When the labelled caption is not the leader, sharpening works against it and the loss rises.

This temperature shares its name with the one that sets how random a chatbot’s replies are. It is a separate setting with a different job. Here it only decides how sharply the scores are turned into shares while the loss is worked out.

## Why it’s here

A plain language model has one way in. That door is a line of tokens, each a vector of the same length, inside a fixed-size context. LLaVA-style models push the picture through that door as image tokens. Flamingo-style models build a second door instead, made of added cross-attention layers. The picture then reaches the words without joining the line. CLIP does not feed a language model at all, because it only matches pictures with captions. Whichever route a design takes, the goal is to get the picture in without losing what matters about it. The job breaks into three smaller problems.

The first problem is turning pixels into something a transformer can use. You have met a close cousin of the answer before. In The architecture zoo, a 3×3 filter slid across the drawn digit one square at a time. The same 9 weights were used at all 64 places. A patch embedding is close to that, because it applies a set of filters once per tile. It jumps a whole tile at a time instead of one square, though. So the tiles never overlap, and each tile gives one answer per filter. That is how the filter idea from the zoo connects to the transformers you have studied since.

The second problem is cost. Image tokens use the same context, attention and computing work as words. Attention compares every token in the line with every other token. Say the line doubles. Then there are twice as many tokens, and each one has twice as many others to compare with. So the attention work grows about 2 × 2 = 4 times. That is why the picture size and the tile size matter so much.

The third problem is agreement, and the designs share that work instead of each starting from nothing. LLaVA, for example, does not train its own picture reader from scratch. It reuses one that CLIP already trained with contrastive learning. Its picture numbers therefore already carry meanings learned from captions. The projector only has to learn to translate them into the language model’s own numbers.

### Read one token

Set **Patch size** back to 4×4, which also sends **Selected patch** back to the first patch. Move it to “#2 of 4”. The **Patch embedding** card should now show the worked example, with “3 of 16 inked” under the tile. Its “= token” column should read 0.26, 0.45, 0.20 and −0.07.

Now move to patch #1, the top-left quarter of the pad. It holds 8 inked pixels, which is half the tile, so **ink** reads 0.50. The other three filters all read 0.00, because its ink is balanced. It has as much ink on the left as on the right. It has as much on top as on the bottom. The two diagonals hold the same amount too.

Then change patch #2 yourself by clicking the cell in row 2, column 8. Count rows from the top and columns from the left, starting at 1. That cell lies in the right half of the tile. The **left − right** answer drops from 0.38 to 0.25, because the new pixel brings a weight of −1/8. Click the same cell again to put the pixel back where it was.

Last, set **Patch size** to 2×2 and step **Selected patch** between “#2 of 16” and “#3 of 16”. The W·x column stays the same for both. The “+ position” column changes only in its last row, from 0.04 to −0.04. That is the only difference in the “= token” column.

### See the line of tokens

In **Token sequence**, the line starts with the tag USER:, which marks the person’s turn. Next comes one chip for each picture tile. After the chips come the words of the question. The line ends with the tag ASSISTANT:, which is where the model’s reply would go. A LLaVA-style model uses this layout, with the picture’s tokens between the user tag and the question. The chips are numbered in reading order, left to right and top to bottom. Clicking any chip selects that patch. In this lab each token is still only 4 numbers long. LLaVA-1.5 converts its picture vectors from 1,024 numbers to 4,096.

### Scale it up

Move to **Real-scale arithmetic**, which does the same counting at real sizes. Set **Image side** to 224px, where px is short for pixels, and **Encoder patch** to 16×16. **Patch tokens** reads (224 / 16)² = 196. With **CLS token** set to “+1 CLS”, **Encoder sequence** reads 197. If you switch it to “none”, the extra summary token disappears and you are left with 196. Switch it back to “+1 CLS” before going on.

Next, set **Image side** to 336px and **Encoder patch** to 14×14, which is the lab’s starting setup. There are 336 / 14 = 24 tiles along each edge, so Patch tokens reads 576. **Values per patch** reads 14·14·3 = 588, where the dot means times, so 14 × 14 × 3 = 588. That counts the numbers in one colour tile before any filter sees it. Each pixel has three colour numbers. The card’s heading names the picture reader, ViT-L/14, and the number after the slash is its tile size.

Two more settings are worth a look. The first is 448px with 14×14 tiles, where a single picture makes 1,024 tokens. That is a quarter of the whole 4,096-token context. The second is 336px with 32×32 tiles, where the tiles do not fit evenly, because 336 / 32 = 10.5. The lab rounds up to 11 tiles per side. It acts as if the picture were resized or padded with a blank border to 352 pixels. Patch tokens then reads (352 / 32)² = 121. The **Pixels** stage, first in the list of stages described next, says this.

### Compare the three designs

First set **Image side** back to 336px, **Encoder patch** to 14×14 and **CLS token** to “+1 CLS”. The shapes below are for that setup. Then switch **Connector** between “CLIP (contrastive)”, “LLaVA (projector)” and “Flamingo-style (cross-attn)”. Read the list of five stages each time. Each stage shows a shape, such as “576 × 588”, which means 576 tiles with 588 numbers each. The first three stages are the same for all three designs. The third one, **ViT-L/14 encoder**, is the vision encoder. Its shape of 577 × 1,024 counts the 576 patches plus the CLS token, each now 1,024 numbers long.

- **LLaVA (projector):** The **MLP projector** stage drops the CLS token. It converts each of the 576 patch vectors to 4,096 numbers. The label MLP names this kind of small converter, which here has two layers. The last stage, **LLM sequence**, puts 576 image tokens into the line beside the text. LLM is short for large language model. **Context share of 4,096** reads 14.1%, the same one-seventh share you met earlier.
- **CLIP (contrastive):** The **Pooled + projected** stage turns the whole picture into one vector of 768 numbers. Pooled means the card combines the tile vectors into one. Projected means it converts that one to a new length. The **Contrastive score** stage then compares it with each caption, giving one number per caption. The context share reads “0% (not in the sequence)”.
- **Flamingo-style (cross-attn):** The **Perceiver resampler** stage turns the picture into 64 vectors. They are shown as 64 × d, where d stands for each vector’s length. The **Gated cross-attention** stage lets the text read those 64 vectors. The context share again reads 0%.

### Match pictures to captions

Finish on **Contrastive pairs**. Its rows are three pictures. A is a ferry at a dock and B is a dog on a pier. C is a ship under storm clouds. Its columns are the three captions, numbered 1 to 3. Each caption was written to match one of the pictures. Each cell shows the similarity score in bold. Next to it are two shares. One is the caption’s share of its row. The other is the picture’s share of its column. A cell is tinted more strongly as its row share grows. The cell the labels call the match is outlined and marked “labelled match”.

A badge on the card reads “hand-authored vectors”. The table at the bottom lists all six vectors by topic. That table lets you see why each pair scores the way it does. Each picture’s highest score sits on its own caption. So **Images that rank their match first** reads 3 of 3. That readout looks only at the scores, so it stays at 3 of 3 at every temperature. Only the labels can change it. At the starting temperature of 0.20, **Contrastive loss** reads 0.235, far below the **Chance loss, ln 3** of 1.099.

**Try this:** Drag **Temperature τ** down from 0.20 to 0.05. Contrastive loss falls to 0.006, because each row’s share piles onto its labelled caption. Then drag it up to 1.00. The shares flatten, and each labelled caption now holds under half of its row, at 44%, 46% and 43%. With the rows that flat, the loss rises to 0.813, much closer to the chance loss.

Then set **Which pairs count as matches** to “shuffled captions”, and notice that not a single score changes. Only the labels change. They now say picture A goes with caption 2, B with caption 3, and C with caption 1. At τ = 0.20, the loss jumps from 0.235 to 2.582, well above the chance loss. Images that rank their match first drops to 0 of 3.

While the labels are still shuffled, lower the temperature again. This time the loss climbs, passing 9 at 0.05. The sharper rows pour their share onto the captions that the labels now call wrong.

## What to notice

- **The grid ignores what is drawn.** At 4×4, the 5’s top bar crosses a patch border. Patches #1 and #2 each hold part of it. Nothing is lost, because later attention layers can bring the pieces together. Still, no single token holds the whole bar.
- **A balanced tile can score 0 without being blank.** Patch #1 is half inked, yet it reads 0.00 on all three contrast filters. Its ink is spread evenly. Only the ink filter shows that the tile is not empty, with a reading of 0.50.
- **Identical tiles differ only by their position code.** The same W is used on every tile. So twin tiles get the same W·x wherever they sit.
- **Doubling the side makes four times as many tokens.** With 16×16 tiles, a 224-pixel picture makes 196 tokens and a 448-pixel picture makes 784. The grid grows along both edges at once, so twice the side means 2 × 2 = 4 times the tiles.
- **Attention work grows faster still.** That work grows with the square of the line’s length. So four times as many tokens means about sixteen times as much attention work.
- **Only LLaVA spends context.** It is the only one of the three designs that puts the picture into the language model’s own line. The other two keep it out.
- **The loss compares scores with labels.** Shuffling the captions changes no score at all, yet the loss jumps. It measures how well the scores agree with the labels, not how alike the pictures and captions are.
- **A close rival costs the most.** Picture C’s ship scores 0.74 with the ferry caption, because a ship is a vehicle too. At 0.20 that rival leaves C’s own caption only 70% of the row, the smallest share of the three. So C adds the most to the loss. A lower temperature shrinks the rival’s share, but it also makes any wrong label cost far more.

Picture a matching quiz where you pair three baby photos with three classmates’ names and get every one right. But the teacher’s answer key was shuffled before marking. Your mark collapses even though your answers never changed. The mark only measures how well your answers agree with the key. The contrastive loss works the same way. The quiz stops matching at one point, though. Your answers stay put, while in training the readers are changed to lower the loss. With a shuffled key, they would be pushed to match the wrong pictures and captions. That is why wrong labels in the training data are so costly.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- The picture is only 8×8, with one black-and-white **channel**, meaning one number per pixel. Real readers see three colour channels, the red, green and blue numbers, at hundreds of pixels per side.
- A person set the four filters in W by hand. The position code is a fixed sine pattern, and nothing is trained. No vision encoder layers run, so the tokens never mix with their neighbours.
- **Token sequence** shows each word as one whole token so that you can read it at a glance. A real tokenizer, the tool that splits text into tokens, often breaks words into smaller pieces.
- **Real-scale arithmetic** uses fixed vector lengths for each family of picture readers and a single 4,096-token context. It computes only shapes. CLIP appears there only as the shape of its output. Cross-attention appears only as the place where the 64 vectors go.
- The original Flamingo used a different picture reader, called NFNet. The card shows a ViT for every design.
- **Contrastive pairs** uses six hand-written lists of four numbers, not the output of real picture and caption readers. The scores, shares and loss are computed for real, but nothing is trained. So the card shows what the loss measures and not how real readers learn to lower it.
- With only three pairs, each picture in **Contrastive pairs** has only two negative pairs to beat. A real batch gives each picture many more.

### Where the real mechanism fails

- **A reader sees only what survives resizing.** A model may shrink a large photo to a 336-pixel square before cutting it. Small text or fine detail can vanish. That is why newer models split a big picture into smaller ones, or accept different sizes.
- **Training makes features useful, not perfect.** Contrastive training and later training on instructions make picture features useful for chosen tasks. How well that works is measured, not guaranteed. Models can still miscount objects, mix up left and right, or misread text inside a picture.
- **Every other pairing counts as wrong.** Picture a batch holding two photos of dogs waiting on piers. Each has the caption “a dog on a pier”. That caption fits both photos. Yet the loss counts it as wrong for whichever photo it was not paired with. This is one reason the labels in a dataset matter, especially in one scraped together automatically from the web.
- **Pictures are untrusted input.** Any text printed inside a picture becomes part of what the model reads. The model has no reliable way to tell those printed words apart from the request itself. That includes instructions someone has hidden there. This attack is called **image-borne injection**, because the instructions travel inside the image.

Picture a friend who asks a model to describe a poster. Tiny print on the poster tells the model to ignore your friend and do something else. The model may follow those printed words, because to the model they are more input to follow. [Failure modes, safety, and security](#/failure-modes-safety) shows the same attack through retrieved text. That is text a model pulls in from documents or searches. This lab has no picture attack, but the route is the same.

### The model in this app

**A common mix-up:** You might assume that if a model family reads images, every copy of it can. This app’s local assistant is Qwen3.5 2B, where the 2B means it has about 2 billion learned numbers. It belongs to a family that accepts images. But its picture reader and projector come in a separate file, often named `mmproj`. The app downloads only the language-model file and never sends images. So the local assistant cannot see this pad or anything else.

## Going deeper

### Where the designs came from

- **2020, Vision Transformer:** The tile idea comes from the Vision Transformer, or ViT. Its paper was titled “An Image is Worth 16x16 Words”. The title makes the same point as this lab: each 16×16 tile becomes one token, like a word.
- **2021, CLIP:** This design trained its picture reader and caption reader together on 400 million picture–caption pairs.
- **2022, Flamingo:** This design kept a language model’s own settings fixed and added two new parts. One is a Perceiver resampler that squeezes the picture into 64 vectors. The other is a set of gated cross-attention layers. A gate here is a learned dial. It sets how strongly the new layers can change what flows through the language model.
- **2023, LLaVA:** This design connected a CLIP picture reader to a language model with a single linear projector. LLaVA-1.5 switched to a two-layer MLP projector and a CLIP reader called ViT-L/14, working at 336 pixels.

### Beyond still pictures

Sound, video and documents reuse the same ideas. They cut the input into pieces, turn each piece into numbers, and let attention mix them. What changes is how many tokens that takes, and so what it costs.

For sound, the input is first turned into a **spectrogram**, which is a picture of sound. Time runs along one edge, and frequency, meaning how high or low a pitch is, runs along the other. Brightness shows how much energy the sound has at each moment and pitch. Whisper, a speech model from 2022, first resamples audio to 16,000 Hz. That means 16,000 measurements of the sound every second.

Whisper works on 30-second segments and starts a new spectrogram column every 10 milliseconds. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second. That makes 100 columns per second, and each column holds 80 numbers, one for each band of pitch. Each column also describes a slice of sound 25 milliseconds long, so neighbouring slices overlap. A 30-second segment therefore comes to 3,000 columns. Whisper’s encoder then halves that count once, leaving 1,500 tokens, which is 50 for every second of sound.

For video, the input is a stack of frames. Frames are the still pictures that play one after another to make a clip. Say a model took one frame per second and cut each frame like the 336-pixel picture in Real-scale arithmetic. One minute would then be 60 × 576 = 34,560 tokens, which is more than eight times this lab’s 4,096-token context. That rate of one frame per second is a made-up example. Real models bring the count down by skipping, merging or pooling frames. Pooling means combining several into one.

For documents, a model can pull out the text first and pass it on as ordinary text tokens. It can use OCR, short for optical character recognition, or a tool that reads the page layout. Or the model can take the page as a picture. At 448 pixels with 14-pixel tiles, that is (448 / 14)² = 1,024 tokens. That count stays the same whether the page is full of writing or blank. Small print can vanish in the resize.
