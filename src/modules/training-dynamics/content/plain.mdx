## The big picture

Picture two classmates who both score 100% on every practice quiz in the back of their maths textbook. On
the real test, though, one of them does well and the other falls apart. The first one had learned how to
solve the problems, while the second one had memorized the answers to those exact questions. If all you
ever saw were their practice scores, you could never tell the two of them apart.

Training an AI model has the same trap, since a model learns by adjusting the numbers inside it until it
does well on a pile of examples. It can get there by picking up the real pattern behind the examples, or
by memorizing the examples themselves, odd details and all. This module is about telling those two apart,
catching the moment a model starts sliding from one to the other, and knowing what to do about it.

The main idea fits in one sentence: keep some examples out of training, and score the model on those as
well. Training only ever looks at the score on its own examples, so the score on the held-back ones is
your honest check on what the model really learned.

### Where you have met it

Whenever you read that a new AI model did well on a test, often called a benchmark, that score was
measured on questions held back from its training. The score only means something if those questions were
truly kept out, since otherwise it measures memorizing instead of skill, and nobody can tell from the
number alone.

The chatbots you may have used face the same risk at one stage of their training. The large models behind
them are first trained on huge amounts of text, and can then be trained again on a much smaller set of
examples, called fine-tuning. That smaller round is one of the places where memorizing becomes a real danger.
The checks you will practise in this lab are the ones people use to catch it.

### Why it matters

Without a held-back score, a training error that keeps shrinking looks like progress even while the
model is busy memorizing its examples. You could end up with a model that shines on its practice examples and fails on
everything new, without finding out until it meets real inputs. You would also have no good way to choose
a model’s size or when to stop training, because the practice score keeps improving almost whatever you
do.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- how a model’s score on the examples it trained on differs from its score on examples it never saw, and
  why only the second one shows real learning;
- how to spot the moment a model starts memorizing, which is called overfitting, and two ways to respond:
  keeping its numbers small and stopping at the right time;
- what happens to a training run when you make the model more or less flexible, or change how hard its
  numbers are pulled toward zero;
- what happens when you change how big each training step is, called the learning rate, and how many
  examples feed into each step, called the batch size.

## What it is

Go back to the two classmates for a moment, because there are two very different things you can measure
about any student. One is how well they do on practice problems they have already worked through, and the
other is how well they do on brand-new problems. You can measure a model in the same two ways, and this
lab draws both measurements as lines on one chart so that you can compare them.

### Two scores from one model

The examples a model trains on are called the **training set**, and they are like the practice problems
with the answers in the back of the book. During training, the model is adjusted over and over to do
better on exactly these examples and no others. Its score on them is the **training loss**, where “loss”
means how far wrong the model is, so a lower loss is a better score.

A second pile of examples is kept out of training on purpose, or “held out”, and it is called the
**validation set**. The model is never adjusted to fit these examples, so they work like a quiz full of
brand-new problems. The score on them is the **validation loss**, and it tells you how well whatever the
model learned carries over to examples it has not trained on.

Doing well on new examples like these is called **generalization**, and it is the only real evidence that
a model picked up a pattern. A memorized answer is no help on a question you have never seen, so a model
that only memorized its practice set will do badly on new examples.

The difference between the two scores, the validation loss minus the training loss, is called the
**generalization gap**, and the lab shows it as `Gap (val − train)`. Neither line tells you much on its
own, because the useful information is in how far apart the two lines sit and whether that distance keeps
growing.

### How a miss is scored

The model in this lab draws a curve, and its loss measures how badly that curve misses the dots it is
being scored on. For each dot, the lab measures the up-and-down distance between the dot and the curve,
squares that distance, and then averages the squares over all 16 dots. That average is called the **mean
squared error**, and it is the loss on every chart in the lab.

Here is a small worked example that uses three dots instead of 16, with the curve missing each dot by a
different amount:

- The curve passes 0.1 above the first dot, and that miss squares to 0.01.
- It passes 0.3 below the second dot, and squaring that miss turns it into a positive 0.09.
- It passes 0.2 above the third dot, and that miss squares to 0.04.
- The squares add up to 0.14, so their average over the three dots, which is the loss, comes to about
  0.047.

Squaring does two useful jobs: it turns misses below a dot into positive numbers, so they cannot cancel
out misses above another dot. It also makes big misses count far more than small ones, since a miss that
is three times bigger counts nine times as much.

To turn a loss back into a distance, take its square root, which gives you the size of a typical miss.
For example, a loss of 0.0625 means a typical miss of about 0.25. That is because 0.25 squared is 0.0625,
and you will meet this exact number again in a moment, when the lab measures its noise.

### The model in this lab

The “student” in this lab is a small model whose only job is to draw a curve that fits a set of dots. The
lab starts from a smooth wave that tilts upward from left to right, which it calls the true curve, and
places 32 dots along it. It then nudges each dot up or down by a random amount, so the dots sit near the
wave but not exactly on it.

Those random nudges are called the **noise**, and in the textbook picture they are like misprints in the
answer key. They are part of what the student studies, but they are not part of the method the student
should learn, so memorizing them can only hurt.

Counting from left to right, every other dot goes into the training set and the rest go into the
validation set, which gives each pile 16 dots. Because the two piles alternate, both of them are spread
across the whole width of the chart. The model only ever trains on the 16 training dots, and it never
sees the true curve at all.

The lab chose the size of the nudges itself, so their average size is known exactly, something real data
never offers. A typical nudge is about 0.25, so the average squared nudge is 0.0625, and the lab calls
this the **noise variance**. It is written σ² and read as “sigma squared”, and it appears as a dotted
line on both loss charts.

That dotted line matters because no curve can predict random nudges, so even the true curve misses the
dots by that much on average. On this lab’s 16 training dots, the true curve scores 0.081, a little above
0.0625, because these particular nudges happen to run on the large side.

The true curve is the best any
curve can do by following the pattern alone. So when a model’s training loss sinks well below the dotted line, it can only have got there
by bending toward some of the random nudges in its own dots. Those
nudges will not be there on any new dot, so matching them can never help on new examples.

### Weights, degree and capacity

The model builds its curve by adding together a set of simple building-block curves of different shapes.
The first block is a flat line, the next is a sloping line, and each block after that bends one more time
than the block before it. Each block comes with its own number, which says how much of that block goes
into the mix, and these numbers are called **weights**. Training a model means adjusting its weights
until the blocks add up to a curve that matches the training dots well.

A curve built from blocks like these is called a **polynomial**, and its degree counts how many blocks
come after the flat one. The **Polynomial degree** control runs from 1 to 13, and next to the degree it
shows how many weights that gives. At degree 1 there are two weights, one for the flat block and one for
the sloping block, so the model can only ever draw a straight line. At degree 11 the control reads
`11 · 12 weights`, and a curve built from that many blocks can bend as many as 10 times.

How flexible a model is, meaning how many different shapes it can draw, is called its **model capacity**,
and in this lab capacity is the polynomial degree. Turning up the degree gives the model more freedom to
bend, and in the much larger models behind chatbots, capacity grows with the number of weights.

### How training moves the weights

Training starts with every weight set to zero, so the first curve is a flat line across the middle of the
chart. From there, training repeats the same move thousands of times, and each repeat is called a step.
In each step it looks at the training dots, works out which small change to each weight would lower the
training loss fastest, and makes that change.

One full pass through all 16 training dots is called an epoch, and the **Epoch** slider in the lab runs
from 0 to 10,000 of them. The lab recorded both losses 83 times along the way, so dragging the slider
shows you the model at any of those moments.

Two settings on **Training dynamics controls** decide how each step is made, and each one comes with an
everyday picture below:

- **Learning rate** sets how big each step is, and the lab writes it with the Greek letter η, read as
  “eta”, set to 0.1 unless you change it. A bigger learning rate gets somewhere faster, but a step that
  is too big can jump past the best values for the weights and land somewhere worse.
- **Batch size** sets how many training dots the model looks at before each step. At `16 (full)` it uses
  every dot and takes one step per epoch, while at 4 it takes four quicker but less reliable steps per
  epoch.

Picture a group chat of 16 friends trying to pick a place for lunch, where you can either wait for
everyone or decide as the replies come in. If you wait for all 16 replies, you make one well-informed
choice, but it takes a while to arrive. If you decide after every 4 replies, you make four quick choices
in the same time, but each one leans toward whichever friends happened to answer first. Smaller batches
work the same way: more steps per epoch, each pointing a little away from the direction that all 16 dots
would agree on.

The picture stops matching in one way. Lunch choices replace each other, but the four small steps add
up, each one starting from where the last one left off. Over an epoch every dot still gets its say, and
the lean only makes the path wobble.

Overshooting deserves its own picture, so imagine someone in a shower who turns the knob a long way every
time the water feels wrong. The water goes from too cold to too hot, so they swing the knob hard the
other way, and each correction overshoots by more than the last. A training run with too big a learning
rate can do the same thing, with its loss swinging wider and wider instead of settling down.

On the chart you will see a steady climb rather than visible swings, because the lab only records the loss now and
then. Once the loss passes 10⁶, which is a 1 followed by six zeros, the lab stops the run and marks it as
`diverged`.

Two more controls round out the card: **Weight decay**, a fix for memorizing that you will try further
down, and **Noise draw**. **Noise draw** picks one of four random draws of the dots, labelled seed 1 to seed 4.
It lets you check whether a result depends on the luck of the draw. A seed is the starting number
the lab feeds into its random-number maker, so the same seed always gives the same dots. A different
seed changes both the up-and-down nudges and exactly where each dot sits from left to right.

### Three ways a run can go

As you drag **Epoch**, a badge on **Training curves** names the situation the run is in, and there are
three main situations to know:

- **Underfitting** means both losses stay high, because the model is too simple to follow the pattern or
  has not trained long enough yet. A straight line at degree 1 settles at a training loss of 0.322 and
  never improves, because no straight line can follow a wave.
- **Fitting** means both losses fall together and then hold fairly steady, which is what you want to see.
  At the default degree 5, validation reaches its lowest point at epoch 11, then creeps up only slightly
  and holds steady for the rest of the run.
- **Overfitting** means the training loss keeps falling while the validation loss turns around and
  climbs. The model is now matching the random nudges in its 16 training dots, which helps on those dots
  but hurts on every new one.

The badge also has a fourth reading, `diverged`, for a run whose steps overshoot so badly that its loss
explodes, as in the shower story. In the textbook picture, overfitting is the student who has memorized
the answer key, misprints and all, so their practice score is perfect for the wrong reason.

### Three piles, not two

The validation set has a weakness of its own, because you also use it to make choices about the model.
You check it to decide how big a model to use, how big a learning rate to pick, and when to stop
training. Every time you keep whichever option scored best on it, you are choosing partly on luck, so
your final choice’s score looks a little better than it really is.

Picture a student who tries ten different ways of studying and keeps whichever one earns the best mark on
the same practice quiz. Part of that winning mark is real, and part of it is luck, because one method
happened to suit those particular questions. To get an honest number, the teacher needs a final exam that
played no part in any of the choosing.

That final pile is called the **test set**, and a real project locks it away and scores it only once, at
the very end. It stays honest only until someone starts tuning against it, because at that point it has
turned into one more validation set. This lab does not draw a test set. The split strip is the row of all 32 dots at the top of **Training dynamics controls**.
The note under it says so in plain words: “No test split is drawn.”

## Why it’s here

The last few labs in this course worked on making a loss go down, and this one asks which loss, measured
on which examples. It also asks when to stop pushing that loss down, which is the difference between just
running training and ending up with a model you can trust.

### Training cannot tell pattern from noise

Training can only push down the score it can see, and the only score it sees is the training loss on its
own dots. From inside that score, a random nudge in a dot looks exactly like part of the real pattern, so
there is no signal that says “ignore this one”. Bending the curve toward a noisy dot lowers the training
loss as surely as following the true wave does. So training does both whenever the model has enough
weights to bend that far, and a high degree gives it plenty.

Nothing in the process is trying to cheat, and nothing in it can tell the difference between a pattern
and an accident. The validation dots are the only outside check, because their nudges are different
random amounts from the nudges in the training dots. A curve that bent toward the training noise has no reason to pass near the validation dots.
So it misses some of them, most of all where no training dot holds it in place.

### Why the damage shows up late

Training does not learn every part of the curve at the same speed, and that turns out to matter a great
deal. Big, smooth changes to the curve lower the loss a lot with each step, so training makes them first,
mostly within the first dozen or so epochs. Fine, wiggly changes lower the loss only a tiny bit with each
step, so training gets to them slowly, over hundreds and then thousands of epochs.

The real pattern here is mostly smooth, while the noise mostly looks like fine wiggles from one dot to
the next, so useful learning arrives first. The harmful fitting creeps in afterwards, which is why, at
degree 11, the validation line falls quickly at the start and only later begins to climb.

Picture a friend who studies the same practice book every night for a month, hoping to do well on the
end-of-term test. In the first few sessions they pick up the methods, and their marks on new problems
improve quickly. Weeks later they have started remembering particular answers, misprints included, so
their practice marks keep creeping up while their marks on new problems slowly slip.

This ordering is the reason that stopping early works, because there is a moment between the two stages
when the pattern is learned but little noise is matched. At degree 11 in this lab, that moment comes at
epoch 13, long before the thousands of epochs in which the damage builds up.

### Why it matters for every score you read

Any claim that a model is good rests on a held-out score of some kind, and a benchmark score is one of
these. So a benchmark score can only be trusted when you know how its test questions were kept apart from
the training data. **Going deeper** shows two ways that can fail.

## How to play with it

The lab’s four guided tasks walk you through this in order: **Split the data**, **Watch the curves**, **Trigger
overfitting** and **Regularize**. The parts below follow the same path, after a quick tour of what each
card shows and how to read its axes.

### Reading the cards

- **Training dynamics controls** holds every control, and at its top is the split strip, which lines up
  all 32 dots in a row. Filled dots above the line are training dots and hollow dots below it are
  validation dots. The strip’s note sums this up as “Hollow only scores it.” In other words, hollow dots
  are only used to score the curve and are never used to train it.
- **Training curves** plots both losses against the epoch, with a solid blue line for training, a dashed
  orange line for validation, and a shaded band for the gap. A green ring marks the lowest validation
  loss of the whole run, and a dotted upright line shows the epoch you have picked.
- **Fitted function** draws the model’s curve, labelled ŷ and read as “y-hat”, over the filled training
  dots and the hollow validation dots. The dashed line is the true curve, which the legend reminds you is
  hidden from the model.
- **Capacity sweep** shows training and validation loss for every degree from 1 to 13 at the epoch you
  have picked, each trained with the same settings. The degree runs along the bottom and the loss up the
  side, so each line runs from simple models on the left to flexible ones on the right.

Both loss charts use a **log scale** on the up-and-down axis, which works differently from the axes you
usually see in maths class. On an ordinary axis, each grid line adds the same amount to the one below it,
such as going up by 1 each time. On a log scale each grid line is ten times the one below, so a tiny loss
like 0.007 and a huge one like 8.63 both fit on one chart.

The epoch axis on **Training curves** works the same way. After 0 at the corner, each labelled mark is
ten times the one before, from 1 up to 10k, which means 10,000. One handy result follows from this: a run that does the same
things, only ten times later, slides about one mark to the right.

### Split the data

Pick the third filled dot from the left on the split strip, then count across to the third filled dot
from the left on **Fitted function**. Do the same with a hollow dot. Because the split alternates along the line, both piles cover the whole width, so the
validation dots test every part of the curve.

### Watch the curves

Start at the default degree 5, and drag **Epoch** back to 0 and then slowly all the way up to the end.
Both lines drop fast in the first few epochs and then level off, with validation sitting a steady
distance above training.

The green ring sits at epoch 11, where validation reaches its lowest value of 0.100. The
`Lowest validation` readout shows this as `0.100 @ 11`, where the @ sign means “at epoch”, and the button
under the slider names the same epoch. Its label reads `Stop at lowest validation (epoch 11)` for now,
and it changes whenever the ring moves.

Now go back to epoch 100, where the slider sat when you first opened the lab, and look at the readouts
under the chart. Training reads 0.017 and validation reads 0.111, and the badge says `fitting`. Their
difference shows up in `Gap (val − train)` as 0.093, and the readouts are rounded, so the subtraction can
look off by 0.001. The gap barely moves for the rest of the run.

Notice that 0.017 is already well below the dotted line, so even this model has matched a good share of
the noise. What makes the run healthy is that it stops there. With only 6 weights it cannot bend any
further, so the gap holds steady and validation does not climb.

### Trigger overfitting

**Try this:** Set **Polynomial degree** to 11, which gives the model 12 weights to turn, then drag
**Epoch** to 10,000 and watch the two lines part ways.

As soon as you set the degree to 11, the green ring and the stop button jump to epoch 13, the lowest point
of the new run. Here is what you should see, in order, as the slider moves:

- Validation drops to its best value, 0.098, at epoch 13.
- After that, validation turns around and climbs, and the badge switches to `overfitting` at epoch 215.
- By the last epoch, validation has climbed to 8.63, which is 88 times worse than the best it reached.
- Meanwhile, the training loss keeps falling the whole way down to 0.007, which is far below the dotted
  noise line.

Now look at **Fitted function** at epoch 10,000, where the curve has stopped following the dashed true
curve and bends toward individual filled dots instead. Near the right edge, the last hollow dot sits
beyond the last filled dot, so no training dot holds the curve in place there. The curve swings so far
that it leaves the chart, which only runs from −2 to 2, and a red note reports a peak of about 24.

That one hollow dot at the right edge accounts for almost all of the 8.63. The curve misses it by about
11.7, which squares to about 136. The other 15 hollow dots have squared misses that average only about
0.14, so most of the curve still passes fairly close to them. This is the squaring rule from earlier at
work, because one huge miss can outweigh everything else in the average.

### Regularize

Any rule that makes it harder for a model to bend toward noise is called **regularization**, and the lab
gives you two kinds to try at degree 11.

First, raise **Weight decay** to 0.03, which makes every step pull each weight except the flat block’s a
little toward zero, as well as toward a better fit. The lab writes the strength of that pull with the
Greek letter λ, read as “lambda”, so the control now shows `λ 0.03`.

Each building block on its own only rises and falls a modest amount. Bending sharply toward one dot and back before the next takes large amounts of the bendiest blocks,
some pushing up and some pushing down. Swinging far off near an edge takes the same. So wild swings need large weights, and a steady pull toward
small weights keeps the curve smoother and calmer between the dots.

Drag **Epoch** to 10,000 again and read the validation loss, which now sits much closer to the best this
model ever reached. It reads 0.135 instead of 8.63, while the training loss ends a little higher than it
did without weight decay. That trade is the whole idea: the model gives up a little fit on its own dots
in exchange for a curve that works on new ones.

Second, set **Weight decay** back to `λ 0 (off)` and press **Stop at lowest validation**, which jumps the
slider back to the ring at epoch 13. Validation there reads 0.098, and this move is called **early
stopping**: same model, same settings, but you keep the version from the best epoch instead of the last.

### More comparisons to run

Once you have seen the main story, these comparisons show how the other settings change a run:

- **Learning rate:** at degree 11, move **Learning rate** from 0.1 down to 0.01 and look at where the
  green ring lands. The best validation loss is almost the same, at 0.097, but it arrives about ten times
  later, at epoch 147. The whole run slides about one mark to the right on the log scale, and it still
  overfits afterwards.
- **Batch size:** go back to degree 5, set **Learning rate** to 0.3, and try each **Batch size** in turn.
  With 16 or 8 dots per step, the run trains normally from start to finish. With only 4 dots per step,
  the loss explodes and the badge reads `diverged` at epoch 22. **What to notice** explains why.
- **Model size and learning rate:** put **Batch size** back to `16 (full)` and set **Learning rate** to
  0.6, then compare two degrees. At degree 5 the run still trains normally, but at degree 11 it diverges
  at epoch 562, so a bigger model can need a smaller learning rate. The training loss bottoms out near
  0.03 in the first few epochs, then climbs steadily for hundreds of epochs before the run is stopped.
  One reason is that the bendiest blocks reach their biggest values near the edges of the chart. A small
  change in their weights moves the curve a lot there, so the loss reacts more sharply. A step size that
  is safe at degree 5 then overshoots at degree 11, like turning a more sensitive shower knob just as far.
- **Capacity sweep:** with every setting back at its default, drag **Epoch** and watch the dashed
  validation line on **Capacity sweep** change shape as training goes on. At epoch 13 the line is nearly
  flat from degree 5 up. At epoch 10,000 it rises steeply after degree 8 and peaks at degree 12. Degree
  13 lands back near 1.0, because on this draw its swing at the right edge happens to miss the last
  hollow dot by less. That dip is the luck of the draw, not the strange pattern described at the end of
  **Where it breaks**.
- **Noise draw:** at degree 11, try each seed. Seed 1 is the most dramatic, ending at 8.63. Seeds 2 and
  3 overfit far less, ending near 0.12 and 0.34. With seed 4 the run barely overfits at all. Its best
  validation comes late, at epoch 909, and it ends at 0.078, only a little above that best of 0.073. How badly a model overfits depends on which dots it
  happened to get, especially near the edges.

In both runs that end in `diverged`, the badge reads `overfitting` for a while first, because validation
has passed 1.5 times its best. Look at the training line, though. It is climbing, not falling, so this is
an unstable run, not a model memorizing its dots.

## What to notice

**A common mix-up:** Validation loss sitting above training loss does not, by itself, mean that the model
is overfitting. The model was adjusted to fit the training dots and has never seen the validation dots,
so on only 16 points some gap is expected from the start. What signals overfitting is a gap that keeps
growing while training goes on, not a gap that holds steady.

With that in mind, here are the signals worth watching as you move the controls:

- **A steady gap is healthy:** at degree 5 the gap settles near 0.09 and stays there for thousands of
  epochs, while at degree 11 it keeps widening.
- **Falling training loss proves nothing by itself:** at degree 11 it falls at every one of the 83
  recorded moments. That includes the ones where validation is 88 times worse than its best. It is the cheapest number to improve,
  because memorizing noise lowers it as well as learning the pattern does.
- **Training loss far below the dotted line means noise is being matched:** even the true curve scores
  0.081 on the training dots. At degree 5 the training loss settles near 0.017, so it has matched a good
  share of the noise. It cannot bend any further, though, and validation holds steady near 0.111. At degree
  11 the training loss ends at 0.007, only a little lower, yet validation ends 88 times worse than its
  best. How far training sinks below the line tells you noise is being matched, but only the validation
  line tells you how much that hurts.
- **Being consistently bad is not the same as generalizing:** at degree 1 the losses sit close together,
  0.322 for training and 0.279 for validation. Validation even lands a little lower here, by the luck of
  which dots fell into each pile. Yet both are poor at every epoch, so a small gap only counts as good
  news when both losses are also low.
- **Capacity matters differently at different times:** at epoch 13, **Capacity sweep** shows every degree
  from 5 upward scoring about the same on validation. Let training run to the last epoch and they spread
  far apart, with degree 7 holding at 0.100 and degree 12 climbing past 10. Memorizing depends on model
  size, the amount of data and the length of training, all acting together.
- **A smaller learning rate tells the same story, only slower:** cutting the learning rate to a tenth
  gives about the same best score, reached roughly ten times later. The run still overfits afterwards, so
  smaller steps buy you time to stop, but they do not prevent the memorizing.
- **Batch size and learning rate work together:** picture the loss as a valley, with the best weights at
  the bottom. With all 16 dots the valley has gentle sides, so a step at learning rate 0.3 moves most of the way
  down toward the bottom without crossing it. The loss on just 4 dots can make a much narrower, steeper valley.
  A step at the same learning rate then jumps clean over the bottom and lands higher up the far wall. The next
  step overshoots even more, like the shower knob, which is how the batch-4 run above diverged. This is a
  different problem from the wobble in the lunch picture.
- **Weight decay shows up in the weights:** the `Weight size ‖θ‖` readout on **Fitted function** gives
  one number for the size of all the weights together. It squares every weight, adds the squares up and
  takes the square root, where θ, read as “theta”, stands for the whole list of weights. At degree 11 and
  the last epoch, it reads 2.48 with no weight decay at all. Raising **Weight decay** to 0.03 brings it
  down to 0.74, which makes the weights about three times smaller overall.
- **One noise draw is one roll of the dice:** changing **Noise draw** changes how badly each degree
  overfits. A single run shows you one roll of the dice, so try a few seeds to see what is typical.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- **Everything is real, but unusually visible:** the model, the data, the training and every loss are
  real, and the same seed gives the same run every time. What is unrealistic is how much you can see: the true curve, the exact noise level, and a simple
  model. It takes in just one number, how far left or right a dot sits, so it fits on a flat chart.
- **The split is unusually even:** half the dots are held out, so that the validation line is not decided
  by a few unlucky points. Real projects usually hold out far less than half, often somewhere between 10
  and 20 percent of their data.
- **The badge uses the lab’s own cut-offs:** it reads `overfitting` once validation is 1.5 times the best
  it reached earlier in the run. It reads `underfitting` while the training loss is above 0.125, which is
  twice the noise variance, and these are sensible choices rather than laws.
- **Overfitting here is slow:** it spreads over thousands of epochs because a polynomial picks up fine
  wiggles very slowly. Other models overfit on their own schedules, and some do it within a few epochs of
  fine-tuning.
- **Only one fix is built in:** weight decay is the only regularizer the lab offers, and early stopping
  is something you do yourself with the **Epoch** slider. The lab has no dropout and no extra data, which
  are other fixes you will meet in the last section.
- **The capacity sweep stops early:** the highest degree, 13, has 14 weights, which is fewer than the 16
  training dots. In general, a curve needs as many weights as there are dots to pass exactly through every
  one of them. A straight line, with its two weights, can always pass through two dots but usually not
  through three. So here the curve can never pass exactly through every training dot, which keeps the lab
  away from a stranger pattern described at the end of this section.
- **Real models cannot be drawn:** a model with one input can be shown as a curve, but a real model takes
  in thousands of numbers at once. Nobody can look at a curve like that, so in real projects overfitting
  is spotted from held-out loss and never seen directly.

### Where the textbook picture stops matching

The textbook picture suggests deliberate cheating, but nothing in a model is choosing to memorize
anything. Fitting the noise is one more way to lower the training score, and the training process has no
way to tell noise from pattern. A student could also look at a misprint and notice that it is wrong,
while the model has nothing to compare its dots against except the dots themselves.

### Where the real thing gets harder

- **Real validation curves are bumpy:** in real projects the validation score jumps around from one check
  to the next, so a single upward point is not a verdict. People smooth the curve, wait for several worse
  checks in a row before stopping, or average over several runs started from different random seeds.
- **Loss and the score you care about can disagree:** picture a model answering multiple-choice
  questions. Instead of naming one answer, it gives each choice a percentage, such as “95% sure it is B.”
  The loss for this kind of model punishes being confidently wrong very hard, so being 95% sure of a
  wrong answer costs far more than being 60% sure of it. A model can get more answers right while growing
  too sure about the few it gets wrong, which is called overconfidence. Its validation loss then rises
  even as its share of right answers improves.

Overfitting is also not the main worry when a large language model, the kind behind a chatbot, is first
trained. Those runs often see most of their text only about once, so there is little
chance to memorize by repetition and the two curves stay close. Overfitting comes back later, when a
model is fine-tuned on a small set of examples or when the same data is repeated many times.

It also returns when a model is trained against an automated judge, itself a model that scores answers, until it
exploits a weakness in that judge. The model ends up learning the judge’s quirks instead of the real
skill, which is memorizing the judge rather than learning the pattern.

There is also a genuinely strange pattern called **double descent**, which shows up in very large models
and runs against the tidy story of this lab. Held-out error can get worse as a model gains enough
capacity to pass exactly through every training point. Then, once the model grows far beyond that size,
the error can start improving again, and a similar rise and fall can even appear as training runs longer.
This lab stops at degree 13, below the 16 training dots, so it never gets there, and its tidy story
describes one situation, not a law of nature.

## Going deeper

### How the weight decay penalty works

Weight decay adds a penalty to the loss that training pushes down, equal to λ times the sum of the squared
weights. It leaves out the flat block’s weight, which only shifts the whole curve up or down and cannot
make it swing. The info panel on **Training dynamics controls** writes this as `λ Σ θₖ²`, where Σ means
“add them all up” and θ stands for the weights. Here is one worked example, using made-up numbers that
are small enough to check in your head:

- Suppose the penalty counts only two weights, 0.5 and −1, and λ is set to 0.1.
- The squares of the two weights are 0.25 and 1, which add up to 1.25.
- The penalty is λ times that total, so 0.1 times 1.25 gives 0.125.

The training line on the chart leaves this penalty out, so both loss lines still measure plain misses on
the dots. Because big weights make a big penalty, training now has two goals at once: fit the dots well,
and keep the weights small. A weight that is twice as big adds four times the penalty, so the largest
weights get pulled down the hardest. Wild swings in the curve need large weights, so they become
expensive, and the curve that wins is a calmer one.

### Other tools against memorizing

Weight decay is one of several real tools for the same problem, and all of them count as regularization:

- **Dropout** is used in neural networks, which are built from many small parts that each pass a number
  along to the next. During training, dropout switches off a random handful of those parts on every step,
  so no part can count on one particular partner and backup routes have to form.
- **More or more varied data** attacks the real cause, because memorizing is hardest when there is too
  much to memorize. One way is to make extra examples by changing existing ones without changing their
  answers, such as flipping a photo of a cat.
- **Early stopping** is regularization by budget, because fewer steps leave less time to fit noise.
- **Label smoothing** stops a model that answers multiple-choice questions from pushing its percentage
  for one choice all the way to 100%.

### Two readouts about the steps

Training works out each step from a list of slopes, one for each weight, called the gradient, and
**Training dynamics controls** has two readouts about it. Each slope says how fast the loss changes as
that weight changes, so together they show which way lowers the loss fastest.

- `Full-batch |∇L|` shows how big the whole list of slopes is when all 16 dots are used, and ∇L is
  shorthand for the gradient of the loss.
- `Minibatch noise` shows how far, typically, the slopes from a smaller batch stray from the full-batch
  ones, and at `16 (full)` it reads `0 (full)`.

Once the big, smooth part of the fit is done, after the first dozen or so epochs, the second readout can
be several times the first. You can see this at degree 11 with **Batch size** set to 4. At epoch 100 in that run, `Minibatch noise` reads 0.199, while
`Full-batch |∇L|` reads only 0.051. By that point, most of each small-batch step is noise rather than
real direction, because the full slope has already become small.

### Problems in the real world

- **Distribution shift:** a model can do well on held-out data from the same source as its training data
  and still fail on real inputs that look different. It is like practising only with one teacher’s
  quizzes and then sitting an exam written by someone with a very different style. That is why where the
  validation set came from matters as much as how big it is.
- **Data leakage:** sometimes near-copies of one example land on both sides of the split, or the test
  questions were quietly in the training data all along. The held-out line then stays reassuring while
  measuring nothing, like a final exam whose questions were printed in the practice book by mistake.
  Everyone aces that exam, and the score tells you nothing about what anyone learned.
