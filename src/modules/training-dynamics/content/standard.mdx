## The big picture

This module is about how a training run unfolds over time, and how to tell a model that is learning
structure that transfers from one that is memorizing its sample. *Loss & gradient descent* and
*Backpropagation* built the machinery that fits parameters to data. Here it runs for thousands of
epochs, and the question is when more fitting stops helping on data the model has not seen.

The answer takes two measurements. **Training loss** is scored on the examples the optimizer fits and
**validation loss** on held-out examples it never fits, so only the second shows **generalization**.
Whether the two curves stay together or part depends on **model capacity** (the flexibility of the
model family), on data size and training time, and on regularization. Learning rate and batch size
mostly set the pace of the run and whether it stays stable or diverges.

### Where it shows up

- **Training pipelines.** Data is split three ways: one set to fit, one to choose settings and when to
  stop, and a **test set** scored once at the end.
- **Large language models.** Pretraining often sees most data about once, which keeps the two losses
  close. Overfitting returns instead in fine-tuning on small datasets, in data repeated many times,
  and in optimization against a learned reward model.
- **Regularizers.** Weight decay, dropout, data augmentation, label smoothing, and early stopping all
  discourage brittle fitting.
- **Evaluation.** **Data leakage** between training and evaluation data, or **distribution shift**
  between held-out data and real inputs, can make a healthy-looking held-out score misleading.

### Why it matters

Training loss is the cheapest number to improve and the least informative on its own. With enough
capacity, a model keeps lowering it by fitting noise in its sample, which is **overfitting**, while
held-out loss rises. Plotted alone, that failure looks like progress. Too little capacity
**underfits**, leaving both losses high. Choosing model size, learning rate, and the epoch to keep all
depend on the held-out curve.

### What you will be able to explain

- What training and validation loss each measure, why the **generalization gap** between them is the
  diagnostic, and why a separate test set is still needed.
- How overfitting shows up as a widening gap, and how early stopping and weight decay respond to it.
- How capacity, weight decay, learning rate, and batch size change a run, so you can predict whether it
  will underfit, fit, overfit, or diverge.

### What the lab shows

The model, its training, and every loss are real and computed in the browser. The data is generated
from a known curve plus noise rather than collected, so the true curve and the noise variance are
known, which no real project offers. Polynomials of degree 1 to 13 are each trained by minibatch
gradient descent on 16 of 32 noisy samples and scored on the other 16, with both losses measured at
83 checkpoints over 10,000 epochs.

- **Training curves**: both losses by epoch, a ring at the lowest validation loss, and a status badge.
- **Training dynamics controls**: **Epoch** and **Stop at lowest validation** choose the moment to
  read, and **Polynomial degree** picks which of the 13 trained degrees to display. Changing a run
  setting (**Weight decay**, **Learning rate**, **Batch size**, or **Noise draw**) retrains all 13.
- **Fitted function**: the model's curve over the data and the true curve.
- **Capacity sweep**: both losses for every degree at the current epoch.

The badge applies thresholds the lab chose, and no test set is drawn. The key experiment: at
**Polynomial degree** 11, drag **Epoch** to 10,000, then compare raising **Weight decay** with setting
it back to 0 and pressing **Stop at lowest validation**.

## What it is

Two losses, measured on different data. **Training loss** is the error on the examples the optimizer
fits. **Validation loss** is the error on examples held out from that fitting. Their difference, the
**generalization gap**, is the diagnostic. Neither curve alone tells you much.

### The run in this lab is real

The lab draws 32 noisy samples of a known curve, `y = 0.8 sin(4x) + 0.4x`, with noise of standard
deviation 0.25. Alternate samples form a 16-point **training set** and a 16-point **validation set**.

A polynomial of the chosen degree, `ŷ = Σ θₖ φₖ(x)`, starts at zero and is trained by minibatch gradient
descent for 10,000 epochs. Both losses are recorded 83 times. Because the noise is known, the chart can
also show what no real project has: the noise variance σ² = 0.0625, the average loss of a perfect model.

### The three regimes

- **Underfitting.** Both losses stay high. A straight line (degree 1) settles at 0.322 on training and 0.279
  on validation by about epoch 24 and stays there. It cannot bend to follow a sine.
- **Fitting.** Both losses fall together. At degree 5, validation reaches 0.100 by epoch 11 and ends at
  0.111.
- **Overfitting.** Training loss keeps falling while validation turns up. At degree 11, validation bottoms
  at 0.098 at epoch 13 and ends at 8.63, while training loss falls to 0.007. The true curve itself only
  scores 0.081 on those training points.

### Why the damage arrives late

Gradient descent fits the smooth, strongly curved directions of the loss first, and the wiggly, weakly
curved directions last. The noise lives mostly in the wiggly directions. So the useful fit happens in the
first dozen epochs and the harmful fit creeps in over thousands. That ordering is why **early stopping**
works as a regularizer.

### Three splits, not two

The training set fits parameters. The validation set chooses capacity, learning rate, and when to stop.
Because you make decisions with it, its number becomes mildly optimistic. A third **test set**, touched
once at the end, gives the honest estimate. This lab does not draw one.

## Why it’s here

Held-out measurement is the difference between a training loop and an engineering practice, and it
underlies every capability claim. A benchmark score is a held-out measurement, and it means
something only if the test data was absent from training. When it was not, the score measures
memorization, and the failure looks like the gap in this lab, except nobody plotted the second curve.

## How to play with it

Read the split strip on **Training dynamics controls**: filled dots train the model, hollow dots only score
it. Find the same dots on **Fitted function**.

Drag **Epoch** from 0 to 10,000 at the default degree 5. Both losses fall together, and the green ring marks
the lowest validation loss, 0.100 at epoch 11.

Set **Polynomial degree** to 11 and drag **Epoch** to 10,000. The status reads overfitting from epoch 215.
On **Fitted function** the curve bends toward individual training dots and swings to about 24 near the
right edge.

Now regularize two ways. Raise **Weight decay** to 0.03, and validation at epoch 10,000 drops from 8.63 to
0.135. Or set it back to 0 and press **Stop at lowest validation**. Then try **Learning rate** and **Batch
size**, and watch **Capacity sweep** as you move **Epoch**.

## What to notice

- Falling training loss proves nothing by itself. At degree 11 it falls at every checkpoint, including the
  ones where validation is 88 times worse than its best.
- A steady gap is normal on 16 points. At degree 5 the gap settles near 0.09 and stays there. A widening
  gap is the signal.
- A ten times smaller learning rate gives almost the same run, slower. At degree 11, η 0.01 reaches its
  best, 0.097, at epoch 147 instead of 13. On the log axis the curves slide one decade to the right.
- Learning rate and batch size interact. At degree 5, η 0.3 trains normally with batch 16 or 8 but
  diverges at epoch 22 with batch 4. The loss on 4 points can curve far more sharply than the average over
  16, so the same step overshoots.
- On **Capacity sweep**, capacity barely matters at epoch 13 and matters enormously at epoch 10,000.
  Overfitting depends on capacity, data, and training time together.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The model, the data, the training, and every loss are real and reproducible from the seed.
What is unrealistic is how much you know: the true curve, the noise level, and a one-dimensional input you
can plot. Half the data is held out so the validation curve is not decided by a few points; real projects
usually hold out 10 to 20 percent.

The status label applies fixed thresholds the lab chose: 1.5 times the best validation loss so far, and
training loss above 2σ². The overfitting here is also slow, spread over thousands of epochs, because a
polynomial's wiggly directions are weakly curved. Networks overfit on their own schedules, sometimes within
a few epochs of fine-tuning.

**In real systems.** Validation curves are noisy, so one upward point is not a verdict. Practitioners
smooth, average over seeds, or wait several evaluations before stopping. Loss and the metric you care about
can disagree: validation loss can rise from overconfidence while accuracy still improves.

The classical picture also does not transfer cleanly to large language model pretraining. Those runs often
see most data about once, so training and held-out loss stay close for the whole run. Overfitting returns
in fine-tuning on small datasets, in data repeated many times, and when a model is optimized against a
reward model until it exploits it.

Large models can also show **double descent**: held-out error rises as capacity approaches the point of
fitting the training set exactly, then falls again well beyond it. This lab stops at degree 13, below the
16 training points, so it never reaches that regime.

## Going deeper

Weight decay is one regularizer among several:

- **Weight decay** adds λ times the sum of squared weights to the objective, pulling weights toward zero.
  At degree 11 and epoch 10,000 it holds the weight size at 0.74 instead of 2.48. AdamW applies it directly
  to the parameters.
- **Dropout** zeroes a random fraction of activations on each step, so no unit can rely on one partner.
- **Data augmentation** enlarges the dataset with label-preserving transformations. It attacks the cause:
  too little data for the capacity.
- **Early stopping** is regularization by budget.
- **Label smoothing** stops a classifier from pushing its confidence all the way to 1.

Two concerns show up in production. **Distribution shift** means a model that generalizes to held-out data
from the same source can still fail on real traffic, so the validation set's provenance matters as much as
its size. **Data leakage**, such as near-duplicates across the split or evaluation text inside pretraining
data, makes the validation curve look healthy while measuring nothing.
