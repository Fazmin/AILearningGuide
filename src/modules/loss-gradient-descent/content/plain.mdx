## The big picture

Nobody sets the numbers inside an AI model by hand, because a model can have millions or even billions of
adjustable numbers. Training has to find good values for every one of them on its own, one small change at
a time. This lesson is about the rule that decides each of those changes, and about what happens when that
rule is used well or badly.

You can picture each of those numbers as a dial on a huge control panel, because turning it changes what
the model does with its input. The rule for turning the dials is called **gradient descent**, and it runs
as a loop. First it measures how wrong the model is, using a single number called the **loss**. Then it
works out which way to turn every dial so the loss falls. Finally it turns each dial a small amount that
way, and the loop starts again from the new settings.

The picture you will carry through this lesson is a hiker walking downhill in thick fog. The hiker cannot
see the bottom of the valley, but they can feel which way the ground tilts under their feet. So they take
one step downhill, stop to feel the ground again, and keep going until the ground is level.

### Where you have met it

**A single neuron** showed how a model's dials decide what it outputs, and **The math you need**
introduced the slope of a curve and a first downhill step. This lab puts those two earlier labs together
and uses them to train a real, if very small, model.

Every neural network in this course, meaning every model built from many small neurons, had its numbers
set by some version of this loop. That includes the network in the **Stacking neurons** lab, which trained
when you pressed a single button. It also includes the language models in later labs, the kind of program
that sits behind a chatbot.

You may also have heard people describe training that went badly, with phrases like "the loss blew up",
"it plateaued" or "the learning rate was too high". Each of those phrases describes something you can make
happen in this lab, and by the end you will be able to point at it on the screen.

### Why it matters

A real model has far too many dials for anyone to try every possible setting. Gradient descent gets
around this because it only needs the tilt where the model stands now, which is why it works for models
of any size.

The tilt tells you which way to go, but it cannot tell you how big a step is safe. You choose that with a
number called the learning rate, and choosing badly can ruin a whole training run. Steps that are too
small make training crawl, while steps that are too large can make the loss grow without end. This lab
shows exactly where the line between those two falls, and why it sits there.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these three ideas in your own words:

- what the **loss** measures, and why training works to make it as small as possible, even though a small
  loss is not quite the same as a good model;
- which way one step of gradient descent will move the dials, worked out before you press the button from
  the numbers the lab shows you;
- how the step size, called the learning rate, and the bend of the valley, called its curvature, decide
  whether the search settles, zigzags past the bottom, or flies off.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab fits a straight line to eight dots, and every number it shows is worked out for real from those
dots rather than sketched by hand. The dots themselves were made up for this lesson, and the problem is

## What it is

### Walking downhill in fog

Picture yourself on a hillside in fog so thick that you cannot see more than a step ahead of you. You want
to reach the lowest point of the valley, but you have no map, so all you can use is the ground under your
feet. With every step, you can feel which way the ground tilts and roughly how steeply it slopes.

So you follow a routine: you feel the tilt, take a step in the downhill direction, stop, and feel the
ground again. Each step uses only what you can feel at the spot where you stand, yet if you repeat the
routine enough times, you end up close to the bottom.

Each part of that picture has a name in machine learning, and you will meet every one of them in the lab:

- Your height on the hill is the **loss**, a single number that says how wrong the model is right now,
  where lower is better.
- The tilt under your feet is the **gradient**, which says which way is uphill and how steep it is, so you
  step the opposite way.
- How far you move with each step is the **Learning rate** times how steep the tilt is. The learning rate
  is the part you choose, with a slider.
- The routine itself, feeling the tilt, stepping the other way and repeating, is **gradient descent**,
  where the word "descent" means going down.

The rest of this section turns each part of that picture into something you can see and measure in the
lab, one piece at a time.

### The model: a line with two dials

The lab has eight blue dots on a graph, and its model is a straight line that tries to pass as close to
all of them as it can. Any straight line can be written as `ŷ = w·x + b`, where `w·x` means w times x, and
`x` is a position along the bottom of the graph. The symbol `ŷ`, read "y-hat", is the height of the line at that position, which
is the line's guess for the dot that sits there.

The line is controlled by two dials: the **slope** `w` sets how steeply it climbs, and the **intercept**
`b` sets the height where it crosses the vertical axis. The numbers a model adjusts while it learns are
called its **parameters**, so this small model has exactly two parameters to set.

The heading of the **Data and fit** card always shows the current line, and at the lab's starting point it
reads `ŷ = −1.00·x + 1.20`. That means w is −1 and b is 1.2 when the run begins. A negative w makes the
line run downhill from left to right, while the dots climb from the bottom left to the top right. So this
starting line is a poor fit, and it was chosen that way on purpose to give the search somewhere to go.

### Loss: one number for how wrong the line is

To score the line, the lab looks at each dot and measures the gap between the dot and the line, straight
up or straight down. That gap is called a **residual**, and the lab draws each one as a short orange
segment between the dot and the line. A residual is positive when the line passes above its dot, and
negative when the line passes below it.

The lab then squares each residual and takes the average of the eight squares, and that average is the
loss, shown on the **Loss L** readout. The lab also prints the formula for this in a note under the bars,
written with symbols:

```
L = (1/8) Σ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)²
```

The formula looks heavier than it is, because each symbol stands for one small instruction. The Σ is the
Greek capital letter sigma, which means "add these up", and the small i means "do this for dot 1, then dot
2, and so on". The symbol ŷᵢ is the line's guess at dot i, and yᵢ is that dot's real height. So the
formula says to take the guess minus the real height for each dot, square the result, add up all eight,
and divide by 8.

Here are the first dot and the last dot worked out for the starting line. For dot 1, the guess is
−1.00 × 0.1 + 1.20 = 1.10, and for dot 8 it is −1.00 × 1.9 + 1.20 = −0.70. The rest of the arithmetic for
these two dots is laid out below:

```
dot 1   x = 0.1   guess  1.10   real height −0.33   miss  1.43   squared  2.04
dot 8   x = 1.9   guess −0.70   real height  2.15   miss −2.85   squared  8.12
```

The bars under the chart show all eight squared misses, and these two sit at either end, with 2.04 on the
far left and 8.12 on the far right. Do the same for all eight dots and the squares add up to about 20.09.
Divide that total by 8 and you get 2.511, which is the number **Loss L** shows at the start.

Squaring the misses, instead of using them as they are, does two useful jobs:

- It turns every miss into a positive number, so a line that is 2 too high and a line that is 2 too low
  both pay a cost of 4. Without squaring, misses above and below the line could cancel each other out and
  hide a bad fit.
- It makes big misses count far more than small ones, because one miss of 2 costs 4, which is as much as
  four misses of 1. That means the line gets pushed hardest to fix its worst misses.

You can check that second job in the numbers printed under the bars. After one step at the starting
learning rate, the 8.12 on the far right drops to 4.55, even though the 2.04 on the far left grows a
little, to 2.56. The bars are redrawn after every change so the tallest one always fills the chart, so
compare the printed numbers from step to step, not the bar heights.

This recipe, the average of the squared misses, is called **mean squared error**, because "mean" is
another word for average. It suits problems where the right answer is a number, like a height or a price,
while language models use a different recipe called cross-entropy, described in Going deeper.

### Loss as the goal of training

A loss is built to be a single number where lower means better, and it is built to change smoothly as you
nudge a dial. That smoothness is what gives it a tilt you can measure at every possible setting. Together,
those two features turn the wish "make the model good" into a clear job: find the settings of the dials
that give this number its lowest value.

Searching for the best settings of something is called **optimization**, and the number being pushed down
is called the **objective function**, or the objective for short. Mean squared error and cross-entropy are
two different objective functions, and each one sets its own rules for which misses cost the most.

Training pushes down exactly the number you give it and nothing else, so the objective has to be a good
stand-in for what you actually care about. You will meet a case in Where it breaks where the two come
apart, even while the loss keeps falling.

### The map: the loss surface

Every pair of settings for w and b gives one line, and every line gets one loss, which means you can draw
a map. Put w along the bottom and b up the side, and mark every position with the loss of the line it
stands for. The **Loss surface** card draws exactly that map, worked out from the eight dots for every
position in view.

The map uses contour lines, like a hiking map, where each contour line joins places that are at the same
height. Here each contour joins dial settings that give the same loss, and three of the lines are labelled
with their loss, from 0.5 on the inside to 8 farther out. Darker shading means higher loss, so the map
gets darker as you move away from the bottom.

The lowest point on the map is marked with a green ×, at w = 1.443 and b = −0.542. The loss there is
0.0205, which is the smallest value this loss can ever reach. The **Data and fit** card draws the line for
that position as a green dotted line, which its legend calls the least-squares line. That means the best
possible line, worked out directly with a formula rather than by stepping.

Notice that even the lowest loss is not zero, because the eight dots do not lie on one straight line. The
best line still misses each dot by a little, so 0.0205 is as low as the loss can go for these dots.

Look at the shape of the contours as well: they are long, thin ovals, which means the map shows a long,
narrow valley rather than a round bowl. The ground rises steeply as you move across this valley and only
gently as you move along its floor, and that difference turns out to matter a great deal.

### The gradient: which way is uphill

Back in the fog, you could feel the tilt under your feet, and the lab gives you that tilt as two numbers
under the map, **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b**. This lesson calls them tilts to keep them apart from the line's
slope `w`, but you will often hear them called slopes as well.

The first one, **∂L/∂w**, answers one question: if you nudge w up a tiny amount while b holds still, does
the loss go up or down, and how fast? You can read it aloud as "the change in L for a small change in w".
The curly ∂ is a kind of letter d, and it means that only one dial moves while every other dial stays
fixed. The second readout, **∂L/∂b**, asks the same question about the other dial, b.

The sign of each tilt tells you which way descent will turn that dial:

- A positive tilt means that raising the dial would raise the loss, so descent turns the dial down.
- A negative tilt means that raising the dial would lower the loss, so descent turns the dial up.
- A tilt close to zero means that turning the dial barely changes the loss from where you stand right now.

At the starting point, **∂L/∂w** reads −3.039 and **∂L/∂b** reads −1.403, so both tilts are negative and
raising either dial would lower the loss. The w tilt is about twice as big once you ignore the minus
signs, which means the loss reacts more strongly to w than to b at this particular position.

Taken together, the two tilts make up the **gradient**, and you can read the pair as an arrow on the map.
Picture an arrow that moves −3.039 in the w direction and −1.403 in the b direction. Since w runs along
the bottom of the map and b runs up the side, that arrow points left and a little down. It points the way
the loss climbs fastest, straight uphill, so gradient descent always flips it and steps right and a little
up instead.

On the map, the lab draws that downhill direction as a pink arrow labelled **−∇L**. The upside-down
triangle ∇ is the usual symbol for a gradient, so −∇L means "minus the gradient of the loss", the steepest
way downhill from where you stand. The arrow is always the same length because only its direction matters
here, and the two readouts below the map give its size.

Two things about that arrow are worth checking with your own eyes before you move on:

- It crosses the nearest contour line at a right angle. Moving along a contour line does not change the
  loss at all, so any part of a step that runs along the line is wasted. The steepest way down has no
  wasted part, so it cuts straight across the line. Rain running down a real hillside crosses the contour
  lines on a hiking map in the same way.
- It does not point at the green ×, because it shows the steepest way down from this one position, not the
  direction of the bottom. In a long, narrow valley, those two directions can be very far apart.

### One step, worked out

A gradient descent step moves each dial against its tilt, by an amount equal to the learning rate times
the tilt. Written out in words, the rule for each dial looks like this:

```
new value = old value − learning rate × tilt
```

The learning rate starts at 0.1 in this lab, and its slider shows it as "η 0.10", using the Greek letter
eta, the usual symbol for a learning rate. Here is one full step from the starting point at that rate,
with both dials worked out:

```
w:   −1.000 − 0.1 × (−3.039)   =   −1.000 + 0.304   =   −0.696
b:    1.200 − 0.1 × (−1.403)   =    1.200 + 0.140   =    1.340
```

Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding, so both dials go up, exactly as the negative tilts
predicted. The new line fits the dots better, and **Loss L** falls from 2.511 to 1.619 after this single
step.

Now picture doubling the learning rate to 0.2 before you take that first step, which makes each change
double as well. The changes become +0.608 for w and +0.281 for b, so the step points the same way but is
twice as long. The learning rate can stretch a step, but it can never turn one, because the direction
always comes from the gradient alone.

### Curvature: how sharply the valley bends

The tilt tells you how steep the ground is where you stand, while **curvature** tells you how quickly that
tilt changes as you move. In other words, curvature is how sharply the ground bends. A narrow canyon,
where the ground changes from steeply down to steeply up within a few steps, bends sharply, while a wide
valley floor barely bends at all.

The valley in this lab lies at a slant on the map, so "across" and "along" are not the w and b
directions, and each one mixes both dials. The lab calls the direction across the valley the **steep
axis** and the direction along its floor the **shallow axis**. These are not the w and b axes of the map,
but two slanted directions of their own.

In this lab's valley, the curvature across the valley is 4.363, but along the floor it is only 0.307. To
see what that means, picture moving 0.1 across the valley, which changes the tilt in that direction by
about 0.44, or 4.363 × 0.1. Moving 0.1 along the floor changes the tilt there by only about 0.03.

So the valley bends about 14 times more sharply across than along. The lab writes the larger curvature as
λ_max, using the Greek letter lambda, where max is short for maximum. You will see that symbol on the
ruler under the **Learning rate** slider.

Curvature puts a speed limit on your steps, and you can see why by picturing a step across a narrow canyon
toward its floor. A short step takes you partway down the wall, while a longer step can carry you right
past the floor and up the far wall. A step that is too long lands you higher on the far wall than where
you started, where the ground is even steeper, so your next step is even longer.

The lab can measure this exactly. In this valley, each step of plain descent, the basic method you have
used so far, multiplies your distance from the valley floor by one fixed number. Here is why:

- Each step is the learning rate times the tilt, so a steeper tilt means a longer step.
- Across this valley, the tilt is the curvature times your distance from the floor, so the farther out
  you are, the steeper it gets.
- So one step removes learning rate × curvature of that distance, and what is left is 1 minus that
  amount.

Take a learning rate of 0.20. One step removes 0.20 × 4.363, about 0.87, of the gap, so about 0.13 of it
is left. The **Loss per step** card shows this number as **Steep axis ×**, a name that means "your
distance across the valley is multiplied by this on every step". Here it is worked out for three learning
rates that you will try in the lab:

```
η 0.20   1 − 0.20 × 4.363  =   0.13    13% of the gap is left, on the same side
η 0.40   1 − 0.40 × 4.363  =  −0.75    lands on the far side, and the gap shrinks to 75%
η 0.48   1 − 0.48 × 4.363  =  −1.09    lands on the far side, and the gap grows by 9%
```

A negative number means the step jumps across the floor and lands on the far wall. That is fine as long
as the number's size, ignoring the minus sign, stays under 1. In that case each jump is smaller than the
one before, but once the size goes past 1, each jump is bigger than the last and the run flies off.

So two learning rates mark the edges between three kinds of behaviour, which the lab calls **regimes**.
You can find both edges from the multiplier:

- At a learning rate of 0.229, the number 1 − 0.229 × 4.363 comes to 0, so a single step lands exactly
  on the floor. With any bigger rate, the number turns negative and the step jumps past the floor, which
  is why the first edge is 1 divided by the curvature.
- At 0.458, the number reaches −1, so each jump lands exactly as far out on the other side as where it
  started. With any bigger rate, each jump grows and the run blows up, which is why the second edge is 2
  divided by the curvature.

The ruler under the **Learning rate** slider colours all three regimes, and a badge beside the **Loss
surface** heading names the one you are in:

- Below 0.229, every step lands on the same side of the valley floor, and the lab calls this regime
  **one-sided**.
- Above 0.229 but below 0.458, steps jump across the floor and zigzag, yet the zigzag keeps shrinking, and
  the lab calls this regime **overshoots**.
- Above 0.458, each zigzag is wider than the last and the loss grows without limit, and the lab calls this
  regime **diverges**. Because every step multiplies the gap by the same number, starting closer to the
  floor only delays the blow-up. Only a smaller learning rate stops it.

The note under the ruler writes the same two edges with symbols, as `1/λ_max = 0.229` and `2/λ_max =
0.458`.

### The slow direction along the floor

The steep direction sets the speed limit, but the gentle direction along the floor sets the pace of the
whole run. The same rule works along the floor, where each step multiplies your distance from the bottom
by 1 minus the learning rate times 0.307. Because 0.307 is so small, that number stays close to 1, which
means each step closes only a small part of the remaining distance. The lab shows this number as **Shallow
axis ×**.

Set the learning rate to 0.20 and **Shallow axis ×** reads 0.939, which is very close to 1. So each step
removes only about 6 percent of the distance still left along the floor, and the run needs dozens of steps
to finish.

You cannot fix that by raising the learning rate very far, because the steep direction blows up above
0.458. The best rate for this valley is about 0.43, because at that rate the gaps across the valley and along
the floor shrink at the same pace. Even then, the gap along the floor shrinks by only about 13 percent a
step. One learning rate has to serve both directions at once, so a valley that is steep one way
and nearly flat the other way is slow to finish.

If you try 0.43 in the lab, though, **Settles by** reads step 21, a little later than the step 19 you
will see at 0.40. That is not a mistake. "Best" here means the slower of the two directions shrinks as
fast as it can, and at 0.43 both **Steep axis ×** and **Shallow axis ×** come to about 0.87 in size. At
0.40 the floor shrinks almost as fast, at 0.877, while the zigzag across the valley dies down much faster,
at −0.75. From where this run starts, that quicker zigzag lets 0.40 finish a couple of steps sooner.

### Momentum: a rolling ball

So far, the hiker stops dead after every step and starts the next one from rest. **Momentum** changes
that, so picture a ball rolling on the same hill instead, carrying some of its speed from one moment to
the next. On a long, steady slope the ball keeps picking up speed, which is exactly what the slow valley
floor needs.

In the lab, the ball keeps a running speed called its velocity, which is updated on every step before the
dials move. The new velocity is the old velocity times a number β, plus the current tilt, and then each
dial moves by the learning rate times the velocity. The number β is the Greek letter beta, which you set
with the **Momentum β** slider, and it decides how much of the old speed carries over.

The ball picture needs one small correction here. A real ball is slowed by friction, which takes away
some of its speed all the time. In the lab, β alone decides how much speed survives each step. A β near 1
is like a ball on almost frictionless ice, which keeps nearly all its speed. A β of 0 is like so much
friction that the ball stops dead after every step.

To see what that rule does, suppose β is 0.8 and the tilt stays at 1 on every step, as it does on a steady
slope along the valley floor:

```
step 1:   0.8 × 0      + 1   =   1.00
step 2:   0.8 × 1.00   + 1   =   1.80
step 3:   0.8 × 1.80   + 1   =   2.44
step 4:   0.8 × 2.44   + 1   =   2.95
```

The velocity keeps creeping toward 5 without ever quite reaching it. That limit is 1 divided by (1 − β),
so on a steady slope momentum ends up taking steps about five times as long as plain descent would.

Now suppose instead that the tilt flips sign on every step, +1 then −1 then +1, as it does when the path
zigzags across a narrow valley:

```
step 1:   0.8 × 0        + 1   =    1.00
step 2:   0.8 × 1.00     − 1   =   −0.20
step 3:   0.8 × (−0.20)  + 1   =    0.84
```

The pushes in opposite directions partly cancel each other out, so the velocity stays small. This
cancelling only helps when the steps are already zigzagging, as plain descent does at learning rates
above 0.229.

At a small learning rate like 0.05, plain descent does not zigzag at all. There, momentum's carried speed
is what makes the path swing past the floor. That swinging is the price of momentum, because a ball that
has built up speed does not stop at the bottom. It rolls past the floor and partway up the other side before it turns
back, and with too much momentum it keeps swinging back and forth for a long time.

When you read the lab, keep in mind that the first step with momentum is exactly the same as a plain step,
because the velocity starts at zero. Setting β to 0 keeps none of the old speed, which turns momentum back
into plain descent exactly. Plain descent and momentum are two different optimizers, which means two
different rules for turning tilts into steps, and the **Optimizer** switch in the lab picks between them.

## Why it’s here

### Opening the button from the last lab

The previous lab, **Stacking neurons**, trained a whole network every time you pressed a single button,
without showing what happened inside. This lab opens that button to show the loop inside, and it adds
three things the button hid: curvature, the three regimes, and momentum.

### Why not work out the answer directly?

For a single straight line there is a shortcut, because a direct formula called least squares gives the
best w and b in one go. That formula is how the lab knows where to put the green ×, without walking there.

A real network has no such shortcut, because its loss depends on millions or billions of dials in a far
more tangled way. Its lowest point is unknown and cannot be found in one go, so training has no target to
aim at. What can be worked out cheaply is the tilt at the current point, for every dial at once, and the
Backpropagation lab shows how that is done. Gradient descent needs nothing more than that tilt, which is
why the same rule can train models of any size.

That is also why the fog picture fits a real network even better than it fits this lab. Here you can see
the whole map, but a real model's map has one direction for every dial, so nobody could ever draw it.
Training a real model really is walking in fog, with nothing to go on but the tilt at the current
position.

### Where the words for broken training come from

When training goes wrong, people describe it with a few stock phrases, and each one is a shape you can
make in this lab:

- "The loss exploded" is a learning rate past the stability limit, which is the 0.458 edge in this lab, so
  each swing across a steep direction grows bigger.
- "It plateaued" means the loss stopped falling for a long stretch, which is often a direction with very
  little curvature, like the flat floor of this valley.
- "It oscillates" means the run keeps swinging back and forth, which is overshoot across a steep
  direction, like the zigzag path you will see at 0.40.

Picture a made-up team that leaves a model training overnight and finds, in the morning, that the loss
curve has shot off the top of the chart. The first few steps looked fine, but after that the loss climbed
faster and faster. Someone says "the learning rate was too high", and after this lab you can say exactly
what that means. Each step was long enough that every swing across a steep valley landed farther out than
the one before.

## How to play with it

The lab walks you through four steps, named Measure error, Read the slope, Take steps and Compare
momentum, and this section follows the same order.

### 1. Measure the error by hand

Start at step 0, which is where the lab opens, and drag **Start w** and **Start b** while you watch the
**Data and fit** card. You can also click anywhere on the **Loss surface** map to start the run from that
position instead. As you drag, the purple line turns and slides, the orange residuals grow and shrink, and
each bar below the chart shows one squared miss.

Keep an eye on **Loss L** while you drag, because a dashed line across the bars marks their average, and
that average is always what **Loss L** shows. The default start, with **Start w** at −1.00 and **Start b**
at 1.20, is the one this lesson uses throughout. At that start, **Loss L** reads 2.511, the same value
worked out step by step earlier in this lesson.

See how low you can push **Loss L** by hand, using the green dotted line as a guide. Lining the purple
line up with it gets you close to 0.0205, but nothing you try gets you any lower. When you are done, set
**Start w** back to −1.00 and **Start b** back to 1.20, so your numbers match the ones in this lesson.

### 2. Predict the first step

Stay at step 0 and read **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b**, which are −3.039 and −1.403 at the default start. Before
you press anything, use their signs to predict which way w and b will move.

Both tilts are negative, so descent will raise both dials, which on this map means the first step should
head up and to the right. Check that the pink **−∇L** arrow points that way, that it crosses the nearest
contour at a right angle, and that it misses the green ×. It misses because it shows the steepest way down
from where you stand, and in this narrow valley that direction points mostly across the valley rather than
toward the bottom. Up and to the right is what "raise both dials" looks like here, because w runs along
the bottom of the map and b runs up the side.

### 3. Take steps and change the step size

**Try this:** Press **Take one gradient step** a few times and watch the orange ball, which marks where
the run is now, move across the map. The solid blue path shows the steps taken so far, and the dashed blue
line shows where the full 80-step run goes. Then drag **Step** to 80 to see the whole run, and set **Learning rate** to 0.05,
0.20, 0.40 and 0.48 in turn. Each time, read **Settles by** on the **Loss per step** card and the regime
badge on the **Loss surface**.

**Settles by** names the first step after which the loss stays within 0.01 of the lowest possible loss,
all the way to step 80. A run that dips close to the bottom and then swings back out again does not count
as settled. Here is what you should see at each of the four learning rates:

- At 0.05 the badge says one-sided, and **Settles by** reads "not in 80", because the run is too slow to
  finish within 80 steps.
- At 0.20 the badge still says one-sided, but the bigger steps now let the run settle by step 39.
- At 0.40 the badge says overshoots, and the path zigzags across the valley, yet the run settles even
  sooner, by step 19.
- At 0.48 the badge says diverges, **Settles by** reads "not in 80" again, and the path flies right off
  the map.

Watch the shape of the dashed blue line change as you go. At 0.05 it slides smoothly down into the valley and
then along the floor, while at 0.40 it bounces from wall to wall on its way to the bottom. The runs at
0.05 and 0.48 both show "not in 80", but for opposite reasons, which is why you need the badge to tell
them apart. The lab opens at a learning rate of 0.10, which settles by step 78, with very little time to
spare.

You can press **Back to step 0** at any time to replay a run from its start. Because the dashed blue line
always shows the full run, you can see where a run is heading before you step through it.

### 4. Compare momentum

Put **Learning rate** back to 0.05 and switch **Optimizer** from **Plain descent** to **Momentum**, and a
new **Momentum β** slider will appear, set to 0.90. Try **Momentum β** at 0.5, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95, reading
**Settles by** each time, and remember that plain descent at this rate had not settled after 80 steps.

- At β 0.5 the run settles by step 77, which is only a little better than plain descent.
- At β 0.8 it settles by step 26, the fastest of the four settings you try.
- At β 0.9, the slider's starting value, it settles by step 46, so more momentum has already made things

- At β 0.95 it does not settle within 80 steps at all, because the rolling ball keeps swinging past the

Watch the path on the map as well, because under momentum it swings past the valley floor and curls back.
It can also overshoot the green × along the length of the valley, not only across it. In Momentum mode,
the **Steep axis ×** readout adds the word "swing" when the motion across the valley swings from side to
side. At learning rate 0.05 it shows for every β you try, so watch the map to see the overshoot along the
valley. In plain mode the readout never says "swing", and a zigzag shows as a negative number instead.

## What to notice

### The direction comes from the gradient alone

The learning rate makes every step longer or shorter, but it never turns a step in a new direction. If the
pink arrow points right and up, the first step goes right and up at every learning rate you choose, and
only its length changes. That is why too large a learning rate overshoots across the valley instead of
finding a better route: it can stretch the step but never aim it.

### Steps shrink on their own near the bottom

Watch the dots along the solid blue path as a run nears the green ×, and you will see them bunch closer
and closer together. Nothing in the lab changed the learning rate along the way, so the shrinking must
come from somewhere else. The tilt gets smaller as the ground flattens out near the bottom, and each step
is the learning rate times the tilt, so the steps shrink by themselves.

Settling down like this, until the loss stops improving because the run has reached the bottom, is called
**convergence**. Right at the bottom the tilt is exactly zero, so a step taken there would not move the
dials at all.

### A fast drop, then a long crawl

In this narrow valley, the steepest way down points mostly across the valley rather than along it toward
the bottom. So a plain run drops quickly down the steep wall and then crawls along the nearly flat floor
for a long time.

You can see both parts on the **Loss per step** card, which draws the loss after every step of the run.
Its vertical axis uses a log scale, which means each gridline stands for ten times the value of the
gridline below it. That spacing lets a loss near 2.5 and a loss near 0.02 fit on the same chart, which an
ordinary scale could not show clearly.

At learning rate 0.05, the curve drops fast for a few steps and then bends into a long, gentle slope. The
fast drop is the steep direction finishing its work, and the slow tail is the shallow direction along the
floor. **Shallow axis ×** reads 0.985 at this rate, so each step closes only 1.5 percent of the distance
left along the floor. That slow pace is what stretches the tail out across the rest of the chart.

### Zigzagging is not the same as blowing up

Between 0.229 and 0.458, the orange ball lands on the far wall with every step, yet the loss still falls on
every step. Try learning rate 0.40 and watch the path zigzag back and forth across the valley while the
loss curve heads steadily downward. Each landing is closer to the floor than the one before, so
overshooting by this much is not a failure, and here it even settles sooner than 0.20 does. The reason is
the slow direction along the floor, which closes faster at the bigger rate, because **Shallow axis ×**
drops from 0.939 to 0.877.

Above 0.458 the story changes, and a learning rate of 0.48 shows how quietly the trouble can begin. The
first step still lowers the loss, from 2.511 to 2.392, so the start of the run looks like progress. After
that, every swing across the valley is about 9 percent wider than the last, so the loss climbs slowly at
first and then faster and faster.

The orange ball leaves the map by step 16, and if you drag **Step** past that, a warning on the map says the run
is off the map. The loss passes 100 by step 25, and the **Loss per step** card marks that moment with a
red note at the top of the chart. A loss that grows without limit like this, because each step is too long
for how sharply the valley bends, is called **divergence**.

### Momentum helps, up to a point

With momentum, the very first step is identical to a plain step, because the ball starts with no speed at
all. After that it builds up speed along the valley floor, which is exactly where plain descent crawls.
With β at 0.8, momentum settles by step 26, while plain descent at the same learning rate has still not
settled by the end of the run.

More momentum is not always faster, though, because at β 0.95 the ball keeps so much of its speed that it
swings up the far side again and again. You can see this on **Loss per step**, where the loss rises and
falls in waves instead of settling. It drops to about 0.06 at step 16, then climbs back above 0.6 by step
20 as the speed carries the ball uphill. There is a best β for this
valley, and it is not the largest one on the slider.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Every number in this lab is computed, from the loss and the tilts to the regime edges and the settling
steps. What makes the lab unrealistic is how small and tidy its problem is, in all of these ways:

- It has only two dials, while a real network has millions to billions of them.
- The eight dots were made up for this lesson rather than collected from the real world.
- Every step uses the exact tilt measured from all eight dots, so the steps have no noise in them.
- The loss is a smooth valley with one lowest point, so it has no false bottoms, no flat plateaus and no
  saddle points (a kind of flat spot explained below).
- You can see the whole map and its lowest point, which is never true when training a real model.
- The learning rate stays fixed for the whole run, with no planned changes and no safety limit on how big
  a step can get.
- Once a run's loss passes one million, the lab stops updating it, so a diverging run stops changing
  instead of growing forever. At learning rate 0.48 that happens at step 76, and at 0.50 it happens at
  step 41.
- The shading on the map only helps your eye, so read loss values from the contour labels and the
  readouts, not from how dark a spot looks.

The fog picture is honest about one thing, which is that gradient descent only ever uses the tilt right
where it stands. It makes everything else easier than real training, because in this lab the tilt you feel
is exact and the valley is smooth with a single bottom.

### Where the picture stops matching

- A hiker picks a comfortable stride, but in gradient descent each stride is the learning rate times the
  tilt, so steps shrink as the ground flattens.
- A hiker walks on a hill with two directions to move in, but a model's hill has one direction for every
  dial, which can mean billions of directions.
- A real hiker wants to reach the bottom and may guess where it is from how the land slopes around them.
  Gradient descent has no goal and makes no guesses. It always steps along the steepest way down from the
  exact spot it is on, even when that points away from the bottom.

### Where real training is harder

A real network's loss is not a simple valley like this one, and its curvature changes from place to place as training moves
through it. So a learning rate that is stable early in a run can turn unstable later, once the run reaches
a more sharply curved region. A real network's lowest point is also unknown, so nobody can measure
"settled" against it the way this lab does.

Real training does not use the exact tilt either, because measuring it from every example in a huge
dataset before every step would be very slow. Instead, each step estimates the tilt from a small random
handful of examples, called a **minibatch**. Gradient descent run this way is called **stochastic gradient
descent**, where the word "stochastic" means "involving chance".

Picture a made-up student council that wants to know which lunch the whole school prefers. Asking every
student would take all week, so they ask ten students picked at random in the lunch queue instead. The
answer comes back quickly, but it is a little off each time, and asking forty students would give a
steadier answer at the cost of more time.

A minibatch works the same way: each step is cheaper but noisier than a step that uses every example. A
larger minibatch gives a steadier direction, but it takes more work to compute each time. That noise is
why real loss curves look jagged, while the curve in this lab is perfectly smooth.

**A common mix-up:** People often picture training getting trapped in a small dip that is not the true
bottom. A point that is lower than everything around it is called a **local minimum**, and on a two-dial
map like this one it is a natural thing to picture. In a model with millions of dials it is rarely the
real problem, because a true dip must curve upward in every one of those directions at once. At a flat
spot where the loss is still high, it is very unlikely that all of those millions of directions curve
upward at once. Usually at least
one direction still leads down, so the run can keep going.

The usual trouble spots are different. A **saddle point** is a spot where the ground is level right
under your feet, but curves down in some directions and up in others. Picture standing in the middle of a mountain pass, where the ground rises
toward the peaks on either side but falls toward the valleys ahead and behind.

In a big model, most flat spots at high loss are saddles rather than dips. Saddles and long, flat
stretches slow real training down more often than bad dips do, because the tilt there is too small to
feel. The dips that large networks do end up in also tend to have similar loss to one another. So landing in one
dip rather than another rarely changes the loss by much.

### Low loss is not the real goal

Loss is a stand-in for what you actually want, which is a model that makes good predictions on data it has
never seen. A line can fit its own eight dots closely and still predict a ninth dot poorly. A small loss
only tells you that the misses on these particular eight dots are small, and nothing about new dots.

Picture a made-up student who prepares for a test by memorising the answers to last year's practice paper.
Their score on that paper becomes perfect, but the real test has new questions, so the memorised answers
do not help at all. A model can fall into the same trap, and the **Training dynamics** lab shows the loss
on practice data still falling while the loss on fresh data rises.

## Going deeper

### Smarter steps: AdamW

Large real training runs rarely use plain descent, and they typically turn to an optimizer called AdamW
instead. AdamW keeps a running average of each dial's tilt and of its squared tilt, and it divides each
dial's step by the square root of that second average. In plain words, it keeps track of how big each
dial's tilts usually are, and it takes smaller steps on dials whose tilts are usually large.

That rescales every direction by its own typical tilt, which is a cheap stand-in for the curvature this
lab works out exactly. As a result, one learning-rate setting can work across very different parts of a
model. On top of that, AdamW shrinks every dial slightly toward zero on each step, a habit called weight
decay, because the dials of a network are often called weights. It applies that nudge to the dials
directly, rather than folding it into the tilt. Keeping it separate means the shrinking is not changed by
AdamW's own step-size adjustments.

### Planned changes to the step size

The learning rate in this lab stays fixed, but real runs change it on purpose as they go, following a plan
called a **learning-rate schedule**. Large runs usually warm up first, starting near zero and growing over
the first few thousand steps, because the earliest tilts are large and unreliable. After that the rate
decays, shrinking along a smooth curve or a straight line, so the late steps can settle into the bottom.

A second safety valve is called gradient clipping, and it checks the size of the tilt before every step.
If the tilt is bigger than a set limit, clipping shrinks it down to that limit, so one strange minibatch
cannot throw the whole run off course.

### Reading real loss curves: cross-entropy and perplexity

Language models are not scored with mean squared error, because their answer is not a number but a choice
of the next piece of text, called a token. For each example, the model gives every possible next token a
chance, written as a number between 0 and 1 and called a probability. Their loss, called
**cross-entropy**, looks only at the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next.

The note under the **Data and fit** card describes cross-entropy for a classifier, which is a model that
picks one answer from a list. There it is written as "−log of the probability it gave the correct class",
which uses a piece of math called a logarithm. You do not need logarithms to follow the idea, because the
pattern in the numbers is what matters:

- If the model gave the correct token a 100% chance, the score is 0, which counts as a perfect answer.
- If it gave the correct token a 50% chance, the score is about 0.7, a small but real cost.
- If it gave the correct token only a 10% chance, the score rises to about 2.3.
- As the chance falls toward zero, the score grows without limit, so a confident wrong answer is very
  costly.

The loss of a language model is usually the average of this score over every token, which keeps runs with
different minibatch sizes fair to compare.

A friendlier way to read that average is **perplexity**, which works out roughly how many choices the
model is torn between. A loss of 2.3 is a perplexity of about 10, as if the model were choosing evenly
among 10 possible tokens. A loss of 2.4 works out to a perplexity of about 11, so even a small drop in
loss can mean about one fewer choice.

### Where the tilt comes from

In this lab the tilt has a short formula, because the model is a single straight line with two dials. A
deep network is a long chain of calculations, far too long for anyone to write that formula out by hand.
The **Backpropagation** lab shows where a real network's tilt comes from instead, working it out one small
piece at a time.
