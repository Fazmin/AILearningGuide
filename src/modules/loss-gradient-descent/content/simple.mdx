## The big picture

Nobody sets the numbers inside an AI model by hand. A model can have millions or even billions of adjustable numbers. Training must find good values for all of them, one small change at a time. This lesson covers the rule that decides each change. It also shows what happens when you use that rule well or badly.

Picture each of those numbers as a dial on a huge control panel. Turning a dial changes what the model does with its input. The rule for turning the dials is called **gradient descent**, and it runs as a loop. First it measures how wrong the model is, using one number called the **loss**. Then it works out which way to turn every dial so the loss falls. Finally it turns each dial a small amount that way, and the loop starts again from the new settings.

The picture you will carry through this lesson is a hiker walking downhill in thick fog. The hiker cannot see the valley floor, but they can feel which way the ground tilts under their feet. So they step downhill, feel the ground again, and repeat until it is level.

### Where you have met it

**A single neuron** showed how a model's dials decide what it outputs. **The math you need** introduced the slope of a curve and a first downhill step. This lab puts those two earlier labs together and uses them to train a real, but very small, model.

Every neural network in this course had its numbers set by some version of this loop. A neural network is a model built from many small neurons. That includes the network in the **Stacking neurons** lab, which trained when you pressed a single button. It also includes the language models in later labs, the kind of program behind a chatbot.

You may have heard people describe training that went badly. They say things like "the loss blew up", "it plateaued" or "the learning rate was too high". You can make each of those happen in this lab. By the end, you will be able to point to each one on the screen.

### Why it matters

A real model has far too many dials for anyone to try every possible setting. Gradient descent gets around this because it only needs the tilt of the ground where the model stands now. That is why it works for models of any size.

The tilt tells you which way to go, but it cannot tell you how big a step is safe. You choose that with a number called the learning rate. A bad choice can ruin a whole training run. Steps that are too small make training crawl. Steps that are too large can make the loss grow without end. This lab shows exactly where the line between those two falls, and why it sits there.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain three ideas in your own words:

- what the **loss** measures and why training pushes it down, even though a small loss can mislead you;
- which way a step of gradient descent will move the dials, read from the lab's numbers;
- how the step size, called the learning rate, and the valley's bend, called its curvature, decide the result. The search may settle, zigzag past the bottom, or fly off.

### What the lab lets you do

The problem is tiny on purpose, so you can see all of it at once. Instead of billions of dials there are only two, so every possible setting fits on a single map. ## What it is

### Walking downhill in fog

Picture yourself on a hillside in fog so thick that you cannot see more than a step ahead. You want to reach the lowest point of the valley, but you have no map. All you can use is the ground under your feet. With each step, you can feel which way the ground tilts and roughly how steep it is.

So you follow a routine. You feel the tilt, take a step downhill, stop, and feel the ground again. Each step uses only what you can feel where you stand. Even so, if you repeat the routine enough times, you end up close to the bottom.

Each part of that picture has a name in machine learning. You will meet every one in the lab:

- **Your height on the hill is the loss.** It is one number that says how wrong the model is, and lower is better.
- **The tilt under your feet is the gradient.** It says which way is uphill and how steep it is, so you step the other way.
- **How far you move is the Learning rate times how steep the tilt is.** You choose the learning rate with a slider.
- **The routine is gradient descent.** You feel the tilt, step the other way and repeat. The word "descent" means going down.

The rest of this section turns each part into something you can see and measure in the lab.

### The model: a line with two dials

The lab shows eight blue dots on a graph. Its model is a straight line that tries to pass as close to all of them as it can. Any straight line can be written as `ŷ = w·x + b`, where `w·x` means w times x. Here `x` is a position along the bottom of the graph. The symbol `ŷ` is read "y-hat", and it is the height of the line at that position. That height is the line's guess for the dot sitting there.

Two dials control the line. The **slope** `w` sets how steeply it climbs. The **intercept** `b` sets the height where it crosses the vertical axis. The numbers a model adjusts while it learns are called its **parameters**, so this small model has exactly two.

The heading of the **Data and fit** card always shows the current line. At the lab's starting point it reads `ŷ = −1.00·x + 1.20`. That means w is −1 and b is 1.2 when the run begins. A negative w makes the line run downhill from left to right. The dots climb from the bottom left to the top right. So the starting line is a poor fit. It was chosen that way on purpose, to give the search somewhere to go.

### Loss: one number for how wrong the line is

To score the line, the lab checks each dot. It measures the gap between the dot and the line, straight up or down. That gap is called a **residual**, and the lab draws each one as a short orange segment. A residual is positive when the line passes above its dot. It is negative when the line passes below.

The lab then squares each residual and takes the average of the eight squares. That average is the loss, and it shows on the **Loss L** readout. The lab also prints the formula in a note under the bars:

```
L = (1/8) Σ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)²
```

The formula looks heavier than it is, because each symbol is one small instruction. The Σ is the Greek capital letter sigma, and it means "add these up". The small i means "do this for dot 1, then dot 2, and so on". The symbol ŷᵢ is the line's guess at dot i, and yᵢ is that dot's real height. So the formula says to take the guess minus the real height for each dot and square it. Then you add up all eight squares and divide by 8.

Here are the first dot and the last dot, worked out for the starting line. For dot 1, the guess is −1.00 × 0.1 + 1.20 = 1.10. For dot 8, it is −1.00 × 1.9 + 1.20 = −0.70. The rest of the arithmetic for these two dots is laid out below:

```
dot 1   x = 0.1   guess  1.10   real height −0.33   miss  1.43   squared  2.04
dot 8   x = 1.9   guess −0.70   real height  2.15   miss −2.85   squared  8.12
```

The bars under the chart show all eight squared misses. These two sit at either end, with 2.04 on the far left and 8.12 on the far right. Do the same for all eight dots, and the squares add up to about 20.09. Divide that total by 8 and you get 2.511, which is the number **Loss L** shows at the start.

Squaring the misses, instead of using them as they are, does two useful jobs:

- **It makes every miss positive.** A line 2 too high and a line 2 too low both pay a cost of 4. Without squaring, misses above and below could cancel out and hide a bad fit.
- **It makes big misses count far more.** One miss of 2 costs 4, which equals four misses of 1. So the line gets pushed hardest to fix its worst misses.

You can check that second job in the numbers printed under the bars. After one step at the starting learning rate, the 8.12 on the far right drops to 4.55. The 2.04 on the far left grows a little, to 2.56. The lab redraws the bars after every change so the tallest one always fills the chart. So compare the printed numbers from step to step, not the bar heights.

The average of the squared misses is called **mean squared error**, because "mean" is another word for average. It suits problems where the right answer is a number, like a height or a price. Language models use a different recipe called cross-entropy, which Going deeper describes.

### Loss as the goal of training

A loss is built so that lower means better. It also changes smoothly when you nudge a dial. That smoothness gives it a tilt you can measure at every possible setting. Together, those two features turn the wish "make the model good" into a clear job. The job is to find the dial settings that give this number its lowest value.

A loss works a little like a golf score. Lower is better, and the whole game is to bring it down. The comparison stops there, because a golf course has a known best score. A model's best loss is usually unknown.

Searching for the best settings of something is called **optimization**. The number being pushed down is called the **objective function**, or the objective for short. Mean squared error and cross-entropy are two different objective functions, and each one decides which misses cost the most.

Training pushes down exactly the number you give it and nothing else. So the objective has to be a good stand-in for what you really care about. Where it breaks shows a case where the two come apart, even while the loss keeps falling.

### The map: the loss surface

Every pair of settings for w and b gives one line, and every line gets one loss. So you can draw a map. Put w along the bottom and b up the side. Mark every position with the loss of the line it stands for. The **Loss surface** card draws exactly that map, worked out from the eight dots for every position in view.

The map uses contour lines, like a hiking map, where each line joins places at the same height. Here each contour joins dial settings that give the same loss. Three of the lines are labelled with their loss, from 0.5 on the inside to 8 farther out. Darker shading means higher loss, so the map gets darker as you move away from the bottom.

The lowest point on the map is marked with a green ×, at w = 1.443 and b = −0.542. The loss there is 0.0205, the smallest value this loss can ever reach. The **Data and fit** card draws the line for that position as a green dotted line. Its legend calls it the least-squares line. That is the best possible line, found directly with a formula and not by stepping.

Even the lowest loss is not zero, because the eight dots do not lie on one straight line. The best line still misses each dot by a little. So 0.0205 is as low as the loss can go for these dots.

Look at the shape of the contours too. They are long, thin ovals, so the map shows a long, narrow valley and not a round bowl. The ground rises steeply as you move across this valley and only gently as you move along its floor. That difference matters a great deal.

### The gradient: which way is uphill

Back in the fog, you could feel the tilt under your feet. The lab gives you that tilt as two numbers under the map, **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b**. This lesson calls them tilts to keep them apart from the line's slope `w`. You will often hear them called slopes as well.

The first one, **∂L/∂w**, answers one question. Suppose you nudge w up a tiny amount while b holds still. Does the loss go up or down, and how fast? You can read it aloud as "the change in L for a small change in w". The curly ∂ is a kind of letter d. It means that only one dial moves while every other dial stays fixed. The second readout, **∂L/∂b**, asks the same question about the other dial, b.

The sign of each tilt tells you which way descent will turn that dial:

- **A positive tilt:** Raising the dial would raise the loss, so descent turns the dial down.
- **A negative tilt:** Raising the dial would lower the loss, so descent turns the dial up.
- **A tilt close to zero:** Turning the dial barely changes the loss from where you stand right now.

At the starting point, **∂L/∂w** reads −3.039 and **∂L/∂b** reads −1.403. Both tilts are negative, so raising either dial would lower the loss. The w tilt is about twice as big once you ignore the minus signs. So the loss reacts more strongly to w than to b at this position.

Taken together, the two tilts make up the **gradient**. You can read the pair as an arrow on the map. Picture an arrow that moves −3.039 in the w direction and −1.403 in the b direction. Here w runs along the bottom of the map and b runs up the side. So that arrow points left and a little down. It points the way the loss climbs fastest, straight uphill. So gradient descent flips it and steps right and a little up instead.

On the map, the lab draws that downhill direction as a pink arrow labelled **−∇L**. The upside-down triangle ∇ is the usual symbol for a gradient. So −∇L means "minus the gradient of the loss", the steepest way downhill from where you stand. The arrow is always the same length because only its direction matters here. The two readouts below the map give its size.

Check two things about that arrow with your own eyes before you move on:

- **It crosses the nearest contour line at a right angle.** Moving along a contour line leaves the loss unchanged, so any step along the line is wasted. The steepest way down has no wasted part, so it cuts straight across. Rain running down a real hillside crosses the contour lines on a hiking map in the same way.
- **It does not point at the green ×.** It shows the steepest way down from this one position, not the direction of the bottom. In a long, narrow valley, those two directions can be very far apart.

### One step, worked out

A gradient descent step moves each dial against its tilt, by the learning rate times the tilt. In words, the rule for each dial looks like this:

```
new value = old value − learning rate × tilt
```

The learning rate starts at 0.1 in this lab. Its slider shows it as "η 0.10", using the Greek letter eta, the usual symbol for a learning rate. Here is one full step from the starting point at that rate, with both dials worked out:

```
w:   −1.000 − 0.1 × (−3.039)   =   −1.000 + 0.304   =   −0.696
b:    1.200 − 0.1 × (−1.403)   =    1.200 + 0.140   =    1.340
```

Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding. So both dials go up, exactly as the negative tilts predicted. The new line fits the dots better, and **Loss L** falls from 2.511 to 1.619 after this single step.

Now picture doubling the learning rate to 0.2 before you take that first step. Each change doubles too. The changes become +0.608 for w and +0.281 for b. The step points the same way but is twice as long. The learning rate can stretch a step, but it can never turn one. The direction always comes from the gradient alone.

Think of turning the hot tap in a shower. A big twist overshoots to scalding water, and a tiny twist takes forever to warm up. The learning rate is the size of your twist. The comparison stops at direction, because you choose which way to twist a tap. In gradient descent, the gradient chooses it for you.

### Curvature: how sharply the valley bends

The tilt tells you how steep the ground is where you stand. **Curvature** tells you how quickly that tilt changes as you move. In other words, curvature is how sharply the ground bends. A narrow canyon bends sharply, because the ground goes from steeply down to steeply up within a few steps. A wide valley floor barely bends at all.

The valley in this lab lies at a slant on the map. So "across" and "along" are not the w and b directions, and each one mixes both dials. The lab calls the direction across the valley the **steep axis**. It calls the direction along the floor the **shallow axis**. They are two slanted directions of their own, not the w and b axes of the map.

In this lab's valley, the curvature across the valley is 4.363, but along the floor it is only 0.307. To see what that means, picture moving 0.1 across the valley. The tilt in that direction changes by about 0.44, which is 4.363 × 0.1. Moving 0.1 along the floor changes the tilt there by only about 0.03.

So the valley bends about 14 times more sharply across than along. The lab writes the larger curvature as λ_max, using the Greek letter lambda, and max is short for maximum. You will see that symbol on the ruler under the **Learning rate** slider.

Curvature puts a speed limit on your steps. To see why, picture a step across a narrow canyon toward its floor. A short step takes you partway down the wall. A longer step can carry you right past the floor and up the far wall. A step that is too long lands you higher on the far wall than where you started. The ground there is even steeper, so your next step is even longer.

The lab can measure this exactly. Plain descent is the basic method you have used so far. In this valley, each step multiplies your distance from the valley floor by one fixed number. Here is why:

- Each step is the learning rate times the tilt, so a steeper tilt means a longer step.
- Across this valley, the tilt is the curvature times your distance from the floor. The farther out you are, the steeper it gets.
- So one step removes learning rate × curvature of that distance, and what is left is 1 minus that amount.

Take a learning rate of 0.20. One step removes 0.20 × 4.363, about 0.87, of the gap, so about 0.13 of it is left. The **Loss per step** card shows this number as **Steep axis ×**. The name means "your distance across the valley is multiplied by this on every step". Here it is worked out for three learning rates that you will try in the lab:

```
η 0.20   1 − 0.20 × 4.363  =   0.13    13% of the gap is left, on the same side
η 0.40   1 − 0.40 × 4.363  =  −0.75    lands on the far side, and the gap shrinks to 75%
η 0.48   1 − 0.48 × 4.363  =  −1.09    lands on the far side, and the gap grows by 9%
```

A negative number means the step jumps across the floor and lands on the far wall. That is fine as long as the number's size, ignoring the minus sign, stays under 1. Then each jump is smaller than the one before. Once the size goes past 1, each jump is bigger than the last and the run flies off.

So two learning rates mark the edges between three kinds of behaviour, which the lab calls **regimes**. You can find both edges from the multiplier:

- **The first edge is at 0.229.** There the number 1 − 0.229 × 4.363 comes to 0. So one step lands exactly on the floor. With any bigger rate, the number turns negative and the step jumps past the floor. That is why the first edge is 1 divided by the curvature.
- **The second edge is at 0.458.** There the number reaches −1. So each jump lands exactly as far out on the other side as where it started. With any bigger rate, each jump grows and the run blows up. That is why the second edge is 2 divided by the curvature.

The ruler under the **Learning rate** slider colours all three regimes. A badge beside the **Loss surface** heading names the one you are in:

- **Below 0.229:** Every step lands on the same side of the floor. The lab calls this regime **one-sided**.
- **Above 0.229 but below 0.458:** Steps jump across the floor and zigzag, but the zigzag keeps shrinking. The lab calls this regime **overshoots**.
- **Above 0.458:** Each zigzag is wider than the last and the loss grows without limit. The lab calls this regime **diverges**. Every step multiplies the gap by the same number, so starting closer to the floor only delays the blow-up. Only a smaller learning rate stops it.

The note under the ruler writes the same two edges with symbols, as `1/λ_max = 0.229` and `2/λ_max = 0.458`.

### The slow direction along the floor

The steep direction sets the speed limit. The gentle direction along the floor sets the pace of the whole run. The same rule works along the floor. Each step multiplies your distance from the bottom by 1 minus the learning rate times 0.307. Because 0.307 is so small, that number stays close to 1. So each step closes only a small part of the remaining distance. The lab shows this number as **Shallow axis ×**.

Set the learning rate to 0.20, and **Shallow axis ×** reads 0.939, which is very close to 1. Each step removes only about 6 percent of the distance still left along the floor. So the run needs dozens of steps to finish.

You cannot fix that by raising the learning rate very far, because the steep direction blows up above 0.458. The best rate for this valley is about 0.43. At that rate, the gaps across the valley and along the floor shrink at the same pace. Even then, the gap along the floor shrinks by only about 13 percent a step. One learning rate has to serve both directions at once. So a valley that is steep one way and nearly flat the other way is slow to finish.

If you try 0.43 in the lab, **Settles by** reads step 21. That is a little later than the step 19 you will see at 0.40. It is not a mistake. "Best" here means the slower of the two directions shrinks as fast as it can. At 0.43, both **Steep axis ×** and **Shallow axis ×** come to about 0.87 in size. At 0.40, the floor shrinks almost as fast, at 0.877. The zigzag across the valley dies down much faster, at −0.75. From where this run starts, that quicker zigzag lets 0.40 finish a couple of steps sooner.

### Momentum: a rolling ball

So far, the hiker stops dead after every step and starts the next one from rest. **Momentum** changes that. Picture a ball rolling on the same hill, carrying some of its speed from one moment to the next. On a long, steady slope the ball keeps picking up speed. That is exactly what the slow valley floor needs.

In the lab, the ball keeps a running speed called its velocity. The lab updates it on every step before the dials move. The new velocity is the old velocity times a number β, plus the current tilt. Then each dial moves by the learning rate times the velocity. The number β is the Greek letter beta. You set it with the **Momentum β** slider, and it decides how much of the old speed carries over.

The ball picture needs one small correction. A real ball is slowed by friction, which takes away some of its speed all the time. In the lab, β alone decides how much speed survives each step. A β near 1 is like a ball on almost frictionless ice, which keeps nearly all its speed. A β of 0 is like so much friction that the ball stops dead after every step.

To see what that rule does, suppose β is 0.8 and the tilt stays at 1 on every step. That is what happens on a steady slope along the valley floor:

```
step 1:   0.8 × 0      + 1   =   1.00
step 2:   0.8 × 1.00   + 1   =   1.80
step 3:   0.8 × 1.80   + 1   =   2.44
step 4:   0.8 × 2.44   + 1   =   2.95
```

The velocity keeps creeping toward 5 without ever quite reaching it. That limit is 1 divided by (1 − β). So on a steady slope, momentum ends up taking steps about five times as long as plain descent would.

Now suppose the tilt flips sign on every step, +1 then −1 then +1. That is what happens when the path zigzags across a narrow valley:

```
step 1:   0.8 × 0        + 1   =    1.00
step 2:   0.8 × 1.00     − 1   =   −0.20
step 3:   0.8 × (−0.20)  + 1   =    0.84
```

The pushes in opposite directions partly cancel each other out, so the velocity stays small. This cancelling only helps when the steps are already zigzagging, as plain descent does at learning rates above 0.229.

At a small learning rate like 0.05, plain descent does not zigzag at all. There, momentum's carried speed is what makes the path swing past the floor. That swinging is the price of momentum. A ball that has built up speed does not stop at the bottom. It rolls past the floor and partway up the other side before it turns back. With too much momentum, it keeps swinging back and forth for a long time.

Keep two facts in mind when you read the lab. First, momentum's opening step is exactly a plain step, because the velocity starts at zero. Second, setting β to 0 keeps none of the old speed, which turns momentum back into plain descent exactly. Plain descent and momentum are two different optimizers, meaning two different rules for turning tilts into steps. The **Optimizer** switch in the lab picks between them.

## Why it’s here

### Opening the button from the last lab

The previous lab, **Stacking neurons**, trained a whole network every time you pressed a single button. It never showed what happened inside. This lab opens that button to show the loop inside. It also adds three things the button hid: curvature, the three regimes, and momentum.

### Why not work out the answer directly?

For a single straight line there is a shortcut. A direct formula called least squares gives the best w and b in one go. That formula is how the lab knows where to put the green ×, without walking there.

A real network has no such shortcut. Its loss depends on millions or billions of dials in a far more tangled way. Its lowest point is unknown and cannot be found in one go. So training has no target to aim at. What can be worked out cheaply is the tilt at the current point, for every dial at once. The Backpropagation lab shows how that is done. Gradient descent needs nothing more than that tilt. That is why the same rule can train models of any size.

That is also why the fog picture fits a real network even better than it fits this lab. Here you can see the whole map. A real model's map has one direction for every dial, so nobody could ever draw it. Training a real model really is walking in fog. You have only the tilt at your current position.

### Where the words for broken training come from

When training goes wrong, people describe it with a few stock phrases. Each one is a shape you can make in this lab:

- **"The loss exploded":** The learning rate is past the stability limit, which is the 0.458 edge in this lab. Each swing across a steep direction grows bigger.
- **"It plateaued":** The loss stopped falling for a long stretch. This is often a direction with very little curvature, like the flat floor of this valley.
- **"It oscillates":** The run keeps swinging back and forth. That is overshoot across a steep direction, like the zigzag path you will see at 0.40.

Picture a made-up team that leaves a model training overnight. In the morning, they find that the loss curve has shot off the top of the chart. The first few steps looked fine, but after that the loss climbed faster and faster. Someone says "the learning rate was too high". After this lab, you can say exactly what that means. Each step was long enough that every swing across a steep valley landed farther out than the one before.

## How to play with it

The lab walks you through four steps, named Measure error, Read the slope, Take steps and Compare momentum. This section follows the same order.

### 1. Measure the error by hand

Start at step 0, which is where the lab opens. Drag **Start w** and **Start b** while you watch the **Data and fit** card. You can also click anywhere on the **Loss surface** map to start the run from that position. As you drag, the purple line turns and slides, and the orange residuals grow and shrink. Each bar below the chart shows one squared miss.

Keep an eye on **Loss L** while you drag. A dashed line across the bars marks their average, and that average is always what **Loss L** shows. The default start has **Start w** at −1.00 and **Start b** at 1.20, and this lesson uses it throughout. At that start, **Loss L** reads 2.511, the same value worked out step by step earlier in this lesson.

See how low you can push **Loss L** by hand, using the green dotted line as a guide. Lining the purple line up with it gets you close to 0.0205. Nothing you try gets any lower. When you are done, set **Start w** back to −1.00 and **Start b** back to 1.20. Then your numbers match this lesson.

### 2. Predict the first step

Stay at step 0 and read **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b**, which are −3.039 and −1.403 at the default start. Before you press anything, use their signs to predict which way w and b will move.

Both tilts are negative, so descent will raise both dials. On this map, the first step should head up and to the right. That is what "raise both dials" looks like here. Remember that w runs along the bottom of the map and b runs up the side. Check that the pink **−∇L** arrow points that way. It should cross the nearest contour at a right angle and miss the green ×. It misses because it shows the steepest way down from where you stand. In this narrow valley, that direction points mostly across the valley and not toward the bottom.

### 3. Take steps and change the step size

**Try this:** Press **Take one gradient step** a few times. The orange ball marks where the run is now, and it moves across the map. The solid blue path shows the steps taken so far. The dashed blue line shows where the full 80-step run goes. Then drag **Step** to 80 to see the whole run. Set **Learning rate** to 0.05, 0.20, 0.40 and 0.48 in turn. Each time, read **Settles by** on the **Loss per step** card and the regime badge on the **Loss surface**.

**Settles by** names the first step after which the loss stays within 0.01 of the lowest possible loss. It must stay there all the way to step 80. A run that dips close to the bottom and then swings back out does not count as settled. Here is what you should see at each of the four learning rates:

- **At 0.05:** The badge says one-sided and **Settles by** reads "not in 80". The run is too slow to finish within 80 steps.
- **At 0.20:** The badge still says one-sided, but the bigger steps now let the run settle by step 39.
- **At 0.40:** The badge says overshoots, and the path zigzags across the valley. Even so, the run settles sooner, by step 19.
- **At 0.48:** The badge says diverges and **Settles by** reads "not in 80" again. The path flies right off the map.

Watch the shape of the dashed blue line change as you go. At 0.05 it slides smoothly down into the valley and then along the floor. At 0.40 it bounces from wall to wall on its way to the bottom. The runs at 0.05 and 0.48 both show "not in 80", but for opposite reasons. That is why you need the badge to tell them apart. The lab opens at a learning rate of 0.10. That settles by step 78, with very little time to spare.

You can press **Back to step 0** at any time to replay a run from its start. The dashed blue line always shows the full run. So you can see where a run is heading before you step through it.

### 4. Compare momentum

Put **Learning rate** back to 0.05 and switch **Optimizer** from **Plain descent** to **Momentum**. A new **Momentum β** slider appears, set to 0.90. Try **Momentum β** at 0.5, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95, and read **Settles by** each time. Remember that plain descent at this rate had not settled after 80 steps.

- **At β 0.5:** The run settles by step 77, which is only a little better than plain descent.
- **At β 0.8:** It settles by step 26, the fastest of the four settings you try.
- **At β 0.9:** This is the slider's starting value, and it settles by step 46. More momentum has already made things slower.
- **At β 0.95:** It never settles within 80 steps, because the ball keeps swinging past the bottom.

Watch the path on the map as well. Under momentum it swings past the valley floor and curls back. It can also overshoot the green × along the length of the valley, not only across it. In Momentum mode, the **Steep axis ×** readout adds the word "swing" when the path swings across the valley. At learning rate 0.05 it shows for every β you try. Watch the map to see the overshoot along the valley. In plain mode the readout never says "swing", and a zigzag shows as a negative number instead.

## What to notice

### The direction comes from the gradient alone

The learning rate makes every step longer or shorter, but it never turns a step in a new direction. Say the pink arrow points right and up. Then the first step goes right and up at every learning rate you choose, and only its length changes. That is why too large a learning rate overshoots across the valley instead of finding a better route. It can stretch the step, but it can never aim it.

### Steps shrink on their own near the bottom

Watch the dots along the solid blue path as a run nears the green ×. You will see them bunch closer and closer together. Nothing in the lab changed the learning rate along the way, so the shrinking must come from somewhere else. The tilt gets smaller as the ground flattens out near the bottom. Each step is the learning rate times the tilt, so the steps shrink by themselves.

Settling down like this is called **convergence**. It means the loss stops improving because the run has reached the bottom. Right at the bottom the tilt is exactly zero. A step taken there would not move the dials at all.

### A fast drop, then a long crawl

Here the steepest way down points mostly across the valley, not along it toward the bottom. So a plain run drops quickly down the steep wall. Then it crawls along the nearly flat floor for a long time.

You can see both parts on the **Loss per step** card. It draws the loss after every step of the run. Its vertical axis uses a log scale. Each gridline stands for ten times the value of the gridline below it. That spacing lets a loss near 2.5 and a loss near 0.02 fit on the same chart. An ordinary scale could not show both clearly.

At learning rate 0.05, the curve drops fast for a few steps. Then it bends into a long, gentle slope. The fast drop is the steep direction finishing its work. The slow tail is the shallow direction along the floor. **Shallow axis ×** reads 0.985 at this rate. So each step closes only 1.5 percent of the distance left along the floor. That slow pace stretches the tail out across the rest of the chart.

### Zigzagging is not the same as blowing up

Between 0.229 and 0.458, the orange ball lands on the far wall with every step. Even so, the loss still falls on every step. Try learning rate 0.40 and watch the path zigzag across the valley while the loss curve heads steadily downward. Each landing is closer to the floor than the one before. So overshooting by this much is not a failure, and here it even settles sooner than 0.20 does. The reason is the slow direction along the floor. It closes faster at the bigger rate, because **Shallow axis ×** drops from 0.939 to 0.877.

Above 0.458 the story changes, and a learning rate of 0.48 shows how quietly the trouble can begin. The first step still lowers the loss, from 2.511 to 2.392. So the start of the run looks like progress. After that, every swing across the valley is about 9 percent wider than the last. The loss climbs slowly at first and then faster and faster.

The orange ball leaves the map by step 16. If you drag **Step** past that, a warning on the map says the run is off the map. The loss passes 100 by step 25. The **Loss per step** card marks that moment with a red note at the top of the chart. A loss that grows without limit like this is called **divergence**. It happens because each step is too long for how sharply the valley bends.

### Momentum helps, up to a point

With momentum, the very first step is identical to a plain step. The ball starts with no speed at all. After that it builds up speed along the valley floor, which is exactly where plain descent crawls. With β at 0.8, momentum settles by step 26. Plain descent at the same learning rate has still not settled by the end of the run.

More momentum is not always faster, though. At β 0.95 the ball keeps so much speed that it swings up the far side again and again. You can see this on **Loss per step**, where the loss rises and falls in waves instead of settling. It drops to about 0.06 at step 16. Then it climbs back above 0.6 by step 20, as the speed carries the ball uphill. There is a best β for this valley, and it is not the largest one on the slider.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Every number in this lab is computed. That covers the loss, the tilts, the regime edges and the settling steps. What makes the lab unrealistic is how small and tidy its problem is, in all of these ways:

- It has only two dials, while a real network has millions to billions of them.
- The eight dots were made up for this lesson and were not collected from the real world.
- Every step uses the exact tilt measured from all eight dots, so the steps have no noise in them.
- The loss is a smooth valley with one lowest point, so it has no false bottoms or plateaus. It also has no saddle points, a kind of flat spot explained below.
- You can see the whole map and its lowest point, which is never true when training a real model.
- The learning rate stays fixed all run, with no planned changes and no cap on step size.
- Once a run's loss passes one million, the lab stops updating it. So a diverging run freezes and does not grow forever. At learning rate 0.48 that happens at step 76, and at 0.50 it happens at step 41.
- The shading on the map only helps your eye. Read loss values from the contour labels and the readouts, not from how dark a spot looks.

The fog picture is honest about one thing. Gradient descent only ever uses the tilt right where it stands. Everything else is easier here than in real training. The tilt you feel is exact, and the valley is smooth with a single bottom.

### Where the picture stops matching

- **Stride:** A hiker picks a comfortable stride. In gradient descent each stride is the learning rate times the tilt, so steps shrink as the ground flattens.
- **Directions:** A hiker walks on a hill with two directions to move in. A model's hill has one direction for every dial, which can mean billions of directions.
- **Goal:** A real hiker wants to reach the bottom and may guess where it is. Gradient descent has no goal and makes no guesses. It always takes the steepest way down, even when that leads away from the bottom.

### Where real training is harder

A real network's loss is not a simple valley like this one. Its curvature changes from place to place as training moves through it. So a learning rate that is stable early can turn unstable later. The run may reach a more sharply curved region. A real network's lowest point is also unknown. So nobody can measure "settled" against it the way this lab does.

Real training does not use the exact tilt either. Measuring it from every example in a huge dataset before every step would be very slow. Instead, each step estimates the tilt from a small random handful of examples, called a **minibatch**. Gradient descent run this way is called **stochastic gradient descent**, and "stochastic" means "involving chance".

Picture a made-up student council that wants to know which lunch the whole school prefers. Asking every student would take all week, so they ask ten students picked at random in the lunch queue. The answer comes back quickly, but it is a little off each time. Asking forty students would give a steadier answer, at the cost of more time.

A minibatch works the same way. Each step is cheaper but noisier than a step that uses every example. A larger minibatch gives a steadier direction, but it takes more work to compute each time. That noise is why real loss curves look jagged, while the curve in this lab is perfectly smooth.

**A common mix-up:** People often picture training getting trapped in a small dip that is not the true bottom. A point that is lower than everything around it is called a **local minimum**. On a two-dial map like this one, it is a natural thing to picture. In a model with millions of dials, it is rarely the real problem. A true dip must curve upward in every one of those directions at once. At a flat spot where the loss is still high, that is very unlikely. Usually at least one direction still leads down, so the run can keep going.

The usual trouble spots are different. A **saddle point** is a spot where the ground is level under your feet. It curves down in some directions and up in others. Picture standing in the middle of a mountain pass. The ground rises toward the peaks on either side and falls toward the valleys ahead and behind.

In a big model, most flat spots at high loss are saddles and not dips. Saddles and long, flat stretches slow real training more often than bad dips do. The tilt there is too small to feel. The dips that large networks do end up in also tend to have similar loss to one another. So landing in one dip instead of another rarely changes the loss by much.

### Low loss is not the real goal

Loss is a stand-in for what you actually want. You want a model that predicts well on data it has never seen. A line can fit its own eight dots closely and still predict a ninth dot poorly. A small loss only tells you that the misses on these particular eight dots are small. It says nothing about new dots.

Picture a made-up student who prepares for a test by memorising the answers to last year's practice paper. Their score on that paper becomes perfect. But the real test has new questions, so the memorised answers do not help. A model can fall into the same trap. The **Training dynamics** lab shows the loss on practice data still falling while the loss on fresh data rises.

## Going deeper

### Smarter steps: AdamW

Large real training runs rarely use plain descent. They typically turn to an optimizer called AdamW instead. AdamW keeps a running average of each dial's tilt and of its squared tilt. It divides each dial's step by the square root of that second average. In plain words, it tracks how big each dial's tilts usually are. It takes smaller steps on dials whose tilts are usually large.

That rescales every direction by its own typical tilt. It is a cheap stand-in for the curvature this lab works out exactly. As a result, one learning-rate setting can work across very different parts of a model. On top of that, AdamW shrinks every dial slightly toward zero on each step. This habit is called weight decay, because the dials of a network are often called weights. AdamW applies that nudge to the dials directly, and does not fold it into the tilt. Keeping it separate means AdamW's own step-size adjustments do not change the shrinking.

### Planned changes to the step size

The learning rate in this lab stays fixed. Real runs change it on purpose as they go, following a plan called a **learning-rate schedule**. Large runs usually warm up first. The rate starts near zero and grows over the first few thousand steps. The earliest tilts are large and unreliable. After that the rate decays along a smooth curve or a straight line. This lets the late steps settle into the bottom.

A second safety valve is called gradient clipping. It checks the size of the tilt before every step. If the tilt is bigger than a set limit, clipping shrinks it down to that limit. That way, one strange minibatch cannot throw the whole run off course.

### Reading real loss curves: cross-entropy and perplexity

Language models are not scored with mean squared error, because their answer is not a number. Their answer is a choice of the next piece of text, called a token. For each example, the model gives every possible next token a chance. This chance is a number between 0 and 1, called a probability. Their loss is called **cross-entropy**. It looks only at the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next.

The note under the **Data and fit** card describes cross-entropy for a classifier. A classifier is a model that picks one answer from a list. There it is written as "−log of the probability it gave the correct class". That uses a piece of math called a logarithm. You do not need logarithms to follow the idea, because the pattern in the numbers is what matters:

- **A 100% chance on the correct token:** The score is 0, which counts as a perfect answer.
- **A 50% chance on the correct token:** The score is about 0.7, a small but real cost.
- **A 10% chance on the correct token:** The score rises to about 2.3.
- **A chance that falls toward zero:** The score grows without limit, so a confident wrong answer is very costly.

The loss of a language model is usually the average of this score over every token. That keeps runs with different minibatch sizes fair to compare.

A friendlier way to read that average is **perplexity**. It tells you roughly how many choices the model is torn between. A loss of 2.3 is a perplexity of about 10. It is as if the model chose evenly among 10 tokens. A loss of 2.4 works out to a perplexity of about 11. So even a small drop in loss can mean about one fewer choice.

### Where the tilt comes from

In this lab the tilt has a short formula, because the model is one straight line with two dials. A deep network is a long chain of calculations. It is far too long for anyone to write that formula by hand. The **Backpropagation** lab shows where a real network's tilt comes from instead. It works the tilt out one small piece at a time.
