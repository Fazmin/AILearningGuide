## The big picture

This module is about how a model's parameters get their values. Training reduces "fit the data" to one
number, the **loss**, then repeatedly moves every parameter a short way downhill on it. Gradient descent
never sees the whole landscape: it works only from the slope at the points it has visited.

**A single neuron** showed weights and a bias setting a model's output, and **The math you need**
introduced the derivative and one gradient step. This module differentiates the loss with respect to
every parameter. The **gradient** sets each step's direction, the **learning rate** scales its length,
and **curvature**, how fast the slope changes along a direction, decides which learning rates are safe.

### Where it shows up

- **This course.** Every neural network the course trains learns its weights by some version of this
  loop. The later language models minimize cross-entropy, the average `−log p` of the correct token,
  instead of squared error.
- **Large-scale training.** Real runs estimate each step's gradient from a small random **minibatch** of
  examples rather than the full dataset, so every step points in a noisy direction.
- **Production optimizers.** Real training usually swaps plain descent for **AdamW**, which rescales each
  parameter's step by its own typical gradient size. Those runs pair AdamW with a warmup-then-decay
  learning-rate schedule and with gradient clipping, which caps an oversized gradient.

### Why it matters

Nobody can locate a real network's minimum in advance, so training cannot aim at it. Training can only
measure the slope where the parameters sit and step downhill. That local slope is all gradient descent
needs, which is why the same rule scales from this lab's two parameters to billions.

The gradient never says how far to go, and the safe distance depends on curvature. Past a limit set by
the most curved direction, each step overshoots further and the loss grows without bound. Below it, that
same cap keeps steps small along the flattest directions, which close in slowly. **Momentum**, a velocity
built from past gradients, speeds those flat directions at the cost of overshoot.

### What you will be able to explain

- Interpret loss as an optimization objective: one number for a model's error on its data, where lower
  is better and a low training value is a proxy, not the goal.
- Predict a gradient step's direction from the partial derivatives, each parameter moving against the
  sign of its own by an amount set by its size, and explain why the step crosses the contour (equal-loss
  line) through the current point at a right angle rather than aiming at the minimum.
- Explain how the learning rate and curvature decide whether descent settles, overshoots, or diverges,
  and why a narrow valley makes plain descent crawl along its floor.

### What the lab shows

The lab runs real gradient descent, not a sketch. It fits `ŷ = w·x + b` to eight fixed, authored points
by mean squared error, so the whole two-parameter loss surface fits on screen. The loss and gradient are
closed-form, and the regime boundaries follow from the bowl's exact curvature. Every step is computed,
noise-free, and replays identically.

**Loss surface** draws the contours, the path, and the pink **−∇L** arrow, with **Loss L**, **∂L/∂w** and

shows the squared residuals, each point's vertical miss squared, whose mean is the loss. **Loss per step** plots the same run on a log scale

On **Optimizer controls**, **Start w** and **Start b**, or a click on the surface, set the start.
**Take one gradient step** moves one step forward, and the **Step** slider shows any step of the run,
forward or back. The key experiment sweeps **Learning rate** and compares the path, the regime badge,
and **Settles by**, then switches **Optimizer** to **Momentum** and varies **Momentum β**.

## What it is

Training is a search for parameter values that make one number small. **Loss** is that number: how wrong
the model is on its data, with lower meaning better. The **gradient** collects the loss's slope with
respect to every parameter. It points in the direction of steepest increase, so **gradient descent**
subtracts it:

`θ ← θ − η · ∇L`

The gradient supplies the direction. The **Learning rate** `η` supplies the distance.

### The lab's loss is real

The lab fits a line `ŷ = w·x + b` to eight fixed points. Its loss is the mean squared error
`L(w, b) = (1/8) Σ (w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)²`. With only two parameters, the whole landscape fits on screen, and
each contour line joins the `(w, b)` pairs that share one loss value.

The gradient is exact: `∂L/∂w = (2/8) Σ rᵢxᵢ` and `∂L/∂b = (2/8) Σ rᵢ`, where `rᵢ = ŷᵢ − yᵢ` is the
residual. Nothing on screen is sketched.

### Two common losses

- **Mean squared error** averages squared misses. It suits numeric targets. Squaring makes one miss of 2
  cost as much as four misses of 1.
- **Cross-entropy** averages `−log p`, where `p` is the probability the model gave the correct class or
  token. It trains every language model later in the course.

### Curvature sets the speed limit

This bowl is about 14 times more curved across its valley (`λ_max = 4.363`) than along it
(`λ_min = 0.307`). Along each of those axes, one plain step multiplies the remaining error by `1 − ηλ`:

- Below `1/λ_max = 0.229`, every step lands on the same side of the valley floor.
- Between 0.229 and `2/λ_max = 0.458`, steps overshoot the floor and zigzag, but the zigzag shrinks.
- Above 0.458, each zigzag is wider than the last and the run diverges.

The shallow axis is the bottleneck. Even at the best rate, `η = 2/(λ_max + λ_min) ≈ 0.43`, the error
shrinks by only about 13 percent per step.

### What momentum adds

**Momentum** keeps a velocity: `v ← βv + ∇L`, then `θ ← θ − ηv`. Along the valley floor the gradient
keeps pointing the same way, so the step grows toward `1/(1 − β)` plain steps. Across the valley the
gradient flips sign and the velocity partly cancels. The price is overshoot: velocity carries the point
past the floor before it turns back.

## Why it’s here

The previous lab, Stacking neurons, trained a network behind one button. This lab opens that button and
adds what it hid: curvature, the learning-rate regimes, and momentum.

It is also where the vocabulary of failed training comes from. "The loss exploded" is a learning rate past
the stability limit. "It plateaued" is often a direction with very little curvature. "It oscillates" is
overshoot across a steep direction.

## How to play with it

Stay at step 0 first. Drag **Start w** and **Start b**, or click the **Loss surface**, and watch **Data and
fit**: each bar is one squared residual, and their mean is **Loss L**. The default start reads 2.511.

Still at step 0, read **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b**: −3.039 and −1.403 at the default start. Both are negative,
so descent increases both `w` and `b`. The pink **−∇L** arrow crosses the nearest contour at a right angle,
and it does not point at the minimum.

Press **Take one gradient step** a few times, then drag **Step** to 80. Set **Learning rate** to 0.05,
0.20, 0.40, and 0.48, and read **Settles by** and the regime badge each time. The measured results are:
not within 80 steps, step 39, step 19 while overshooting, and diverges.

Finally, return to 0.05 and switch **Optimizer** to **Momentum**. Compare **Momentum β** at 0.5, 0.8, and
0.95 with plain descent.

## What to notice

- Direction comes from the gradient alone. The learning rate scales every step and never turns one.
- Steps shrink on their own near the minimum because the gradient shrinks there. That needs no schedule.
- In a narrow valley the steepest direction points mostly across the valley, not toward the minimum. So
  plain descent drops quickly, then crawls along the floor.
- A learning rate that is too large does more than waste steps. Past 0.458 the swing across the valley
  grows on every step. At 0.48 the first step still lowers the loss, from 2.511 to 2.392, but it passes
  100 by step 25.
- Momentum's first step equals plain descent's, because velocity starts at zero. At η 0.05, β 0.8 settles
  by step 26 while plain descent has not settled by step 80. At β 0.95 the run spirals and does not settle
  within 80 steps either. More momentum is not always faster.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The loss is a real mean squared error on eight authored points. Every step, gradient,
regime boundary, and settling step is computed. What makes it unrealistic is how easy it is: two
parameters, the exact full-data gradient, a perfectly quadratic bowl with one minimum, and a minimum you
can see. The contour shading is a display layer, not a colour scale you can read values from.

**In real systems.** A network has millions to billions of parameters, so its landscape cannot be drawn and
its minimum is unknown. The loss is not quadratic, so curvature changes during training, and a learning
rate that was stable early can turn unstable later. Each step uses a minibatch gradient, a noisy estimate
of the true direction.

"Stuck in a local minimum" also transfers badly. In high dimensions, most flat points at high loss are
saddles: downhill in some directions and uphill in others. Saddles and long flat regions slow real training
more often than bad minima do.

Low training loss is not the goal either. The **Training dynamics** lab shows training loss falling while
held-out loss rises.

## Going deeper

Production runs rarely use plain descent. **AdamW** keeps running averages of each parameter's gradient and
squared gradient, and divides each step by the square root of the second. That rescales every direction by
its own typical gradient size, a cheap stand-in for the curvature this lab computes exactly. AdamW also
applies weight decay directly to the parameters rather than through the gradient.

**Learning-rate schedules** matter as much as the optimizer. Large runs warm up from near zero over the
first few thousand steps, then decay along a cosine or linear path. Gradient clipping rescales any gradient
whose norm exceeds a threshold before the update, so one odd minibatch cannot throw the run off.

Two conventions help when reading real curves. Loss is usually a per-token average, so runs with different
batch sizes stay comparable. For language models, cross-entropy in nats converts to perplexity as
`e^loss`: a loss of 2.4 is a perplexity of about 11.0, and 2.3 is about 10.0.
