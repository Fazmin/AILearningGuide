## The big picture

This module is about **transfer learning**: reusing weights trained on one distribution as the
starting point for another. **Full fine-tuning** does it by continuing ordinary training on the new
data with every parameter free to move. The question is what that buys on the new corpus, and what
the same weights lose on the old one.

It builds on *Train one from scratch*: the same kind of 900-weight character bigram table, trained
by the same minibatch SGD on cross-entropy, except that training now starts from weights already
fitted to another corpus. It then adds
two levers on the trade, freezing and replay, and the decision that comes first: whether to
fine-tune at all.

### Where it shows up

- **Specializing a pretrained model.** Almost no model in production is trained from scratch for its
  job. Llama 2's supervised fine-tune is one documented case, and Hugging Face TRL provides
  `SFTTrainer` for that step.
- **Guarding old behaviour.** Replay, the standard mitigation, mixes a sample of earlier data into
  the fine-tuning set, and forgetting is checked on held-out old tasks. Reinforcement stages add a KL penalty that keeps outputs near a frozen
  reference model.
- **Next to prompting and retrieval.** Production systems often combine fine-tuning with those two
  routes rather than choosing one.

### Why it matters

Transfer lets a small task dataset go far. Shared structure gives **forward transfer**, a
better-than-chance score on the new corpus before any update, and that head start counts most
exactly where data is scarce.

The price is **catastrophic forgetting**: loss on the original data rising while loss on the new
data falls. A fine-tune scored only on the target sentences it trained on hides that damage. It
also hides **overfitting**, a fit to the trained-on examples that does not carry to new ones and
grows as the set shrinks. Only a separate score on the old data shows the first, and only target
sentences kept out of training show the second.

### What you will be able to explain

- Why pretrained weights beat a from-scratch start on a small corpus, and why the gap narrows as
  the budget grows.
- How to measure catastrophic forgetting rather than assume it.
- How to predict what freezing parameters does to adaptation and to forgetting.
- When fine-tuning is the right route, and when prompting or retrieval should come first.
- How replay protects old behaviour, and why a fine-tune is judged on target sentences it never
  trained on.

### What the lab shows

Every loss on the page is measured, not closed-form. The lab trains its own 900-weight bigram table
once, for a fixed 60 epochs on Harbor weather notes, then fine-tunes it on Recipe steps live. Both
corpora are fixed text, eight sentences each, and each run is a single seed, not an average over
seeds.

- **Transfer against training from scratch** pits the fine-tune against a run from a zeroed table
  on identical data, seed and budget.
- **Forgetting meter** scores that one model on both corpora at every checkpoint.
- **Fine-tuning controls** sets the **adaptation budget** with **Fine-tune epochs** and **Fine-tune
  learning rate**.
- **Layer freezing** stops updates to chosen blocks of weight rows. The model has no layers, so the
  blocks group the table by context character instead.
- **Replay and held-out target** runs its own fine-tune with **Replay share** and **Held-out target
  sentences**.
- **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb** is a hand-written rule table over four yes/no questions.
  Nothing on it is trained or measured.

The key experiment: drag **Fine-tune epochs** from 0 to 60, and the **Forgetting meter** shows
Recipe steps falling while Harbor climbs, both scored on the same weights.

## What it is

**Full fine-tuning** is ordinary training that starts somewhere other than zero. The base model
here is a 900-weight character bigram table, the same kind of model as in *Train one from
scratch*. It is not the model you trained there: this lab trains its own copy once, for a fixed 60
epochs on Harbor weather notes, and no control changes it.

Before anything else happens that base scores 1.921 nats per token on Harbor and 2.556 on Recipe
steps.

Fine-tuning continues the same minibatch SGD on softmax cross-entropy, but every batch now comes
from Recipe steps and the starting weights are the pretrained ones. **Fine-tune epochs** and
**Fine-tune learning rate** set the **adaptation budget**. Alongside it the lab trains a second
model on identical data, seed, and budget from a zeroed table — the dashed "started from zero"
curve. Nothing differs between the two runs except where they started.

A further card, **Replay and held-out target**, runs a fine-tune of its own: some Recipe steps
sentences are kept out of training, and some Harbor sentences are mixed back in.

### What the corpora share, and do not

Both are English at the character level, drawn from the same 30 symbols, with similar letter
frequencies and the same dominance of spaces, `e`, and `t`. The **domain shift** is stylistic:
Harbor weather notes describes, Recipe steps instructs. Shared character statistics are what
**transfer learning** exploits; the style difference is what still has to be learned. There is only
one weight table throughout, and the forgetting meter plots that single table scored on both
corpora at every checkpoint.

## Why it’s here

Fine-tuning is one route from a general model to a specific one: the one this lab measures, and
not the first one to try. Both halves of its trade become measurable here rather than assumed.

**Catastrophic forgetting** is the half that surprises people, and it is not mysterious decay.
The loss being minimized mentions only Recipe steps, so nothing in the objective has any reason
to preserve Harbor behaviour. The weights that encoded the source distribution are the same
weights being overwritten.

### Should you fine-tune?

Try the routes that are cheaper and easier to undo first.

- **Prompting first.** **Prompt engineering** changes the input to an unchanged model: clearer
  instructions, worked examples, the right context. A behaviour you can get from a few example lines
  is not worth a training run, and a prompt is fixed by editing text. *ICL & reasoning* shows why
  examples in a prompt work at all.
- **Retrieval for facts that change or need sources.** Weights are frozen when training stops, so a
  fact learned in training is stale the day it changes and points to nothing. Retrieval reads the
  current document when the question arrives and can show the passage it used. *Retrieval-augmented
  generation* is the lab.
- **Fine-tuning for behaviour.** Style, format, tone, or one narrow task done the same way every
  time are habits, and examples teach habits well. It can also pay for itself at scale: a small
  fine-tuned model can replace a long prompt or a larger model on a task that runs very often.

The cost side is what the rule's last question asks about. A fine-tune needs hundreds of good
examples that someone wrote or checked. It needs an **evaluation set** to show it worked and to
measure what **catastrophic forgetting** took, which is the meter in this lab. And it needs
maintenance, because a new base model means doing the work again.

## How to play with it

Start by setting **Fine-tune epochs** to 0. The transfer model already sits at 2.556 on Recipe
steps while the from-scratch model sits at 3.401 — a zeroed table gives every character equal
probability, so its loss is exactly `ln(30)`. That gap, before one gradient step on the target,
is **forward transfer**.

Return to the defaults, 20 epochs at learning rate 0.6. **Transfer, target loss** reads 2.123 and
**Scratch, target loss** reads 2.247, so the third metric reads **Transfer is lower by** 0.123.
**Source before** is 1.921, **Source after** is 2.065, and **Forgetting** is +0.144. **Target
gained** reports the same 2.556-to-2.123 drop from the other side.

Drag **Fine-tune epochs** to 60: Recipe steps falls to about 1.96 while Harbor climbs to about
2.22. Watch where the two target curves converge as you go.

Then, on **Layer freezing**, freeze two blocks, for instance `␣abcde` and `fghijk`. **Frozen rows**
reads 12 / 30 and **Trainable weights** drops to 540. At the same 20-epoch budget Recipe steps only
reaches 2.345 instead of 2.123, and Harbor holds at 1.990 instead of 2.065. The row strip under the
grid shows those twelve rows flat at zero.

Then treat epochs and learning rate as one quantity: halve **Fine-tune epochs**, double
**Fine-tune learning rate**, and compare **Forgetting** with what it was.

Next, on **Replay and held-out target**, leave 3 recipe sentences held out, **Replay share** at no
replay, and **Fine-tune epochs** at 20. **Trained-on target** reads 2.162 and **Held-out target**
reads 2.424, a gap of 0.262 nats: the fine-tune looks better on the sentences it saw than on the
three it never did. Raise **Fine-tune epochs** to 60 and the gap widens to 0.472, 1.927 against
2.399, while the Forgetting row climbs to +0.296.

Return to 20 epochs and raise **Replay share** to 50%. The Forgetting row for all original sentences
falls from +0.123 to +0.042, Trained-on target rises from 2.162 to 2.232, and Optimizer steps double
from 320 to 640. Now read the two split rows. The four replayed sentences end 0.038 nats better than
the base started, while the four not replayed forget by +0.129, as much as with no replay. At 100%
the overall row reads +0.001.

Last, on **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb**, leave all four answers on No: the route reads
Prompt first. Flip only the format question and it becomes Prompt with examples, then collect data.
Flip the examples question as well and it becomes Fine-tune. Now flip the changing-facts question
and retrieval joins the route. The rule table under the controls lists all five rows, and the first
match wins.

## What to notice

- The head start is largest early. At small budgets the two target curves sit far apart; as the
  budget grows the from-scratch run closes most of the distance. Transfer buys sample efficiency,
  not a better ceiling.
- **Forgetting** has no threshold. It grows checkpoint by checkpoint with the budget, from +0.144
  at 20 epochs to roughly +0.30 at 60. There is no setting where adaptation is free.
- Freezing works in both directions at once: 12 frozen rows cost 0.222 nats of adaptation (2.345
  against 2.123) and save 0.075 nats of forgetting (1.990 against 2.065). It is a dial between
  the two, not a fix.
- Frozen rows still have their gradients computed here. The trainer works them out and discards
  them, so **freezing** saves no compute in this lab. Frameworks usually skip that work, which is
  where real freezing saves memory.
- Blocks are not interchangeable. Freeze `xyz.<>` alone and almost nothing moves: `x`, `z`, and
  the two reserved brackets never occur in Recipe steps, so those rows receive no gradient either
  way. The row strip shows them at zero even when trainable, next to `j` and `q`. The block holding
  the space row is the opposite extreme.
- A fine-tune can look better than it is. The first cards score recipe sentences the model trained
  on, so their target loss is a training loss. Hold three out and the same fine-tune reads 2.424
  against 2.162, and the gap grows with the budget: 0.157 at 10 epochs, 0.262 at 20, 0.472 at 60.
  Trained-on loss keeps falling while held-out loss flattens near 2.40.
- Small data overfits. Hold four out and train 60 epochs: trained-on loss falls to 1.865 while
  held-out loss bottoms out at 2.541 and ends at 2.570. Only sentences kept out of training can
  show that, which is what makes **overfitting** measurable here.
- Replay protects what it replays and little else. At 50% the replayed sentences end 0.038 nats
  better than they started, against +0.119 for the same sentences with no replay. The sentences not
  replayed forget +0.129 with replay and +0.128 without.
- The overall Forgetting readout flatters replay, because the replayed sentences are part of what it
  scores. That is why the card splits it, and why a replay set has to represent the behaviour you
  want to keep.
- Replay costs fit and compute. Trained-on target rises from 2.162 to 2.232 at 50% and the run takes
  640 steps instead of 320. Held-out target barely moves, 2.424 to 2.417.
- Both samples use the same prompt and the same seed, so every difference between them comes from
  the weights. Neither is fluent.
- Read **Source after** and **Target gained** together. Either alone lets you tell a flattering
  story about the same run.
- On the decision card, having examples is not a reason to fine-tune. With the format question on
  No, flipping the examples question changes no route. The data answer only matters once a behaviour
  is needed, and then it separates prompting with examples from fine-tuning.
- Changing facts and citations give the same route, retrieval, whatever else is true. A fine-tune
  can neither show a source nor be updated without training again.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The pretraining, both fine-tuning runs, the freezing, and every score are
computed live. But apart from the replay card, nothing on this page is held out. The first cards
score Recipe steps in full, and it is also the entire fine-tuning set; Harbor is scored in full and
was the entire pretraining set.

For measuring forgetting that is the right comparison — you want to know whether the old behaviour
survived — but none of those numbers say anything about generalization.

The **Replay and held-out target** card holds out up to four of the eight recipe sentences, a few
hundred characters, so differences under about 0.05 nats there are unresolved and every run is one
seed. Its forgetting rows still score text the model pretrained on, including the replayed
sentences.

The **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb** card is the exception to "computed live". Its five rules
are written by hand, nothing on it is trained or measured, and it reduces each situation to four
yes/no answers. Real choices come in degrees and also weigh cost, latency, privacy and risk.

The **Layer freezing** grid is the second compromise. This model has no layers, so the grid groups
the weight table by which character a row predicts *from*, and the lab says so on screen. The
mechanism is the real one; the grouping is not. A real freeze grid names layers, embeddings, or
projection matrices.

The model is also tiny: one character of context, 900 weights, no attention, eight sentences per
corpus. **Replay** is the standard mitigation and the replay card gives it a control, but a bigram can
only memorize which characters follow which, so replaying a few sentences protects those sentences
and little else. Pretraining is fixed at 60 epochs on seed 1, and **Fine-tune learning rate** runs to 2.0,
far past anything you would use.

The starting model also differs between labs. *LoRA adapters* rebuilds this same 60-epoch, seed 1
Harbor table, but *Model merging* trains its own for 48 epochs, so its base reads 1.953 on Harbor and
2.553 on Recipe steps. Each lab fixes its own epoch count and none tests the difference, so compare
methods within a lab, not numbers between labs.

**In real systems.** Real replay mixes a sample of earlier data into the fine-tuning set, sized so
the old behaviour stays in the loss. Forgetting is checked on held-out old tasks rather than on the
replayed examples.

The routes are not exclusive, and production systems often combine them. The
cheapest route that clears your evaluation set is the right one. Fine-tuning can also teach facts,
but it is a weak way to add new knowledge, which is why the rule sends changing or cited facts to
retrieval.

Forgetting is not only a perplexity number. Instruction following, output
format, tool-call syntax, refusal behaviour, and calibration can all degrade while loss on a generic
corpus barely moves, so a single source-loss readout understates the damage.

Freezing is also not
free out there: freezing the *early* layers lets you stop the backward pass short and does save
compute, while freezing late layers still requires backpropagating through them to reach anything
below. Real domain shift usually changes vocabulary, task, and format together rather than style
alone, which makes both the head start and the forgetting larger than here.

## Going deeper

Every mitigation for forgetting works by putting the source back into the optimization. **Replay**
mixes original data into the fine-tuning batches, so the loss mentions it again.

The Replay share slider is that mechanism, and its split rows show the catch: the loss protects
only what it mentions. Weight-space
regularizers penalize distance from the pretrained weights, and elastic weight consolidation weights
that penalty by how much each parameter mattered before. Reinforcement stages add a KL penalty
against a frozen reference model, constraining the output distribution rather than the weights.

Freezing is the crudest member of the family: it sets the penalty to infinity for a chosen subset.

The shrinking head start has a simple reading. Pretrained weights are a prior and target data is
evidence. With little evidence the prior dominates and transfer looks decisive; with enough evidence
any reasonable starting point converges to a similar place. That is why transfer matters most exactly
where data is scarce, and why gaps between initialization schemes narrow as datasets grow.

In practice freezing is rarely all-or-nothing. Gradual unfreezing releases layers from the top down,
discriminative learning rates give lower layers smaller steps instead of zero, and embeddings are
often frozen because they are large and generic. The cheapest defense remains stopping early, since
**Forgetting** tracks the budget.

Choosing between the routes is an evaluation problem before it is a training problem. Prompt first and
score it on held-out cases. Add retrieval when the failures are missing or stale facts. Fine-tune when
the failures are a consistent format or behaviour gap that examples would close. *Evaluation* is where
the score you compare them with comes from.

One cost this lab does not charge you: full fine-tuning produces an entirely new 900-weight table per
task, so serving ten specializations means storing and loading ten complete copies. That is the
problem the next lab attacks.

### Toy versus real

- **Rates are not comparable.** Llama 2's supervised fine-tune used a cosine schedule from an
  initial 2 × 10⁻⁵, with weight decay 0.1, batch 64, and 2 epochs, against 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ at
  pretraining for the 7B model. The QLoRA paper's LoRA fine-tunes used 2 × 10⁻⁴ for 7B and 13B
  models and 1 × 10⁻⁴ for 33B and 65B. This lab's plain-SGD slider, 0.05 to 2, is in other units.
- **Data size.** Llama 2-Chat's supervised stage stopped after 27,540 annotations, which its paper
  describes as tens of thousands being enough for high quality. This lab fine-tunes on eight
  sentences.
- **Tools.** Hugging Face TRL provides trainers for supervised fine-tuning (`SFTTrainer`), DPO and
  GRPO, and its documentation lists PEFT among its integrations, so the same loop can train the LoRA
  adapters of the next lab.
- **Checks.** Real fine-tunes score held-out sets for the new task and for old behaviours. Here only
  the replay card holds anything out.
