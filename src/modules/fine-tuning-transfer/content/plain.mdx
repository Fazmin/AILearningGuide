## The big picture

Imagine a small program that has learned to guess the next letter of weather reports, and now you
want it to guess the next letter of cooking instructions instead. Training a program like this means
letting it practise guessing on a text and adjusting its settings a little after each guess, so its
later guesses improve. You could throw it away and train a new one from a blank start, or you could
keep it and carry on training it on the recipes. This module is about the second choice, which is
called fine-tuning, and about what it gains you and what it quietly costs.

### Where you have met it

Almost no AI model in real use is trained from scratch for its exact job. Instead, people start from
a general model that has already learned from a lot of text, and then they adapt it to the job. For
example, one step in building the chat model Llama 2-Chat was fine-tuning the general Llama 2 model
on 27,540 examples that people had prepared.

Fine-tuning is not the only way to adapt a model, and it is often not the first one to try. If you
have ever added an example to show a chatbot the format you wanted, you have used the cheapest
route. It changes the message you send, called the prompt, not the model itself.

Another route lets a system look up documents when you ask a question, so the answer can use the
current text and show you the passage it came from.

### Why it matters

If you could not reuse a trained model, every new job would need a model trained from nothing. That
would throw away everything an existing model has already picked up, and it hurts most when you have
only a little text for the new job. In this lab, a model started from zero begins as a blind
guesser, while the model that already trained on weather notes starts well ahead.

The catch is that a model like this is really one big table of numbers, and it has only one.
Training it on recipes changes the same numbers that handled weather notes, so it can get worse at
its old job while it gets better at the new one.

If nobody measures that damage, a team can end up with a model that is better at one thing and
quietly broken at another. A fine-tuned model can also look better than it really is when you only
test it on the sentences it practised on.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- why a model that has already learned from one kind of text beats a blank one when the new text is
  small;
- how to measure forgetting as a number, instead of assuming that it happens or that it does not;
- what freezing part of the model does, both to how well it learns the new text and to how much of
  the old text it loses;
- when fine-tuning is the right tool, and when a better prompt or a document lookup should come
  first;
- how mixing old text back into training protects old behaviour, and why you should judge a
  fine-tune on new sentences it never trained on.

### What the lab lets you do

Everything in this lab, apart from the last card, is real training that runs live as you move the
controls, so every number you see was measured, not made up. Six cards each show one part of the
story:

- **Transfer against training from scratch** races the model that already trained on weather notes
  against one that starts from zero, on the same recipe text.
- **Forgetting meter** measures that one fine-tuned model on both kinds of text at once, so you can
  watch it get better at recipes while it gets worse at weather notes.
- **Fine-tuning controls** holds the two sliders that decide how hard the training pushes, plus
  three readouts that sum up the result.
- **Layer freezing** lets you lock parts of the model so they cannot change, and shows which parts
  moved.
- **Replay and held-out target** mixes some old text back in and keeps some new sentences out, so
  you can test the model fairly.
- **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb** asks four yes-or-no questions and suggests a route, and it
  is a hand-written guide, not a measurement.

## What it is

### The model you start from

In *Train one from scratch* you met a tiny model that reads one character and guesses the next one.
It works with 30 symbols: the 26 letters, the space, the dot that ends a sentence, and two bracket
signs, `<` and `>`, that never appear in the texts here.

All this model holds is a table of 900 numbers, called **weights**. Picture the table as a notebook
with 30 pages, one page for each symbol, where each page lists 30 scores, one for every symbol that
might come next. When the model reads `t`, it opens page `t` and converts those scores into odds for
the next character. Because `h` so often follows `t`, page `t` gives `h` a high score.

This lab trains its own model of that kind once, on a text called Harbor weather notes. It makes a
fixed 60 full passes through that text, and each pass is called an **epoch**, so the model reads all
eight Harbor sentences 60 times over. They sound like this one: “the morning fog settles over the
harbor and the air feels cool.”

This first round of training is called pretraining, and its result is the base model that every
fine-tune on this page starts from. It is not the copy you trained in the earlier lab, and no
control on this page changes it.

### How the lab scores a model

To judge a model, the lab runs it along a whole text and checks, at every character, what odds it
gave to the character that really came next. High odds for the right character earn a small penalty,
while low odds earn a big one. The average penalty over the whole text is called the **loss**, so a
lower loss means better guessing.

The loss is measured in units called **nats** per character, and you do not need the math behind the
unit, because two landmarks tell you how to read it. A loss of 0 would mean the model gave the right
character 100% odds every single time, which no real model manages. A loss of 3.401 is what you get
by giving all 30 symbols the same 1-in-30 chance, which is blind guessing.

Any loss in between has an everyday meaning too: a loss of 2.556 means the model is, on average,
about as unsure as someone choosing among 13 equally likely characters. A loss of 1.921 is more like
choosing among 7. Before any fine-tuning, this lab’s base model has a loss of 1.921 nats per
character on Harbor. On Recipe steps, the new text you want it to handle, its loss is 2.556.

Most charts label the unit nats/char, but the first card says nats/token. For this model a token is
one character, so the two labels mean the same thing.

### Fine-tuning: carry on training on new text

**Full fine-tuning** is the same kind of training that built the Harbor model, except that it starts
from the Harbor model’s weights instead of a blank table. You keep those 900 weights and carry on
training. This time, every batch of practice comes from Recipe steps, with sentences such as “warm
the pan over medium heat and add a spoon of oil.” People often call one run of this training, and
the model it produces, a fine-tune.

The lab calls Harbor weather notes the source, since it is where the model came from. It calls
Recipe steps the target, since that is where you want the model to go. That is why the readouts have
names like **Source before** and **Target gained**.

The training method itself stays the same as before. The trainer takes a small batch of character
pairs from the text and works out which way each weight should move to give the real next characters
higher odds. That direction is called the **gradient**. The trainer then nudges the weights a little
in that direction, but only on the pages the model opened to make that batch’s guesses. That round
of nudges counts as one step.

For example, say the batch holds a `t` followed by an `h`. The trainer then raises the score for `h`
on page `t` a little and lowers the other scores on that page. Step by step, this pushes the loss on
the practice text down. A page changes only when its own character turns up in the practice text, so
a letter that never appears there leaves its page untouched.

The part of the trainer that makes these nudges is called the optimizer, so the lab counts them as
optimizer steps. The word “full” means that all 900 weights are free to move.

Two sliders on the **Fine-tuning controls** card decide how hard you push. **Fine-tune epochs** sets
how many passes the model makes through the recipe text, from 0 up to 60. **Fine-tune learning
rate** sets how big each nudge is, from 0.05 up to 2.00. Together they make the **adaptation
budget**, how much change you allow, which works out roughly as the number of epochs times the
learning rate.

Beside the fine-tuned model, the lab trains a second model on the same recipe text, with the same
budget and the same random seed. A seed is a number that fixes every random choice in a run, such as
the order the practice batches are shuffled into, so both runs make the same choices.

The second model starts from a table of zeros, which gives every symbol the same odds, and it
appears as the dashed “started from zero” line. Until you freeze anything, the only difference
between the two runs is where they began. The **Layer freezing** card, which comes later, locks
parts of the fine-tuned model only, while the model started from zero always trains all 900 weights.

The lab calls the fine-tuned model the transfer run and the one started from zero the scratch run,
from the everyday phrase “from scratch”, which means from nothing. That is where the readouts
**Transfer, target loss** and **Scratch, target loss** get their names.

### What the two texts share, and where they differ

Harbor weather notes and Recipe steps are both written in English with the same 30 symbols. A
collection of training text like each of these is called a **corpus**, and two or more are called
corpora.

The two corpora also share habits. In both of them, about one character in five is a space, `e` and
`t` are the most common letters, and `h` is the letter that most often follows `t`. Weights shaped
by Harbor already expect those habits, which is why they are any use for recipes at all.

What differs is the style. Harbor weather notes describes what the weather does, while Recipe steps
gives orders such as warm, stir, add, pour and simmer. The gap between the old text and the new one
is called the **domain shift**, and here it is a shift in style, not in the alphabet.

Reusing weights trained on one kind of text as the starting point for another is called **transfer
learning**, and it pays off because of what the two texts share.

Here is a made-up picture of why that head start matters. Picture two friends playing a game where
they guess the next letter of a hidden recipe, one letter at a time.

One friend has spent weeks playing the same game on weather notes, while the other has never seen
English writing and has to treat all 30 symbols as equally likely. Neither has seen a recipe before,
yet the weather player already expects `h` after `t` and plenty of spaces, so they start far ahead.
In this lab, the weather player is the pretrained model and the newcomer is the one started from
zero.

### One notebook, written in pencil

Keep the notebook in mind for the rest of the lesson, because it is the main picture here. The
weight table is one notebook written in pencil, with a page for each symbol. Learning Recipe steps
means erasing and rewriting entries in that same notebook, with no second copy and no blank section
set aside for recipes.

Take page `o` as an example, since the two texts disagree about it. In Harbor weather notes, the
letter after `o` is most often `r`, as in harbor, morning and storm. In Recipe steps it is most
often `n`, as in onions and one. Fine-tuning pencils the score for `n` up on page `o` and the score
for `r` down. After the default run, the odds for `n` on page `o` rise from about 7% to 24%. The
odds for `r` fall from about 35% to 12%, so harbor, morning and storm become more surprising to the
model.

**Freezing** is taping over some pages so nothing can be written on them. A taped page keeps its
Harbor scores, which protects what Harbor needs from that page, but it also means the recipe
training can never improve it. In large models, freezing works on processing stages called layers.
This tiny model has none, so the **Layer freezing** card tapes over groups of six pages instead.

**Replay** is mixing a few old Harbor sentences into the recipe practice text, so that the pencil
edits also have to keep those sentences well predicted.

The picture stops matching in a few places. The entries are not facts you could read, like “the
harbor is foggy”. Each one is a score for one pair of characters, and it is used every time the
page’s character turns up in any word.

Nothing is erased in a single stroke, since the scores drift a little at every step and the old text
gets worse as a side effect. And in large real models, the weights do not line up with letters at
all.

### The extra card

A further card, **Replay and held-out target**, runs a fine-tune of its own with two changes. Some
Recipe steps sentences are kept out of training, or held out, so you can test the model on sentences
it never practised on. Some Harbor sentences are mixed back in, which is replay.

## Why it’s here

### A small text needs a head start

A model trained from zero has to learn even the most basic habits of the language from the new text
alone. When that text is small, like the eight recipe sentences here, much of the training goes into
habits any English text could have taught, such as where spaces go. Transfer gives the model most of
those habits for free, because the pretrained weights already carry them, so more of the training
budget goes on what is actually new.

That is the first problem fine-tuning solves: getting a useful model for a new job out of a small
amount of new text. Fine-tuning is one way to turn a general model into a specialist, and it is the
way this lab measures. As the help-desk story below shows, though, it is usually not the first route
to try.

### The cost: catastrophic forgetting

The other side of the bargain is the part that surprises people. As the model gets better at
recipes, its loss on Harbor weather notes climbs, and this is called **catastrophic forgetting**.
Despite the word “forgetting”, it is not wear or decay, and the model does not fade the way an
unpractised memory might.

The real reason is more direct. The loss that training lowers during fine-tuning is calculated on
Recipe steps alone, so nothing in it gives the trainer any reason to protect Harbor. Every step
moves the shared weights toward recipes, and those same weights were what made Harbor work, so
adapting the weights and overwriting them are one and the same act.

### Should you fine-tune?

Since fine-tuning has real costs, it is worth asking whether you need it at all. Here is a made-up
workplace story that maps onto the three routes you can take. Picture a new hire who answers
customers at a help desk, and think of three ways to help them do it well.

You could leave a clear note on their desk, which is quick to write and quick to take away. You
could point them to the current handbook, which keeps their facts fresh and lets them show the
customer the page. Or you could send them on a training course, which changes how they work every
day. A course takes time, needs good material, and can overwrite habits they already had.

Those are the three routes, and the cheaper ones come first.

- **Prompting first** is the note, quick to write and quick to undo. **Prompt engineering** means
  getting the behaviour you want from an unchanged model by changing what you send it: clearer
  instructions, worked examples, or the right background. A behaviour you can get from a few example
  lines is not worth a training run. When a prompt goes wrong, you repair it by editing the text,
  with no training at all. A model picking up a pattern from examples inside the prompt is called
  in-context learning, or ICL, and the *ICL & reasoning* lab shows why that works at all.
- **Retrieval** is the handbook, which stays current and can be shown. A model’s weights stop
  changing when training stops, so a fact learned in training goes stale the day it changes. The
  model also cannot point to where that fact came from. Retrieval looks up the current document when
  the question arrives and can show the passage it used, and *Retrieval-augmented generation* is the
  lab for it.
- **Fine-tuning** is the course, which changes the habits themselves. Style, format, tone, or one
  narrow task done the same way every time are habits, and examples teach habits well. It can also
  save money on a task that runs thousands of times a day. A long prompt has to be processed again
  with every request. A small fine-tuned model can do the same job with a short prompt instead, or
  replace a larger, costlier model.

The picture stops matching in one important way. A person who took a course can still look things up
on their own, but a model cannot look anything up unless you build retrieval around it.

The cost side is what the last question on the **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb** card asks
about: a fine-tune needs hundreds of good examples that someone wrote or checked. It also needs an
**evaluation set**, which is a collection of examples kept out of training and used only for
judging. It covers both the new task and the old behaviour you must not lose.

That set shows whether the fine-tune worked and measures what catastrophic forgetting took, which is
the same kind of measurement the meter in this lab makes. Finally, a fine-tune needs upkeep. When a
newer, better base model comes out, your fine-tune does not carry over to it, so you have to
fine-tune the new one all over again.

## How to play with it

### Step 1: the head start before any training

First set **Fine-tune epochs** to 0, which the slider shows as “no adaptation”. **Transfer, target
loss** reads 2.556 on Recipe steps, while **Scratch, target loss** reads 3.401, the blind-guess loss
of a table full of zeros. That number is exact: it is `ln(30)`, which is what the ln key on a
scientific calculator gives you for 30.

The third readout, **Transfer is lower by**, shows a gap of 0.845 nats before a single step of
recipe training, and that head start is called **forward transfer**. The pretrained model already
beats chance on the new text, because the two texts share structure. In everyday terms, the
pretrained model is about as unsure as someone choosing among 13 characters, while the blank one is
choosing among all 30.

### Step 2: the default run

Now return to the defaults, 20 epochs at learning rate 0.6, and the badge on the first card shows
that each run took 500 steps. **Transfer, target loss** reads 2.123 and **Scratch, target loss**
reads 2.247, so the third readout says **Transfer is lower by** 0.123. The lab subtracts the
unrounded losses, which is why it shows 0.123 rather than the 0.124 you get from the rounded
readouts. Both models improved a lot, but the one that started from Harbor is still ahead.

On **Fine-tuning controls**, **Source before** reads 1.921, the base model’s loss on Harbor, and
**Source after** reads 2.065, the fine-tuned model’s loss on that same text. The **Forgetting**
readout on the **Layer freezing** card is the difference between them, 2.065 − 1.921 = +0.144. In
everyday terms, the model slipped from choosing among about 7 characters on Harbor to about 8.

**Target gained** reads 0.433, which is how far the recipe loss fell, from 2.556 to 2.123, shown as
a gain. Read it next to **Source after**, because each one alone tells only half the story of the
run.

**Try this:** drag **Fine-tune epochs** slowly from 0 up to 60 and watch the two lines on the
**Forgetting meter** move toward each other and then cross. Both lines start from the base model, so
the solid Recipe steps line begins higher, at 2.556, and the dashed Harbor line lower, at 1.921.

At about 25 epochs the two lines meet at the right-hand end of the chart. By 60 epochs the Recipe
steps line has fallen to about 1.96 while the Harbor line has climbed to about 2.22. The model now
finds weather notes harder than recipes, the reverse of where it began. Every epoch that helps
recipes hurts Harbor a little more, and the badge on that card counts the damage.

### Step 3: freeze some pages

Set the epochs back to 20, go to **Layer freezing**, and click two blocks, `␣abcde` and `fghijk`, so
their labels change from trainable to frozen. The `␣` mark stands for the space. Each block covers
six pages of the notebook, which the lab calls rows. So **Frozen rows** reads 12 / 30, and
**Trainable weights** drops from 900 to 540, since 18 pages of 30 scores are left.

At the same 20-epoch budget, **Transfer, target loss** only gets down to 2.345 instead of 2.123,
while **Source after** holds at 1.990 instead of 2.065. On the first card, the third readout even
flips to **Transfer is higher by**. The model started from zero ignores the freeze and still trains
every page, so it still reads 2.247.

That flip does not mean pretraining hurt. It means the twelve locked pages, which include busy ones
such as space, `a` and `e`, cost more recipe learning than the head start was worth.

Under the grid sits the row strip, with one bar per page showing how far that page’s scores moved.
The twelve frozen pages are hatched, which means shaded with diagonal stripes, and they sit at zero.
The pages you left trainable show how hard the recipe training pulled on them.

### Step 4: spend the budget a different way

Now test the idea that the two sliders share one budget: unfreeze both blocks, then halve
**Fine-tune epochs** to 10 and double **Fine-tune learning rate** to 1.20. Since 10 × 1.2 and
20 × 0.6 both make 12, the budget is about the same, and **Forgetting** lands back near +0.144,
within a hundredth of a nat. Fewer, bigger nudges did roughly the same work as more, smaller ones.

### Step 5: test on sentences it never saw

Set the sliders back to 20 epochs and 0.60, then go to **Replay and held-out target** and leave
**Held-out target sentences** at 3 and **Replay share** at no replay. This fine-tune trains on the
first five recipe sentences and never sees the last three, such as “boil the pasta in salted water
until it is tender.”

Its table shows **Trained-on target** at 2.162 and **Held-out target** at 2.424, a gap of 0.262
nats. The fine-tune looks better on the sentences it saw than on the three it never did, even though
all eight come from the same recipe text. This card’s badge reads 320 steps instead of 500, because
with three sentences left out, each pass has fewer character pairs to work through.

Raise **Fine-tune epochs** to 60 and the gap widens to 0.472, with 1.927 against 2.399. Meanwhile
the **Forgetting, all original sentences** row climbs to +0.296.

### Step 6: mix old sentences back in

Go back to 20 epochs and raise **Replay share** to 50%, which mixes the first four Harbor sentences
into the training text. A second column, **With 50% replay**, appears beside **No replay**, so you
can compare the two fine-tunes row by row. In each item below, the first number comes from the No
replay column and the second from the replay column.

In the No replay column, forgetting reads +0.123, less than the +0.144 on the first cards. That is
because training on five recipe sentences instead of eight takes only 320 steps instead of 500, so
the weights get fewer nudges away from Harbor.

- **Forgetting, all original sentences** falls from +0.123 to +0.042, so far less of Harbor is lost
  overall.
- **Trained-on target** rises from 2.162 to 2.232, which means the model fits recipes a little less
  closely, because the same pages are also being pulled toward Harbor. More training does not close
  that gap: at 60 epochs the replay run still reads 2.052 against 1.927 without replay.
- **Optimizer steps** doubles from 320 to 640, because every pass now also works through the four
  Harbor sentences. Those happen to hold about as many characters as the five recipe sentences.
- **Forgetting, the replayed sentences** goes from +0.119 to −0.038, a swing of about 0.16 nats.
  With replay, those four sentences end about where the base model started, or slightly better,
  because the fine-tune gave them 20 more passes of practice.
- **Forgetting, the sentences not replayed** goes from +0.128 to +0.129, which means replay did
  nothing for them.

Push **Replay share** to 100% and the overall Forgetting row reads +0.001, almost no forgetting,
because now every Harbor sentence is part of the training text.

### Step 7: the decision card

Last, on **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb**, leave all four answers on No, and the
**Recommended route** reads Prompt first. Flip only “Do you need a consistent format, style or
behaviour?” to Yes and it becomes Prompt with examples, then collect data. Flip “Do you have
hundreds of good examples and an evaluation set?” to Yes as well, and it becomes Fine-tune.

Now flip “Does the knowledge the answers depend on change often?” to Yes, and retrieval joins the
route as Retrieval for the facts, fine-tuning for the style. The rule table under the controls lists
all five rows in order, the first row that matches wins, and the winner is marked (matched).

## What to notice

### On the first four cards

- The head start is biggest early: at small budgets the two target curves sit far apart, and the
  longer both run, the more the started-from-zero model catches up. Pretraining mostly buys speed,
  not a better final result.
- **Forgetting** has no safe zone where recipes improve for free. The charts plot the model at saved
  snapshots along the run, called checkpoints, and at the defaults the Harbor loss rises at every
  one of them. It goes from +0.144 at 20 epochs to roughly +0.30 at 60, and only the very smallest
  budgets, which barely help recipes either, keep it near zero.
- Freezing moves both numbers at once: those 12 frozen rows cost 0.222 nats of recipe learning and
  save 0.075 nats of forgetting. That makes freezing a trade between the two, not a fix.
- In this lab, frozen rows still have their gradients worked out and then thrown away, so freezing
  here saves no computing work. Real training tools usually skip working out those changes, which
  saves memory, but as the section on real systems explains, it saves computing work only in some
  cases.
- The row strip shows which rows really moved: frozen rows sit at zero, and so do `j`, `q`, `x`,
  `z`, `<` and `>`, even when they are trainable. None of them appears in Recipe steps at all, and
  the trainer only edits a page when it reads that page’s character, so their pages never get a
  gradient.
- Blocks are not equal, since freezing `xyz.<>` alone changes almost nothing: `x`, `z` and the two
  brackets never appear in Recipe steps, so those rows get no gradient either way. The other two,
  `y` and `.`, are usually followed by a space in both texts, so their Harbor scores already
  suit recipes.
- The first block, `␣abcde`, is the opposite extreme: freezing it on its own costs 0.146 nats of
  recipe learning, more than any other block. It holds several pages that recipes both use a lot and
  need to change, such as `a`, space and `e`.
- How often a page is used is only half of it, because what matters is how far recipes need that
  page to move. Space is the most common character in the recipe text, yet freezing page `a` alone
  costs more than freezing the space page alone.
- The two lines on the **Forgetting meter** do not come from two models. They are one model scored
  on two corpora at every checkpoint, so when the recipe line falls and the Harbor line rises, both
  changes come from the same moving weights.
- The **Forgetting meter** also shows two samples, which are short pieces of text the model writes
  by itself, picking each next character at random, with likelier characters picked more often. They
  are labelled **Before adaptation** and **After adaptation**. Both start from the same starting
  text, or prompt, `the `, with the same random seed, so every difference between them comes from
  the weights.
- At the defaults, the before sample contains Harbor fragments such as `arbo`, while the after
  sample has picked up fragments such as `pan`. Neither one is fluent, because a model that sees one
  character at a time can only learn which letter tends to follow which.
- Read **Source after** next to **Target gained**, because if you pick only one of them, you can
  tell a flattering story about the very same run.

### On the replay card

A fine-tune can look better than it really is. The first cards score the very recipe sentences the
model trained on, so their target loss is really a training loss. Hold three out and the same kind
of fine-tune reads 2.424 on the new sentences against 2.162 on the practised ones.

That gap grows with the budget:

- at 10 epochs, it is 0.157;
- at 20 epochs, it is 0.262;
- at 60 epochs, it is 0.472.

Trained-on loss keeps falling, while held-out loss flattens out near 2.40, because extra training
mostly helps with the exact sentences the model keeps seeing.

Here is a made-up story that shows the same thing. Picture a classmate who prepares for a quiz by
drilling the same five practice questions over and over, until those five feel much easier. They do
noticeably better on those five, but on three new questions of the same kind they do only a little
better than before. Only the new questions reveal how much they really learned.

The picture stops matching in one place. The model never aces anything, and it cannot memorize whole
sentences. After 20 epochs it is still about as unsure on its five practice sentences as someone
choosing among 9 characters. What it over-fits is how often each letter follows each other letter in
those five.

Fitting the practised examples much more closely than new examples of the same kind is called
**overfitting**, and small training sets make it worse. Set **Held-out target sentences** to 4 and
**Fine-tune epochs** to 30, and **Held-out target** reads 2.541, about its lowest point. Raise the
epochs to 60 and it climbs back to 2.570, while **Trained-on target** keeps falling to 1.865. Only
sentences kept out of training can show that rise, which is what makes overfitting measurable here.

- Replay protects what it replays and little else: at 50% the replayed sentences end 0.038 nats
  better than they started, while the same sentences with no replay forget +0.119.
- The sentences that were not replayed get no protection at all, since they forget +0.129 with
  replay and +0.128 without it.
- The overall Forgetting number flatters replay, because the replayed sentences are part of what it
  scores. That is why the card splits it into two rows, and why a replay set has to represent the
  behaviour you want to keep.
- Replay costs both fit and computing work, since Trained-on target rises from 2.162 to 2.232 at 50%
  and the run takes 640 steps instead of 320. Held-out target barely moves, from 2.424 to 2.417.

### On the decision card

- Having examples is not a reason to fine-tune by itself. With the format question on No, flipping
  the examples question changes no route. The data answer only matters once a consistent behaviour
  is needed, and then it decides whether fine-tuning joins the route. Without retrieval, it turns
  “Prompt with examples, then collect data” into “Fine-tune”. With retrieval, it turns “Retrieval”
  into “Retrieval for the facts, fine-tuning for the style”.
- A Yes to changing facts, a Yes to citations, or a Yes to both gives the same answer: retrieval is
  part of the route, whatever the other two answers say. A fine-tune can neither show a source nor
  be updated without training it again.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The pretraining, both fine-tuning runs, the freezing and every loss are computed live, but apart
from the replay card, nothing on this page is held out. The first cards score Recipe steps in full,
which is also the whole fine-tuning set, and they score Harbor in full, which was the whole
pretraining set.

For measuring forgetting, that is the right comparison, because you want to know whether the old
behaviour survived. But none of those numbers tell you how the model does on text it has never seen.

Every run on this page also uses a single seed, not an average over several. So you never see how
much a different shuffle of the practice batches would move the numbers, including gaps such as the
0.123 head start at the defaults.

The **Replay and held-out target** card holds out at most four of the eight recipe sentences, which
is only a few hundred characters. Because these sets are so small, a difference under about 0.05
nats there could come from luck rather than from the setting you changed. The luck could be as
simple as the order the batches happened to be shuffled in. Its forgetting rows still score text the
model pretrained on, including the replayed sentences, and the replayed sentences are always the
first ones of Harbor, not a random pick.

The **Should you fine-tune? Rule of thumb** card is the one exception to “computed live”. Its five
rules were written by hand, nothing on it was trained or measured, and it squeezes each situation
into four yes-or-no answers. Real choices come in degrees, such as how often facts change or how
many examples you have. They also weigh cost, privacy, risk, and latency, which is how long each
answer takes to arrive.

The **Layer freezing** grid is the other compromise. Real models are built as a stack of processing
stages called layers, but this model has none. So the grid groups the rows of the weight table, the
notebook’s pages, into blocks of six symbols, and the lab says so on screen. The freezing itself is
real, while the grouping by letters is a stand-in, since a real freeze grid would name layers or the
large weight tables inside them.

The model is also tiny: it sees one character of context, meaning it looks only at the single
character before its guess. It holds 900 weights, trains on eight sentences per corpus, and has none
of the attention machinery that large models use to look back over earlier text.

**Replay** is the standard fix for forgetting, and the replay card gives it a control. In large
models, replaying a well-chosen sample of old text is meant to protect the old behaviour in general,
not only the replayed examples.

A model this small can only memorize which characters follow which, so replaying a few sentences
protects those sentences and little else. Replaying four Harbor sentences pulls the shared pages
toward those sentences’ own letter pairs. That leaves the replayed four slightly better fitted than
before. The other four get no help from that pull, so they forget as much as with no replay at all.

A few settings are fixed or stretched on purpose. Pretraining is locked at 60 epochs, and
**Fine-tune learning rate** goes up to 2.0, far past anything anyone would use. The “started from
zero” model is a table of exact zeros, while a real model trained from scratch starts from small
random numbers instead.

The starting model also differs between labs. *LoRA & adapters* rebuilds this same 60-epoch Harbor
table, but *Model merging* trains its own for 48 epochs, so its base reads 1.953 on Harbor and 2.553
on Recipe steps. Each lab fixes its own epoch count and none of them tests the difference, so
compare methods within one lab, not numbers between labs.

**A common mix-up:** the model did not forget Harbor because the Harbor text was deleted, or because
the model filled up and ran out of room. Nothing was deleted, and there are still exactly 900
weights: with 100% replay, those same weights keep Harbor almost intact, at +0.001. The only thing
that changed is which text the gradient steps came from, and Harbor was never in that text.

### In real systems

Real replay mixes a sample of earlier data into the fine-tuning set, sized so that the old behaviour
stays in the loss. Forgetting is then checked on old tasks that were held out, not on the replayed
examples themselves.

The three routes are not exclusive, and real systems often combine them, but the cheapest route that
passes your evaluation set is the right one. Fine-tuning can also teach facts, but it is a weak way
to add new knowledge. It also cannot keep those facts current or point to where they came from,
which is why the rule sends changing or cited facts to retrieval.

Forgetting also shows up in ways a single loss number misses. A model can get worse at following
instructions or at formatting its output. It can get worse at writing the exact commands it sends to
other programs, such as a calculator or a search tool, or at refusing harmful requests. It can also
lose calibration, which is how well its confidence matches how often it is right.

All of that can fall apart while its loss on general text barely moves, so a single readout like
**Source after** can understate the damage.

In real systems, freezing saves less computing work than you might hope. Picture a real model as an
assembly line of stations, called layers. Text goes in at the first station, each station reworks
the numbers and passes them on, and the guess comes out at the last one.

To work out how to change each station, training starts from the error at the end and traces it
back, station by station, toward the start. Tools skip working out changes for the frozen stations
themselves, which saves memory. But a frozen late station still has to pass the trace back to any
trainable station nearer the start, so freezing only the late layers does not shorten that trace.

Freezing the early layers is different. If every station from some point back to the start is
frozen, the trace can stop before it reaches them, which saves real computing work.

Real domain shift usually changes vocabulary, task and format all at once, not only the style. That
usually makes the forgetting bigger than it is here, and how much head start is left depends on how
much structure the two kinds of text still share. When they share too little, starting from the old
model can even make things worse, which is called negative transfer.

## Going deeper

### Most defenses put the old data back

Every defense that changes the training itself works in the same basic way. It puts the old data, or
the old model, back into the loss that training is trying to lower. The old data goes back in as
text to practise on, and the old model as a penalty for drifting away from it. **Replay** mixes
original text into the fine-tuning batches, so the loss is calculated on it again. The **Replay
share** slider is that mechanism, and its split rows show the catch: the loss protects only the text
it is calculated on.

Other methods add a penalty for moving the weights far from where they started, and some make that
penalty bigger for the weights that mattered most to the old text. Some later training stages score
the model’s answers and reward the good ones. They also add a penalty when the model’s odds drift
too far from those of an untouched copy of the starting model. Freezing is the bluntest member of
this family, because it acts like a penalty so large that the rows you picked cannot move at all.

### Why the head start shrinks

In this lab, the head start shrinks because both runs keep fitting the same eight recipe sentences.
Given enough passes, almost any start ends up close to the same best fit for those sentences, so the
gap closes. That is more practice on the same text, not new evidence.

The same idea applies to data in general. Think of the pretrained weights as a starting guess and
the target text as evidence. With little evidence, the starting guess dominates, so transfer looks
decisive, but with plenty of evidence almost any sensible start ends up in a similar place. That is
why transfer matters most where data is scarce.

### How real practice freezes

Real practice rarely freezes everything or nothing. Layers can be unfrozen one at a time as training
goes on, starting from the output end, or given smaller steps instead of zero steps. The large
lookup table at the very start of a model turns each piece of text into numbers, and it is called
the embedding table. It is often frozen, because it is huge and general. Still, the cheapest defense
is to stop early, because **Forgetting** grows with the budget.

### Choosing a route is about testing

Choosing between the routes is a testing problem before it is a training problem. Prompt first and
score the results on held-out cases. Add retrieval when the failures are missing or stale facts.
Fine-tune when the model keeps getting the format or behaviour wrong in a way a set of examples
could fix. The *Evaluation* lab shows how to build the score you compare the routes with.

### A full copy for every job

One cost this lab does not charge you for is storage, because full fine-tuning leaves you a whole
new 900-weight table for every task. Ten specialized models means ten complete copies to store and
load, which is the problem the next lab, *LoRA & adapters*, goes after.

### Toy versus real

- **Learning rates are not comparable.** Llama 2’s fine-tune started its learning rate at 2 × 10⁻⁵,
  which is 0.00002, and lowered it gradually over 2 epochs, with a batch size of 64. Pretraining its
  7B model, the version with about 7 billion weights, used 3.0 × 10⁻⁴, which is 0.0003.
- **Other fine-tunes use other rates.** The QLoRA paper’s LoRA fine-tunes, the kind the next lab
  teaches, used 2 × 10⁻⁴, or 0.0002, for models of 7B and 13B weights. For the larger 33B and 65B
  models they used half that, 1 × 10⁻⁴. This lab’s slider runs from 0.05 to 2 with a simpler
  training method, so its numbers are in different units.
- **Data size:** the fine-tuning stage of Llama 2-Chat stopped after 27,540 examples prepared by
  people, and its paper describes tens of thousands as enough for high quality. This lab fine-tunes
  on eight sentences.
- **Tools:** TRL, a collection of ready-made training code from the AI company Hugging Face,
  includes a trainer for fine-tuning called `SFTTrainer`. It also has trainers for two other
  methods, DPO and GRPO, that train a model on feedback about which answers are better. Its
  documentation lists PEFT, a toolkit for fine-tuning only a small part of a model, among the tools
  it works with. That means the same training code can train the LoRA adapters of the next lab.
- **Checks:** real fine-tunes score held-out sets for the new task and for the old behaviours, while
  here only the replay card holds anything out.
