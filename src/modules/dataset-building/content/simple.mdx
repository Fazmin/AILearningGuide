## The big picture

Copy all the text from almost any web page and you get more than the article you wanted. You also get the menu, a newsletter sign-up box, a row of share buttons and a copyright line. A program that collects web pages automatically picks up all of that. It often collects the
same page, or a copy of it, more than once.

Models like the ones behind chatbots learn from huge piles of text gathered this way. A **model**
here is a program that learns one job. The job is guessing what comes next in a piece of text, one small piece
at a time. Learning that job is called **training**, and it works by adjusting a fixed set of numbers
inside the model.

The text a model trains on is called its **dataset**, and this module is about building one. You
collect raw text, clean out the junk, and then check honestly whether the cleaning helped.

Most people skip that last step, yet only it shows whether your work paid off. It is tempting to assume that deleting junk must help, or that more text must always be better.
This lab tests both ideas with a real model that it trains in front of you.

### Where you have met it

The language models behind today’s chatbots train on text that somebody collected and cleaned. So
choices like the ones in this lab help shape how those models behave.

The same problems show up in real datasets, on a far bigger scale. Researchers found one 61-word
sentence repeated more than 60,000 times in C4. That is a large collection of web text used to train models.
They also found that models trained on such collections sometimes write out training passages word for word.

Another team got an older model called GPT-2 to write out real names, phone numbers and email
addresses. Each one had appeared in only one of its training documents.

### Why it matters

A model has only a fixed set of numbers to adjust, so it has a limited amount of room. Think of a
notebook with a fixed number of pages. Every extra copy of a copyright line pushes those numbers toward guessing copyright lines. That leaves them a little worse at guessing the text you care about.

You also need an honest way to tell whether a change helped, because a test can be fooled. Suppose
you test a model on text it already trained on. Its score goes up, but the model gets no better at
anything new. This is like a student who saw the quiz early and earns a higher mark
without learning more.

Removing copies does make a model repeat its training text less often, but cleaning has limits. A
filter can spot a phone number on a page. It cannot tell from the text alone whether you may keep
that number. It cannot tell whether you may reuse the page’s words, or whether the website asked not
to be collected.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- the kinds of junk that cleaning removes, including copies of test text, and which filter catches
  each one;
- why test text must stay out of all training, and what goes wrong when it leaks in;
- why you judge a data change by the model’s score on new text, not by how much stays;
- when a model starts repeating its training text word for word;
- why personal information, permission to reuse text and consent need checks that copy removal
  cannot give.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab gives you 20 lines that look like they came from a small weather website. This page is
fixed and made up, so you cannot change it. Each line carries a tag that says whether the cleaning
kept it or removed it, and why.

You switch filters on and off and watch the tags change. A chart shows how much text survives each
filter. Every time you change a filter, the lab trains the same tiny model twice. One copy trains on
the whole collected page, and the other trains on the lines you kept. The lab then scores both models
on three sentences that neither one saw in training. That shows whether your cleaning helped.

Those scores come from real training, not from made-up numbers. Each score comes from one fixed run,
though, so a tiny gap between two scores can be luck.

One button sneaks a test sentence into the training text. You can watch a score improve for the
wrong reason. A last card swaps in a simpler kind of model. It is only a table of counts and is
not trained at all. You choose how many earlier characters it looks at. Raise that number
and watch for the point where it starts copying its training text word for word.

## What it is

### A pile of collected text

Text copied automatically from web pages is called a **scrape**. If the program follows links from
page to page across the web, it is called a **crawl**. The lab’s scrape is 20 lines long. Each line stands in for a **document**, one item in a dataset such as a web page.

The card **Source documents and cleaning verdicts** lists every line with a small tag, called its
**verdict**. The verdict says what happened to the line. It is one of `kept`, `boilerplate`,
`too short`, `exact repeat`, `near-duplicate` or `held-out overlap`. Each line gets exactly one.
The **Show** control switches the list between two views. **All lines** shows everything with its
verdict, and **Kept only** shows what would go into training.

### Four kinds of junk, and one unfair copy

Mixed in with real sentences about fog, wind and boats are four kinds of junk. All four turn up in
real crawls.

- **Boilerplate** is text that belongs to the website, not to any article on it. Examples are
  `copyright 2019 all rights reserved.` and `click here to subscribe to our newsletter.` Nine of the
  20 lines are boilerplate, and the copyright line alone appears four times.
- An exact repeat is a line that matches an earlier line character for character. The sentence about
  morning fog over the harbor appears twice. Removing copies like this is called **exact
  deduplication**, since a duplicate is a copy.
- A **near-duplicate** says the same thing with a tiny difference. One line writes `harbour`, the
  British spelling, where an earlier line writes `harbor`, and nothing else changes.
- Two fragments, `ok.` and `yes.`, are scraps. They hold almost nothing for a model to learn from.

There is one more problem, and you can add it yourself with a button described later. A test copy is
a line that repeats one of the three test sentences the lab keeps aside. The section
How the lab keeps score explains those sentences. A human reader would not call a test copy junk,
but it makes the test unfair.

### How each filter decides

The card **Cleaning stages** holds four switches: **Strip boilerplate**, **Drop exact duplicates**,
**Drop near-duplicates** and **Decontaminate**. It also has a slider called **Minimum line length**.
Each filter removes lines by a rule you could say out loud, so you can check its choices.

The filters run one after another in a fixed order, like stations on a factory line. A chain of
filters like this is called a cleaning **pipeline**. That is why the card’s small heading reads
`Pipeline`. Each line gets the verdict of the first filter that catches it.

1. **Strip boilerplate** removes any line containing one of five phrases. They are `copyright`,
   `all rights reserved`, `click here`, `subscribe` and `share this page`.
2. **Minimum line length** removes lines shorter than the number of characters you set. It starts
   at off.
3. **Drop exact duplicates** keeps the first copy of a line and removes any later line that matches
   it exactly.
4. **Drop near-duplicates** removes a line that overlaps an already kept line by 0.7 or more. The
   next part explains how that overlap is measured.
5. **Decontaminate** removes a line that shares a run of 8 words in a row with the three test
   sentences. The lab keeps those sentences out of training. This filter catches a test copy.

A computer stores text as a row of small code numbers called **bytes**. That is why the note under
**Drop exact duplicates** calls these copies **byte-identical**. That means the same codes in the
same order. Change one letter and the bytes change, so this filter misses the `harbour` line
completely.

Real pipelines find exact copies by turning each document into a short code number, called a
**hash**. The same text always gives the same hash. The pipeline then keeps only the first document
with each hash.

### Measuring how much two lines overlap

To compare two lines, the near-duplicate filter chops each one into every run of three characters in
a row. Each run is called a **trigram**. The word `harbor` gives `har`, `arb`, `rbo` and `bor`. The
word `harbour` gives `har`, `arb`, `rbo`, `bou` and `our`.

Next, the filter counts the trigrams the two words share. It divides that count by the number of
different trigrams found in either word. Here 3 are shared out of 6 different ones in total. So the
overlap is 3 divided by 6, which is 0.5. This measure is called the **Jaccard similarity**, and the
lab shortens it to **J**.

Now take the two full fog lines. One extra letter barely matters, because the rest of the line is
identical. Only the few trigrams around the `u` differ, so the lines share 54 of their 59 different
trigrams. That puts their overlap at about 0.915, well over the 0.7 cut-off. So when
**Drop near-duplicates** is on, the `harbour` line gets the verdict `near-duplicate · J 0.92`.

### A shortcut for billions of pages

Comparing every pair of lines works for 20 lines. Real datasets hold billions of documents, though,
and one web page can have thousands of trigrams. Comparing full sets of trigrams would take far too
long.

A note at the bottom of **Cleaning stages** shows a shortcut that real pipelines use, called
**MinHash**. It gives each trigram a code number and then scrambles those codes in 64 different ways.
Think of reshuffling a deck of numbered cards 64 times. From each scramble it keeps only the smallest
code. Every line ends up with a list of 64 numbers, called its **signature**.

To see why matching signatures tell you about overlap, take `harbor` and `harbour` again. Between
them they have 6 different trigrams, and 3 are shared. One scramble gives each of the 6 a new random
code. Any one of the 6 is equally likely to end up with the smallest code.

Suppose the smallest code lands on `har`, `arb` or `rbo`. Both words contain that trigram, so their
smallest codes match. Suppose it lands on `bor`, `bou` or `our`. Only one word has that trigram, so
their smallest codes differ. That is 3 chances in 6, or 0.5, which is exactly their overlap.

So the share of the 64 places where two signatures match is an estimate of the overlap. For the two
fog lines, 5 of the 64 places differ, so 59 of the 64 places match. This 59 is a coincidence and not
the same 59 as the trigram count above. It gives an estimate of 0.922, close to the exact answer of
0.915.

A signature is always 64 numbers, however long the page is. So comparing two signatures is much
quicker than comparing two full sets of trigrams. Real pipelines add a grouping trick so that they
do not have to compare every pair at all. Where it breaks describes that trick.

### A picture to keep in mind

Picture getting ready for a weather quiz by studying a stack of pages that someone printed from a
website. On the quiz you get new sentences about the weather. You have to guess, letter by letter,
how each one continues.

Many of the pages end with the same copyright line. One page was printed twice, and another was
reprinted with `harbour` spelled the British way. Two scraps of paper say only `ok.` and `yes.` A
sensible student would ignore the scraps, skip the footers and read the repeated page once.

The model cannot skip anything. It practises on every pair of neighbouring characters in the pile.
Each copy of a line pushes its numbers all over again. Four copies of the copyright line push
four times as hard as one copy. So repetition works like a vote, not like harmless clutter.

The quiz also means something only if its sentences are not in the pile. Suppose one of them was
printed in the stack. A good mark would then partly show what you had memorized, not how well you
predict new text.

The picture stops matching in two places. A student understands what fog is. This model only
adjusts numbers that score which character tends to follow which. A student can also choose what to
study. The model trains on whatever is in its dataset, as many times as it appears.

### How the lab keeps score

The card **Validation loss for both datasets** is where the lab checks whether cleaning paid off. It
trains the same small model twice. One run uses the whole collected page and the other uses the
lines you kept. Nothing else differs between the two runs.

The whole page is 20 lines, or 21 once you leak a test sentence into it. Only the four switches, the
leak button and **Minimum line length** start new training. **Show** leaves the chart alone. So does
the **Context length** slider on the probe card, described below.

The model is the simplest kind of language model, called a **bigram** model. It predicts each
character from only the one character before it. It works with 30 symbols. These are the 26 letters, a space,
a full stop and two spare symbols the text never uses. For each symbol it keeps 30 numbers that
score how likely each symbol is to come next. That makes 30 times 30, or 900 numbers in all.

Training means going through the text and nudging those numbers. Each time, the real next character
gets a slightly higher score. A character and the one after it form a training pair. For example,
`fog` has 3 characters but only 2 pairs, `fo` and `og`. The last character has nothing after
it.

In the same way, the raw scrape’s 899 characters give 898 pairs, and the default cleaned set gives
515. Those are the numbers in the chart’s labels, `raw (898 pairs)` and `cleaned (515 pairs)`. The
cleaned one also appears as **Training pairs** on the card **Dataset statistics**.

Both models go through their whole text 25 times, and each full pass is called an **epoch**. The lab
then tests both models on the same three sentences, 194 characters in all. Neither training set
contains them. Text kept out of training like this is called **held-out** text. It is the only
fair test of how a model handles text it has not seen.

### What the score means

For each character of the held-out text, the model gives all 30 symbols a chance of coming
next. After any character, those chances always add up to 100 percent. So more chance for one symbol
always means less for the others.

If the model gave the real next character a high chance, it collects a small penalty. If it gave
that character a low chance, it collects a large one. A penalty score like this is called a **loss**.
The average penalty over the held-out text is the **held-out loss**, and lower is better.

The loss is measured in a unit called **nats**, and the chart’s side axis reads
`held-out loss (nats/token)`. A **token** is one piece of text, and in this lab it is a single
character. You do not need the maths behind nats, because a few reference points tell you what the
numbers mean.

A model with no idea gives every symbol the same chance, and it would score about 3.4 nats. A
score of 2.3 nats is about as unsure, on average, as choosing among 10 equally likely characters. So
a model that scores 2.3 has narrowed 30 possible next characters down to about 10.

Three numbers under the chart sum up the comparison. **Raw held-out** is the raw model’s score, and
**Cleaned held-out** is the cleaned model’s score. **Difference** is the cleaned score minus the raw
one. A minus sign means cleaning lowered the loss, so the cleaned model predicts new text better.
That difference is the only claim the lab makes about whether cleaning helped.

The chart draws both scores as training goes on. A dashed line shows the raw model and a solid line
shows the cleaned one. Below it, the card **Survival funnel** shows how many lines and characters
are left after each filter. It lists the filters in the order they run.

### The regurgitation probe

The last card, **Regurgitation probe**, asks a different question. When does a model start giving
back its training text word for word? To regurgitate means to bring back up something you swallowed.
A probe is a test that pokes at something to see how it reacts.

This card uses a different kind of model. It is nothing more than a table of counts, and it is not
trained at all. The **Context length** slider sets how many characters it looks back at, and those
characters are called its **context**. For every run of that many characters in its training text,
the table records which character came next.

Here is a made-up example. Say the context length is 3. In the training text, `the` is followed
by a space 4 times and by `r` once. The table’s row for `the` then reads space 4, `r` 1. So after
`the`, the model writes a space 4 times out of 5. It writes an `r` the other 1 time.

The table has one row like this for every different run of 3 characters it has seen. Each of those
runs is one of its contexts. If a row lists only one next character, the model has no choice but to
write it.

At a **Context length** of 1, the model predicts from one character, like the bigram on the chart.
That is why the slider then reads `1 character · the bigram`. The card builds two tables,
**Trained on the raw scrape** and **Trained on the cleaned set**. Each one starts from the
opening characters of its own training text and writes 300 characters.

The card then measures how much of that writing is copied **verbatim**, which means word for word.
Here it means character for character. A character counts as copied when it sits in a run of at
least 16 characters. That run must appear exactly in the training text. That is what the 16 in the readout
**Copied verbatim (16+ chars)** refers to. The bigram model on the chart cannot copy like this.
The probe shows what changes when a model can look further back.

## Why it’s here

### Every copy pulls on the same numbers

The model on the chart has only 900 numbers, and the same numbers serve every line it trains on. The
number that scores `r` after `y` is the same whether that `y` comes from `copyright` or
`sky`.

To see what that means, follow the letter `y` through the raw scrape. It is followed by `r` four
times, always inside `copyright`. It is also followed by a space four times, in words like `sky` and
`by`. So training makes the raw model give `r` about as much chance after `y` as a
space.

The cleaned set has no copyright lines, so `r` never follows `y` there. The three held-out sentences
use `y` in `stays` and in `every`, and neither one is followed by `r`.

Remember that the chances after `y` always add up to 100 percent. So any chance the raw
model saves for `r` is chance it takes from the real answer. In the test, that answer is an `s` or a space. That
raises its penalty. The raw model’s habit of expecting `r` after `y` costs it on the test and earns
it nothing.

Now repeat that for every letter pair in the footers, and you have the whole idea behind **data
quality**. The term sounds like a slogan, but it is not one single property. In practice it means
few copies, the style you want, clean formatting and no test text mixed in.

The first three of those come down to where the model’s limited room gets spent. The fourth is about
keeping the score honest, which the next part covers. How much a model can fit is called its
**capacity**, and with 900 numbers this model has very little.

A line that appears four times pulls those numbers four times as hard as a line that appears once.
So the model’s habits lean toward whatever was copied most, not toward whatever matters most.

### A test only counts if it is new

Cleaning is only half the job, because you also need a fair way to tell whether a change helped.
That brings in the second problem this lab shows, called **leakage**. Leakage is when test text
slips into the training data.

Training on a test sentence nudges the model’s numbers toward that sentence’s exact letter pairs. So
on the test, the model is partly marking its own homework. The score rewards it for having trained
on that exact sentence, not for predicting new text well. That is why the three held-out sentences
stay out of both training sets on purpose. It is also the only reason the chart’s comparison means
anything.

Careful teams go further and cut their data into three separate slices before training starts. The
model learns from the training slice. You check your choices against the **validation** slice.
You save the test slice for one final check at the end. Together these are called a
**train/validation/test split**. The held-out score is often called a **validation loss**, which is
where the chart’s card gets its name.

Real crawls make it hard to keep test text out. A web page can quote a test sentence, and
the next crawl then collects that page. The button **Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape**
copies a test sentence into the page this way. That lets you watch what a leak does.

## How to play with it

### Read before you filter

Set **Show** to **All lines** and read all 20 verdicts before you touch a switch. Try to spot the
four kinds of junk yourself first. They are the footers, the repeated fog line, the `harbour` line and the
two fragments.

At the defaults, **Strip boilerplate** and **Drop exact duplicates** are on and everything else is
off. That is why the `harbour` line, `ok.` and `yes.` are all still marked `kept`.

### Walk through the switches

Now step through the settings below in order, and write down **Cleaned held-out** at each one. Treat
it as a small experiment where you change one thing at a time and compare.

1. Turn every switch off and leave **Minimum line length** at off. All 20 lines and 899
   **Characters** stay in. **Cleaned held-out** reads 2.365, and the two lines on the chart sit on top of
   each other. **Difference** reads +0.000, because both models trained on the same text. This
   is your starting point, which scientists call the control. It is the setting you compare every
   other one against.
2. Turn on **Strip boilerplate** alone, which leaves 11 lines and 580 characters. The score falls to
   2.306, which is better.
3. Add **Drop exact duplicates**, which brings you back to the defaults with 10 lines and 516
   characters. The score is now 2.300, and **Difference** reads −0.065.
4. Add **Drop near-duplicates**, which leaves 9 lines and 451 characters. The score rises to 2.312,
   which is worse.
5. Add **Minimum line length** at 20 chars as well, and the score moves to 2.316. That change of
   0.004 is too small to trust on its own, as Where it breaks explains.

The −0.065 is small but real. Here it is in the “equally likely characters” terms from earlier. The
raw model was about as unsure as choosing among 10.6 characters. The cleaned one was about as unsure
as choosing among 10.

The **Characters** readout counts letters, spaces and full stops the way the model receives them.
Before counting, the lab turns anything outside the model’s 30 symbols into a space. It also squeezes
repeated spaces into one. So `copyright 2019 all` becomes `copyright all`, and the digits do not
count.

**Try this:** switch **Strip boilerplate** off and leave **Drop exact duplicates** on, with
everything else off. You get 13 lines and 623 characters. **Cleaned held-out** reads 2.292, a little
lower than the defaults, even though footer lines are back in the dataset.

The verdicts show why. The footer lines are exact copies of one another. So **Drop exact duplicates**
already removes every copy after the first. The 13 kept lines are the default 10, plus one
copyright, one subscribe and one share line. Together those add 107 characters.

Once each footer appears only once, it stops acting like a loud vote. Its letter pairs are ordinary
English pairs that the weather sentences use too. Examples are `th` in `this` and `on` in
`on social media`. So at one copy each, the footers cost the model almost nothing. In this one run
they even come out slightly ahead. A later section shows why you cannot trust a gap this
small.

### Leak a sentence, then clean it out

Go back to the defaults, with **Strip boilerplate** and **Drop exact duplicates** on and everything
else off. Then press **Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape**.

The button copies “the fog returns in the morning and the pattern repeats.” into the page. That
sentence is one of the three test sentences. This is how a quoted test item can end up in a real
crawl. Four things change as soon as it lands.

- **Cleaned held-out** improves from 2.300 to 2.245. That looks like progress until you read the
  rest of the card.
- **Raw held-out** improves too, from 2.365 to 2.324. The raw model trains on the whole page,
  copied sentence included. The leak flatters both scores.
- The contamination check under the chart looks for test text mixed into the training text. It now
  finds a 10-word run shared with the test text. That run is the whole copied sentence, and the check
  marks it as flagged.
- A new line appears under the check. It gives the cleaned model’s score on the two test sentences
  that the leak did not copy. It reads 2.422.

Now turn on **Decontaminate**, which removes training lines that overlap the test text. This step is
called **decontamination**. The copied line gets the verdict `held-out overlap` and leaves the
cleaned training set. **Cleaned held-out** goes back to 2.300.

The line about the two untouched sentences now reads 2.397. That is the cleaned model’s honest score
on them. **Difference** reads −0.024 instead of −0.065, because only the cleaned side was
decontaminated. The raw model still trains on the copied sentence.

### Probe for recitation

Before you start, press the leak button again so it reads `off`, and turn **Decontaminate** off. You
are now back at the defaults. Scroll down to the **Regurgitation probe** card. The
**Context length** slider starts at 3, so move it down to 1 first.

Now step the slider up one notch at a time to 8. At each step, compare **Copied verbatim (16+ chars)**
for the two models.

- At 1 character of context, both models read 0 percent, and the samples are gibberish. The raw
  model’s sample starts `l. cit cl wn yried`, which uses real letter pairs but no real words.
- At 3, the raw model copies 52 percent of its sample and the cleaned one 39 percent. The raw sample
  now starts `yright all with warms the fog settles`. That is scraps of real lines stitched
  together.
- At 4, the raw model copies 98 percent and the cleaned one 93 percent.
- At 8, both read 100 percent. The raw sample now reads like the scrape itself:
  `all rights reserved. by noon the clouds break apart`.

Keep an eye on **Footer text in the sample** as well. It counts how much of each sample comes from
the footer phrases. At 8 characters of context, 21 percent of the raw
sample is footer text. None of the cleaned sample is.

Below the two columns, a table runs the same test at eight context lengths at once. These are 1 to 6, 8 and
12. It lets you compare them without moving the slider. When the slider sits on one of those eight
values, that row is shaded.

## What to notice

### The filters overlap

Watch the badge on **Drop exact duplicates**. It reads −1 lines while **Strip boilerplate** is on.
The only copy left for it to catch is the repeated fog sentence.

Switch boilerplate off and the same badge reads −7. Now it also catches three extra copyright lines,
two extra subscribe lines and one extra share line. The two filters were partly removing the same
lines. So you cannot judge either one by looking at it alone.

### Lines and characters tell different stories

The **Survival funnel** has one row per stage: **Scraped**, **Strip boilerplate**, **Length floor**,
**Exact dedup**, **Near-dup** and **Decontaminate**. **Length floor** is the **Minimum line length**
slider, and dedup is short for deduplication. At the defaults, those rows read 20, 11, 11, 10, 10, 10
lines.

Boilerplate takes 9 of the 20 lines, which is almost half of them. Yet it takes only 319 of the 899
characters, about a third, because footer lines are short.

So the same filter can look huge when you count documents and modest when you count characters. When
someone reports how much a filter removed, it matters which of the two they counted.

A stage that is switched on but removes nothing still shows a full row that reads
`removes nothing here`. That is not a mistake. Either an earlier stage already took its lines,
or nothing on this page matches its rule. **Decontaminate** does this when no
sentence has leaked.

### Less text helped, but only up to a point

At the defaults, cleaning removed about 40 percent of the characters and the score still improved.
More text helps only when it brings something new. The fourth copy of a footer brings nothing that
the first copy did not.

Removing more does not keep helping, though. In the walk above, adding **Drop near-duplicates** made
the score slightly worse in this run. Adding **Minimum line length** moved it by too little to read.
Neither one clearly helped, even though both delete lines that you would call junk if you read them.

### When a change is too small to read

Now compare what a **Minimum line length** of 20 does in two different setups. With
**Drop near-duplicates** on, it moves the score from 2.312 to 2.316, which is slightly worse. With
near-duplicates off, the same setting moves the score from 2.300 to 2.296, which is slightly better.

In this one run, the same setting seems to help in one case and hurt in the other. Both gaps are only
0.004 nats, which Where it breaks shows is too small to trust on its own. So take this as a warning
and not as a result. This lab cannot tell you whether a length floor of 20 helps.

The badge on **Drop exact duplicates** showed the firmer version of the same lesson. A filter is
always measured on whatever text the other filters left, so you cannot judge it by itself.

At 12, **Minimum line length** removes only `ok.` and `yes.`, the two fragments, and nothing else.
The shortest real sentence is 56 characters long, so the slider starts deleting real sentences at 57.

### Diversity is counted per character

**Distinct trigrams** counts how many different three-character runs the text contains. It drops
from 366 in the raw scrape to 285 after cleaning. You might guess that this is because the cleaned
text is shorter, but it is not. With only **Drop exact duplicates** on, the text shrinks by about a third
and still has all 366. Every copy repeats runs that the first one already had.

The drop comes from the footer lines. They hold runs like `cop` and `sub`, from `copyright` and
`subscribe`, that no real sentence uses. Removing those lines removes those runs for good.

**Repetition** is the share of all trigrams in the text that repeat ones already counted. For example, `ha ha ha` has 6 trigrams once you count the spaces. They are `ha␣`, `a␣h`, `␣ha`, `ha␣`, `a␣h` and
`␣ha`, where ␣ stands for a space. Only 3 of them are different, so half are repeats. Its repetition
is 50 percent.

Cleaning lowers **Repetition** from a **Raw repetition** of 60 percent to 45 percent. So the cleaned
text has fewer different sequences in total, yet it repeats itself less.

The share of trigrams that are new, not repeats, is called **trigram diversity**. It is the flip side
of **Repetition**, so 45 percent repetition means 55 percent diversity. It measures how varied the
text is per character, not how much text there is.

### The split bar

At the defaults, the **train/validation/test split** bar on **Dataset statistics** reads `train 7`,
`val 2` and `test 1`. It is cut by whole lines, so no sentence lands in two slices.

The three held-out sentences on the chart are separate again. The bar is only a picture of a split,
since no model trains or scores on it.

### A leak looks like progress

Picture a class where one question from Friday’s quiz gets posted in the group chat the night
before. Everyone gets that question right and the class average jumps. But the average now tells the
teacher less about what the class can do, not more.

The lab shows the model version of this, with one twist that makes the point even sharper. With the
leak on, the headline score got better. But the cleaned model got worse on the two test sentences
that were not copied. It scored 2.397 on them without the leak and 2.422 with it.

That is not wobble, because the same happens when training is rerun in eight different shuffled
orders. The lab does not show exactly why. The bigram cannot recite the copied sentence. But it has
now practised every letter pair in it, so its shared numbers lean toward those pairs.

That lean seems to have cost the other two sentences slightly. Either way, the better headline number
came from one copied answer, not from a better model.

### Ordinary overlaps are short; copies are long

Compare the cleaned training text with the test
sentences. Without the leak, the longest run of words they share is two words long: “the morning”. Ordinary phrases overlap by a word or two, while a copied
sentence shares 10 words in a row.

That gap is why a check for 8 words in a row catches the copy and nothing else. A run of words in a
row is called an **n-gram**, where n stands for the number of words. Here the pieces are whole words,
while the trigrams earlier were three characters. So this check looks for shared 8-grams.

Without the leak, turn **Decontaminate** on and its badge reads −0 lines. No ordinary line shares 8
words with the test text.

### Recitation switches on quickly

Sometimes a model gives back a stretch of its training text when you hand it the
start. This is called **memorization**. In the probe, it switches on over a narrow range of context
lengths. Nothing is copied at 1 or 2 characters of context, and by 5 almost every character is.

Picture a friend who knows every line of one film by heart. Say one word, like “the”, and they
cannot tell which scene you mean, because dozens of lines contain it. Say eight words in a row, and
only one line in the whole film starts that way. Now they can carry on word for word.

The probe works the same way, although it only counts and understands nothing about the text. It also
counts characters, not words, so 8 characters is only a word or two. In text this small, even that
is enough to pin down one line.

Set the slider to 8 and read the note under each sample. It counts how many contexts have only one
possible next character. For the raw model, 541 of its 555 contexts are like that. So it has no
choice but to carry on with the text it was built from.

Set the slider back to 1 and the same note for the cleaned model reads 4 of 26. With one character of
context, most contexts have several possible next characters. So the samples wander instead of
copying.

### Repeated footers get recited more

Repeated lines carry more counts, so the model picks them more often. At a context length of 8,
footer text makes up 21 percent of the raw model’s sample. The raw scrape holds four copies of the
copyright line.

To see that the copies matter, switch **Strip boilerplate** off and leave
**Drop exact duplicates** on. Now each footer survives exactly once. At a context length of 8, footer
text now makes up only 6 percent of the cleaned model’s sample.

Cleaning does not stop the cleaned model from copying its own sentences, though. It copies 100
percent at 8 as well. Removing copies changes which text gets recited and how often. It does not
change whether a model with enough context recites at all.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Both models really train, and both held-out scores are real, so the lab is not faking its results.
Its limits come from its small size and from the things you cannot adjust.

Twenty lines is nowhere near a real dataset. The gaps between the good settings are only a few thousandths of a nat. That is smaller than the wobble from training on the same text in a different order.

The model visits its training pairs in a shuffled order. A recipe that starts from a fixed number,
called a **seed**, makes that shuffle. The same seed always gives the same shuffle, like dealing a
deck in the same order every time. The lab has no slider or switch to change it.

So every score you see comes from a single run. There is no **error bar**, which shows how
much a result would wobble if you ran it again.

Behind the scenes, the people who built the lab trained both models again with every seed from 1 to
8. Across those runs, the gap between the raw and cleaned scores always stays between 0.065 and
0.085. So you can trust that cleaning helped.

The gap between 2.292 and 2.300 points one way at some seeds and the other way at others. So you
cannot say which of those two settings is really better.

Picture two sprinters who finish a hundredth of a second apart and then swap places when they race
again. That tiny gap tells you nothing about who is faster. A gap counts as evidence only when it is
bigger than the usual wobble. The gap between the two best settings here is not.

The lab’s filters and readouts are also cruder than they look, in several ways.

- The 20 lines are a fixed teaching page, not a real crawl. You cannot paste in a page of your
  own.
- The boilerplate filter is only five English phrases, so it misses footers worded any
  other way.
- The near-duplicate test compares single short lines. Real near-duplicate checks compare whole
  documents that can be thousands of words long. Its 0.7 cut-off is also fixed, with no control to
  change it.
- That test compares every pair of lines directly, which works only because there are 20 of them.
  The **MinHash** number is computed for real, but the filter itself does not use it.
- **Trigram diversity** counts letter sequences. It calls a page varied for using many of them, not
  for covering many topics.
- The split bar on **Dataset statistics** never trains or scores a model. So the only held-out score
  on the page is the three-sentence passage on the chart.

The model on the chart predicts from one character, so it cannot recite long passages. All the chart
can show is the model’s limited room being spent on the wrong patterns.

The **Regurgitation probe** uses a table of counts built from only 516 to 899 characters. In so
little text, almost every long context is always followed by the same character, so reciting is
certain. A real model is far larger and picks up general patterns where this table cannot. It also
memorizes only part of what it trained on.

The probe also starts each sample from the opening characters of its training text. That mirrors how
an attempt to pull text out of a real model starts from a known beginning. In this tiny table,
starting from another point inside the training text would copy about as much. Almost every
long context has only one continuation. A prompt from outside the training text
gives a model less to copy. So it usually copies less.

The probe’s own seed is fixed as well, so each context length always prints the same text.

**A common mix-up:** it is tempting to think that deleting data is what helped, but it is not.
Removing about 40 percent of this scrape improved the score. Yet **Drop near-duplicates** removes
even more and makes the score worse. Smaller is not automatically better, and neither is less
repeated. The `harbour` line is a near-repeat, but removing it did not help here. The held-out score
tells you whether a change helped. The character count does not, and neither does a filter that
sounds more responsible.

### Where real pipelines struggle

Real pipelines face problems that this lab is too small to show. Each one is a place where the real
mechanism can fail.

**Finding copies at web scale:** hashes still find exact copies across the whole
web, but they miss near-copies. Nobody can compare every pair among billions of documents. So
near-copy search uses MinHash, or a similar method called SimHash, along with a sorting trick called
locality-sensitive hashing.

That trick only compares documents whose signatures land in the same group, which saves most of the
work. Even so, near-copy search is one of the most expensive steps in the whole pipeline.

**Judging quality:** real boilerplate removal uses a trained program to find a page’s main text.
It does not rely on a short list of phrases. Quality filtering usually means a **classifier**, which is a
program that sorts documents into groups.

A classifier scores each document by how much it looks like a reference collection. So whatever that
collection leans toward, the filter leans toward too. If the reference is mostly one style of
writing, other styles get pushed toward the bottom.

Separate steps handle which language a page is in, personal data, licences and toxic text. Each of
these is a policy decision as much as a technical one.

**Keeping test text out:** teams search for long runs of words shared with **benchmarks**.
Benchmarks are the standard public tests used to compare models. GPT-3’s report used runs 13 words long. Leaks still
happen, because benchmark questions get quoted in posts that the next crawl collects. Reworded copies
also slip past a search for exact runs. In practice, a held-out set stays held out only until
someone publishes it.

**Counting in tokens:** near-copy and contamination checks first tidy the text,
such as by making it lowercase. Real models split text into tokens in different ways. Some
use single characters like this lab. Others use whole words or pieces of words, such as `harb`
plus `our`.

So the same page can count as 900 tokens with one method and 200 with another. A funnel reported in
tokens only makes sense for the method that counted them.

**Memorizing a single copy:** removing copies lowers how often a model recites its training text.
A large model can still memorize text that appears only once. The next section shows why that matters
for names, phone numbers and email addresses.

## Going deeper

### Choosing what gets seen twice

Filtering decides what is allowed into a dataset. The **data mixture** decides what share of the
training each source gets. Giving a small, high-quality source a few extra passes is normal practice.
So after deduplication removes the accidental copies, teams put some repetition back on purpose. The
rule is not “duplicates are bad” but “you should be the one choosing which text gets seen twice”.

Picture making a road-trip playlist. First you delete the songs that got added three times by
accident. Then you choose to play your two favourites twice. The second step is still
repetition, but it is repetition you chose. That is the difference between an accidental duplicate
and a mixture.

### What a held-out score settles

**Held-out loss** answers one question well: did this change help the model predict text like the
held-out sample? It cannot tell you whether that sample looks like the text you care about.

A filter that strips out blocks of computer code might look fine when you score on ordinary
sentences. It could still ruin the model’s results on a coding test. That is why real pipelines
score against several held-out sets and expect them to disagree.

### Why the split has three parts

You check the validation slice again and again while you tune your choices. So it slowly stops being
truly held out. Picture trying 50 different filter settings and keeping whichever scores best on the
validation slice.

Part of the winner’s lead is luck that happens to suit those few sentences, like the sprinters’
wobble. The more settings you try, the more your pick is tuned to the validation slice itself. Its
score starts to flatter you. That is why the test slice stays untouched until one final check.

Cutting the **train/validation/test split** by whole document, not by sentence, is what makes the
whole thing work. Two sentences from the same page share a lot of wording. Splitting a page
between training and testing would let one leak into the other.

### Memorization and regurgitation

**Memorization** is a model giving back training text when you hand it the start. Carlini and colleagues measured it across several families of models. They found it
grows steadily with three things.

- The model’s capacity, which means how big it is.
- How many times an example was duplicated in its training data.
- How much context the prompt supplies. The prompt is the text you hand the model to start it off.

Each time one of these doubles, the amount memorized rises by roughly the same step. The probe’s
slider is closest to the third one. Raising it also makes the table bigger, though, so it changes the
model’s size as well.

Of the three, duplication is the one that the people building a dataset can control. Lee and
colleagues tested models trained on standard datasets. Over 1 percent of their unprompted writing was copied word for word
from their training data. In C4, the team found
one 61-word sentence repeated over 60,000 times.

Training on their deduplicated data produced models that gave out memorized text ten times less
often. That is a big improvement, but it is not the whole answer.

In another study, Carlini and colleagues got GPT-2 to write out hundreds of word-for-word passages
from its training text. These included names, phone numbers and email addresses. Each of those
passages appeared in only one document. The team also found that larger models were more
vulnerable.

### Data governance: personal data, licences, consent

Three questions about a dataset cannot be answered by reading its text. May this person’s details be
used? May this text be reused? Did its owners agree? So no filter in this lab can answer them.
Keeping records and rules about questions like these is called **data governance**. It covers things
such as where each document came from and what may be done with it.

- **Personal data** is text that identifies a person, such as a name, phone number or email address.
  Spotting it is a detection problem that a filter can help with. Deciding whether it may be used is
  a legal one. The GPT-2 result above shows why even one appearance matters.
- **Licences** are the rules that say who may reuse a piece of text, and how. An audit checked more
  than 1,800 text datasets on the websites that host them. It found licence information missing in
  over 70 percent of cases. In a separate count, it found licences labelled wrongly in over 50
  percent. The two figures measure different things, so they do not add up to one whole.
- **Consent** is about whether the owners of a text agreed to its use. Websites state their wishes in
  their terms of service and in a file called `robots.txt`. That file tells automatic collectors what
  they may and may not take. A 2024 audit looked at 14,000 web domains, which are website names. It found
  these restrictions rose sharply in a single year. If those files were respected, they would now fully block over 5 percent of the
  text in C4. The audit also found that 45 percent
  of C4 is restricted by the sites’ terms of service.

Rules are arriving, too. One of them is a European Union law called the EU AI Act. It covers models
built for many different tasks, called general-purpose AI models. It asks their makers to keep a
policy for following EU copyright law.

Rights holders are the people who own the copyright in a piece of writing. Some of them have stated
that nobody may use their work this way. This is called reserving their rights. The makers’ policy
has to cover these cases. The law also asks them to publish a detailed enough summary of what they
trained on.

Both of these duties need a record kept alongside the data, not a better filter.

### Toy versus real

- **Scale:** the lab’s scrape is 20 lines and 899 characters. Its most repeated line, the
  copyright footer, appears four times. Lee and colleagues found one 61-word sentence repeated over
  60,000 times in C4.
- **Overlap checks:** the lab flags an 8-word run shared with the test text. GPT-3’s report flagged
  any test example that shared a 13-word run with its training data. Lee and colleagues
  also checked the validation sets of standard datasets. Over 4 percent of them overlapped with their training
  data.
- **Reciting:** the probe copies everything at 8 characters of context because its text is tiny. In
the models Lee and colleagues trained, over 1 percent of unprompted writing was
copied word for word. Carlini and colleagues also found that memorization grows as models get
  bigger. So a real model’s size affects how much it recites.
- **What real pipelines add:** language detection, trained quality classifiers and personal-data
  detection. They also keep records of licences and opt-outs, plus a log of why they dropped each
  document.
