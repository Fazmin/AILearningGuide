## The big picture

Copy all the text from almost any web page and you get more than the article you wanted. You also
get the menu, a box asking you to subscribe to a newsletter, a row of share buttons and a copyright
line at the bottom. A program that collects web pages automatically picks up all of that too, and it
often collects the same page, or a copy of it, more than once.

Models like the ones behind chatbots learn from huge piles of text gathered in this way. A **model**
here is a program that learns one job: guessing what comes next in a piece of text, one small piece
at a time. Learning that job is called **training**, and it works by adjusting a fixed set of
numbers inside the model.

The collection of text that a model trains on is called its **dataset**, and this module is about
building one. That means collecting raw text, cleaning out the junk, and then checking honestly
whether the cleaning actually helped.

That last step is the one people most often skip, even though it is the only step that tells you
whether your work paid off. It is tempting to assume that deleting junk must help, or that more
text must always be better. This lab tests both ideas with a real model that it trains in front of
you, instead of leaving them as guesses.

### Where you have met it

The language models behind today’s chatbots are trained on text that somebody collected and
cleaned, so choices like the ones in this lab help shape their habits.

The problems in this lab also show up in real datasets, on a far bigger scale. Researchers found one
61-word sentence repeated more than 60,000 times in C4, a large collection of web text used to train
models. They also found that models trained on collections like it sometimes write out passages from
their training text word for word.

Another team got an older model called GPT-2 to write out real names, phone numbers and email
addresses, though each had appeared in only one of its training documents.

### Why it matters

Because a model has only a fixed set of numbers to adjust, it has a limited amount of room, a bit
like a notebook with a fixed number of pages. Every extra copy of a copyright line pushes those
numbers toward guessing copyright lines, which leaves them a little worse at guessing the text you
actually care about.

You also need an honest way to tell whether a change helped, because a test can be fooled. If you
test a model on text it already trained on, its score improves while the model gets no better at
anything new. It is like a student who saw the quiz questions in advance and gets a higher mark
without learning more.

Removing copies does make a model repeat its training text less often, but cleaning has limits. A
filter can spot a phone number on a page, but it cannot tell from the text alone whether you are
allowed to keep it. Nor can it tell whether you may reuse the page’s words, or whether the website
asked not to be collected.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- the kinds of junk that cleaning removes from collected text, including copies of test text, and
  which filter catches each one;
- why the text you test a model on has to stay out of everything the model trains on, and what goes
  wrong when some of it leaks in;
- why you judge a change to a dataset by the model’s score on new text, not by how much text you
  kept or threw away;
- when a model starts repeating its training text word for word, and why personal information,
  permission to reuse text, and consent need checks that removing copies cannot give.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab gives you 20 lines that look like they were copied from a small weather website. They are a
fixed, made-up page that you cannot change, and each line is tagged with whether the cleaning kept
it or removed it, and why.

You switch filters on and off and watch lines change their tags, while a chart shows how much text
survives each filter. Every time you change a filter, the lab trains the same tiny model twice, once
on the whole collected page and once on the lines you kept. It then scores both models on three

Those scores come from real training, not from made-up numbers. Each one comes from a single fixed
run, though, so a tiny gap between two scores can be luck.

One button sneaks a test sentence into the training text, so you can watch a score improve for the

## What it is

### A pile of collected text

Text copied automatically from web pages is called a **scrape**, or a **crawl** when the program
follows links from page to page across the web. The lab’s scrape is 20 lines long, and each line
stands in for a **document**, meaning one item in a dataset, such as one web page.

The card **Source documents and cleaning verdicts** lists every line with a small tag, called its
**verdict**, that says what happened to it. A verdict is one of `kept`, `boilerplate`,
`too short`, `exact repeat`, `near-duplicate` or `held-out overlap`, and each line gets exactly
one. The **Show** control switches the list between **All lines**, which shows everything with its
verdict, and **Kept only**, which shows what would go into training.

### Four kinds of junk, and one unfair copy

Mixed in with real sentences about fog, wind and boats are four kinds of junk, and all four turn up
in real crawls:

- **Boilerplate** is text that belongs to the website rather than to any article on it, like
  `copyright 2019 all rights reserved.` or `click here to subscribe to our newsletter.` Nine of
  the 20 lines are boilerplate, and the copyright line alone appears four times.
- An exact repeat is a line that matches an earlier line character for character. The sentence about
  morning fog over the harbor appears twice, and removing copies like this is called **exact
  deduplication**, since a duplicate is a copy.
- A **near-duplicate** says the same thing with a tiny difference: one line writes `harbour`, the
  British spelling, where an earlier line writes `harbor`, and nothing else changes.
- Two fragments, `ok.` and `yes.`, are scraps with almost nothing in them for a model to learn
  from.

There is one more problem, which you can add yourself with a button described later. A test copy is
a line that repeats one of the three sentences the lab keeps aside to test the model. The section
How the lab keeps score explains those sentences. A test copy is not junk to a human reader, but it would make
the test unfair.

### How each filter decides

The card **Cleaning stages** holds four switches, **Strip boilerplate**, **Drop exact duplicates**,
**Drop near-duplicates** and **Decontaminate**, plus a slider called **Minimum line length**. Each
filter removes lines by a rule you could say out loud, so you can check every decision it makes.

The filters run one after another in a fixed order, like stations on a production line, and a chain
of filters like this is called a cleaning **pipeline**. That is why the card’s small heading reads
`Pipeline`. Each line gets the verdict of the first filter that catches it:

1. **Strip boilerplate** removes any line containing one of five phrases: `copyright`,
   `all rights reserved`, `click here`, `subscribe` or `share this page`.
2. **Minimum line length** removes lines shorter than the number of characters you set, and it
   starts at off.
3. **Drop exact duplicates** keeps the first copy of a line and removes any later line that matches
   it exactly.
4. **Drop near-duplicates** removes a line that overlaps an already kept line by 0.7 or more, on a
   measure explained below.
5. **Decontaminate** removes a line that shares a run of 8 words in a row with the three test
   sentences, which the lab keeps out of training. This is the filter that catches a test copy.

A computer stores text as a row of small code numbers called **bytes**. That is why the note under
**Drop exact duplicates** calls these copies **byte-identical**, meaning the same codes in the same
order. Add or change one letter and the bytes change, which is why this filter misses the `harbour`
line completely. Real pipelines find exact copies by turning each document into a short code number,
called a **hash**, where the same text always gives the same code. They then keep only the first
document with each hash.

### Measuring how much two lines overlap

To compare two lines, the near-duplicate filter chops each one into every run of three characters in
a row, called a **trigram**. The word `harbor` gives `har`, `arb`, `rbo` and `bor`, while
`harbour` gives `har`, `arb`, `rbo`, `bou` and `our`.

Next, the filter counts the trigrams the two have in common and divides by the number of different
trigrams found in either one. Here 3 are shared out of 6 different ones in total, so the overlap is
3 divided by 6, which is 0.5. This measure is called the **Jaccard similarity**, and the lab
shortens it to **J**.

For the two full fog lines, one extra letter barely matters, because the rest of the line is
identical. Only the few trigrams around the `u` differ, so the lines share 54 of their 59 different
trigrams. That puts their overlap at about 0.915, well over the 0.7 cut-off. So when
**Drop near-duplicates** is on, the `harbour` line gets the verdict `near-duplicate · J 0.92`.

### A shortcut for billions of pages

Comparing every pair of lines like this works for 20 lines. Real datasets hold billions of
documents, though, and one web page can have thousands of trigrams, so comparing full sets of
trigrams would take far too long.

A note at the bottom of **Cleaning stages** shows a shortcut that real pipelines use, called
**MinHash**. It gives each trigram a code number, then scrambles those codes in 64 different ways,
like reshuffling a deck of numbered cards 64 times. From each scramble it keeps only the smallest
code, so every line ends up with a list of 64 numbers, called its **signature**.

To see why matching signatures tell you about overlap, take `harbor` and `harbour` again. Between
them they have 6 different trigrams, and 3 are shared. One scramble gives each of the 6 a new random
code, and any one of the 6 is equally likely to end up with the smallest code.

If the smallest code lands on `har`, `arb` or `rbo`, both words contain it, so their smallest codes
match. If it lands on `bor`, `bou` or `our`, only one word has it, so their smallest codes differ.
That is 3 chances in 6, or 0.5, which is exactly their overlap.

So the share of the 64 places where two signatures match is an estimate of the overlap. For the two
fog lines, 5 of the 64 places differ, so 59 of the 64 places match. (This 59 is a coincidence, not
the same 59 as the trigram count above.) That gives an estimate of 0.922, close to the exact answer of 0.915.

A signature is always 64 numbers, however long the page is, so comparing two signatures is much
quicker than comparing two full sets of trigrams. Real pipelines also add a grouping trick, described
in Where it breaks, so that they do not have to compare every pair at all.

### A picture to keep in mind

Picture getting ready for a weather quiz by studying a stack of pages that someone printed from a
website. On the quiz you will be given new sentences about the weather and asked to guess, letter by
letter, how each one continues.

Many of the pages end with the same copyright line, one page was printed twice, and another was
reprinted with `harbour` spelled the British way. Two scraps of paper say only `ok.` and `yes.`,
and a sensible student would ignore them, skip the footers and read the repeated page once.

The model cannot skip anything, because it practises on every pair of neighbouring characters in the
pile, and each copy of a line pushes its numbers all over again. Four copies of the copyright line
push four times as hard as one copy, so repetition works like a vote rather than harmless clutter.

The quiz also means something only if its sentences are not in the pile. If one of them were printed
in the stack, a good mark would partly show what you had memorized rather than how well you predict
new text.

The picture stops matching in two places. A student understands what fog is, while this model only
adjusts numbers that score which character tends to follow which. A student can also choose what to
study, while the model trains on whatever is in its dataset, as many times as it appears.

### How the lab keeps score

The card **Validation loss for both datasets** is where the lab checks whether cleaning paid off. It
trains the same small model twice, once on the whole collected page and once on the lines you kept,
and nothing else differs between the two runs.

The whole page is 20 lines, or 21 once you leak a test sentence into it. Only the four switches, the
leak button and **Minimum line length** start new training. **Show** leaves the chart alone, and so
does the **Context length** slider on the probe card, described below.

The model is the simplest kind of language model, called a **bigram** model, which predicts each
character from only the one character before it. It works with 30 symbols: the 26 letters, a space,
a full stop and two spare symbols that this text never uses. For each symbol it keeps 30 numbers
that score how likely each symbol is to come next, which makes 30 times 30, or 900 numbers in all.

Training means going through the text and nudging those numbers so that the real next character
gets a slightly higher score each time. Each character and the one after it form a training pair.
For example, `fog` has 3 characters but only 2 pairs, `fo` and `og`, because the last character has
nothing after it.

In the same way, the raw scrape’s 899 characters give 898 pairs, and the default cleaned set gives
515. Those are the numbers in the chart’s labels, `raw (898 pairs)` and `cleaned (515 pairs)`, and
the cleaned one also appears as **Training pairs** on the card **Dataset statistics**.

Both models go through their whole text 25 times, and each full pass through the text is called an
**epoch**. Both are then tested on the same three sentences, 194 characters in all, which are in
neither training set. Text kept out of training like this is called **held-out** text, and it is
the only fair test of how a model handles text it was not trained on.

### What the score means

For every character of the held-out text, the model gives each of the 30 symbols a chance of coming
next. After any character, those chances always add up to 100 percent, so giving more chance to one
symbol always means giving less to the others.

If the model gave the real next character a high chance, it collects a small penalty, and if it gave
it a low chance, it collects a large one. A penalty score like this is called a **loss**, and the
average penalty over the held-out text is the **held-out loss**, where lower is better.

The loss is measured in a unit called **nats**, and the chart’s side axis reads
`held-out loss (nats/token)`. A **token** is one piece of text, which in this lab is a single
character. You do not need the maths behind nats, because a few reference points tell you what the
numbers mean.

A model with no idea at all, which gives every symbol the same chance, would score about 3.4 nats.
A score of 2.3 nats is about as unsure, on average, as choosing among 10 equally likely characters.
So a model that scores 2.3 has, in effect, narrowed 30 possible next characters down to about 10.

Three numbers under the chart sum up the comparison: **Raw held-out** is the raw model’s score, and
**Cleaned held-out** is the cleaned model’s score. **Difference** is the cleaned score minus the raw
one, so a minus sign means cleaning lowered the loss and the cleaned model predicts new text better.
That difference is the only claim the lab makes about whether cleaning helped.

The chart itself draws both scores as training goes on, with a dashed line for the raw model and a
solid line for the cleaned one. Below it, the card **Survival funnel** shows how many lines and
characters are left after each filter, in the order the filters run.

### The regurgitation probe

The last card, **Regurgitation probe**, asks a different question: when does a model start giving
back its training text word for word? To regurgitate means to bring back up something you
swallowed, and a probe is a test that pokes at something to see how it reacts.

This card uses a different kind of model, which is nothing more than a table of counts and is not
trained at all. You set how many characters it looks back at with the **Context length** slider,
and the characters it looks at are called its **context**. For every run of that many characters in
its training text, the table records which character came next.

Here is a made-up example. Say the context length is 3, and in the training text `the` is followed
by a space 4 times and by `r` once. The table’s row for `the` then reads space 4, `r` 1. So after
`the`, the model writes a space 4 times out of 5 and `r` 1 time out of 5.

The table has one row like this for every different run of 3 characters it has seen, and each of
those runs is one of its contexts. If a row lists only one next character, the model has no choice
but to write it.

At a **Context length** of 1, it predicts from one character, like the bigram on the chart, which is
why the slider then reads `1 character · the bigram`. The card builds two of these tables, shown as
**Trained on the raw scrape** and **Trained on the cleaned set**. Each one starts from the opening
characters of its own training text and writes 300 characters.

The card then measures how much of that writing is copied **verbatim**, which means word for word,
or here, character for character. A character counts as copied when it sits inside a run of at
least 16 characters that appears exactly in the training text. That is what the 16 in the readout
**Copied verbatim (16+ chars)** refers to. The bigram model on the chart cannot copy like this, and
the probe shows what changes when a model can look further back.

## Why it’s here

### Every copy pulls on the same numbers

The model on the chart has only 900 numbers, and the same numbers serve every line it trains on.
The number that scores `r` after `y` is the same number whether that `y` comes from `copyright` or
from `sky`.

To see what that means, follow the letter `y` through the raw scrape, where it is followed by `r`
four times, always inside `copyright`. It is also followed by a space four times, in words like
`sky` and `by`. So training pushes the raw model to give `r` about as much chance after `y` as it
gives a space.

The cleaned set has no copyright lines, so `r` never follows `y` there. The three held-out sentences
use `y` in `stays` and in `every`, and neither one is followed by `r`.

Remember that the chances after `y` always add up to 100 percent. So every bit of chance the raw
model saves for `r` is taken away from the `s` or the space that really comes next in the test.
That raises its penalty. The raw model’s habit of expecting `r` after `y` therefore costs it
on the test and earns it nothing.

Now repeat that for every letter pair in the footers, and you have the whole idea behind
**data quality**. The term sounds like a slogan, but it is not one single property. In practice it
means little duplication, text in the style you want, correct formatting and no test text mixed in.

The first three of those come down to where the model’s limited room gets spent, while the fourth is
about keeping the score honest, which the next part covers. How much a model can fit is called its
**capacity**, and with 900 numbers this model has very little.

A line that appears four times pulls those numbers four times as hard as a line that appears once.
So the model’s habits lean toward whatever was copied most, not toward whatever matters most.

### A test only counts if it is new

Cleaning is only half the job, because you also need a fair way to tell whether a change helped.
That brings in the second problem this lab is built to show, called **leakage**, which is when test
text slips into the training data.

Training on a test sentence nudges the model’s numbers toward that sentence’s exact letter pairs.
So on the test, the model is partly marking its own homework. The score rewards it for having
trained on that exact sentence rather than for predicting new text well. That is why the three
held-out sentences are kept out of both training sets on purpose, and it is the only reason the
chart’s comparison means anything.

Careful teams go further and cut their data into three separate slices before training starts. The
model learns from the training slice, you check your choices against the **validation** slice, and
you save the test slice for one final check at the end. Together these are called a
**train/validation/test split**, and the held-out score is often called a **validation loss**,
which is where the chart’s card gets its name.

Real crawls make it hard to keep test text out, because a test sentence can be quoted on some web
page that the next crawl collects. The button **Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape** copies a
test sentence into the page in that way, so you can watch what a leak does.

## How to play with it

### Read before you filter

Set **Show** to **All lines** and read all 20 verdicts before you touch a switch. Try to spot the
four kinds of junk yourself first: the footers, the repeated fog line, the `harbour` line and the two
fragments.

At the defaults, **Strip boilerplate** and **Drop exact duplicates** are on and everything else is
off. That is why the `harbour` line, `ok.` and `yes.` are all still marked `kept`.

### Walk through the switches

Now step through the settings below in order, and write down **Cleaned held-out** at each one.
Treat it as a small experiment where you change one thing at a time and compare.

1. Turn every switch off and leave **Minimum line length** at off, so all 20 lines and 899
   **Characters** stay in. **Cleaned held-out** reads 2.365, the two lines on the chart sit on top of
   each other, and **Difference** reads +0.000, because both models trained on the same text. This is
   your starting point, which scientists call the control: the setting you compare every other one
   against.
2. Turn on **Strip boilerplate** alone, which leaves 11 lines and 580 characters. The score falls
   to 2.306, which is better.
3. Add **Drop exact duplicates**, which brings you back to the defaults with 10 lines and 516
   characters. The score is now 2.300, and **Difference** reads −0.065.
4. Add **Drop near-duplicates**, which leaves 9 lines and 451 characters. The score rises to 2.312,
   which is worse.
5. Add **Minimum line length** at 20 chars as well, and the score moves to 2.316. That is a change
   of only 0.004, which is too small to trust on its own, as Where it breaks explains.

The −0.065 is small but real, and here it is in the “equally likely characters” terms from earlier.
The raw model was about as unsure as choosing among 10.6 characters, and the cleaned one about as
unsure as choosing among 10.

The **Characters** readout counts letters, spaces and full stops the way the model receives them.
Before counting, the lab turns anything outside the model’s 30 symbols into a space and squeezes
repeated spaces into one. So `copyright 2019 all` becomes `copyright all`, and the digits do not
count.

**Try this:** switch **Strip boilerplate** off and leave **Drop exact duplicates** on, with
everything else off. You get 13 lines and 623 characters, and **Cleaned held-out** reads 2.292, a
little lower than the defaults, even though footer lines are back in the dataset.

The verdicts show why: the footer lines are exact copies of one another, so
**Drop exact duplicates** already removes every copy after the first. The 13 kept lines are the 10
from the defaults plus one copyright line, one subscribe line and one share line, which together add
107 characters.

Once each footer appears only once, it stops acting like a loud vote. Its letter pairs, such as `th`
in `this` and `on` in `on social media`, are ordinary English pairs that the weather sentences use
too. So at one copy each, the footers cost the model almost nothing. In this one run they even come
out slightly ahead, though a later section shows why a gap this small cannot be trusted.

### Leak a sentence, then clean it out

Go back to the defaults, with **Strip boilerplate** and **Drop exact duplicates** on and everything
else off, and then press **Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape**.

The button copies “the fog returns in the morning and the pattern repeats.” into the page, and that
sentence is one of the three test sentences. This is how a quoted test item can end up in a real
crawl, and four things change as soon as it lands:

- **Cleaned held-out** improves from 2.300 to 2.245, which looks like progress until you read the
  rest of the card.
- **Raw held-out** improves too, from 2.365 to 2.324, because the raw model trains on the whole page,
  copied sentence included. The leak flatters both scores.
- The contamination check under the chart, which looks for test text mixed into the training text,
  now finds a 10-word run shared with the test text. That run is the whole copied sentence, and the
  check marks it as flagged.
- A new line appears under the check, giving the cleaned model’s score on the two test sentences
  that the leak did not copy. It reads 2.422.

Now turn on **Decontaminate**, which removes training lines that overlap the test text, a step
called **decontamination**. The copied line gets the verdict `held-out overlap` and leaves the
cleaned training set, and **Cleaned held-out** goes back to 2.300.

The line about the two untouched sentences now reads 2.397, which is the cleaned model’s honest
score on them. **Difference** reads −0.024 rather than −0.065, because only the cleaned side was
decontaminated, and the raw model still trains on the copied sentence.

### Probe for recitation

Before you start, press the leak button again so it reads `off`, and turn **Decontaminate** off, so
you are back at the defaults. Then scroll down to the **Regurgitation probe** card, where the
**Context length** slider starts at 3, and move it down to 1 first.

Now step the slider up one notch at a time to 8, and at each step compare
**Copied verbatim (16+ chars)** for the two models:

- At 1 character of context, both models read 0 percent, and the samples are gibberish. The raw
  model’s sample starts `l. cit cl wn yried`, which uses real letter pairs but no real words.
- At 3, the raw model copies 52 percent of its sample and the cleaned one 39 percent. The raw
  sample now starts `yright all with warms the fog settles`, which is scraps of real lines stitched
  together.
- At 4, the raw model copies 98 percent and the cleaned one 93 percent.
- At 8, both read 100 percent, and the raw sample reads like a passage lifted straight from the
  scrape: `all rights reserved. by noon the clouds break apart`.

Keep an eye on **Footer text in the sample** as well, which counts how much of each sample comes from
the footer phrases. At 8 characters of context, footer text makes up 21 percent of the raw model’s
sample and none of the cleaned model’s.

Below the two columns, a table runs the same test at eight context lengths at once: 1 to 6, 8 and
12. It lets you compare them without moving the slider, and when the slider sits on one of those
eight values, that row is shaded.

## What to notice

### The filters overlap

Watch the badge on **Drop exact duplicates**, which reads −1 lines while **Strip boilerplate** is
on, because the only copy left for it to catch is the repeated fog sentence.

Switch boilerplate off and the same badge reads −7, because now it also catches three extra
copyright lines, two extra subscribe lines and one extra share line. The two filters were partly
removing the same lines, so you cannot judge how useful either one is by looking at it alone.

### Lines and characters tell different stories

The **Survival funnel** has one row per stage, named **Scraped**, **Strip boilerplate**,
**Length floor**, **Exact dedup**, **Near-dup** and **Decontaminate**. **Length floor** is the
**Minimum line length** slider, and dedup is short for deduplication. At the defaults, those rows
read 20, 11, 11, 10, 10, 10 lines.

Boilerplate takes 9 of the 20 lines, which is almost half of them. Yet it takes only 319 of the 899
characters, about a third, because footer lines are short.

So the same filter can look huge when you count documents and modest when you count characters.
When someone reports how much a filter removed, it matters which of the two they counted.

A stage that is switched on but removes nothing still shows a full row that reads
`removes nothing here`. That is not a mistake. Either an earlier stage already took the lines it
would have caught, or nothing on this page matches its rule, as with **Decontaminate** when no
sentence has leaked.

### Less text helped, but only up to a point

At the defaults, cleaning removed about 40 percent of the characters and the score still improved.
More text helps only when it brings something new, and the fourth copy of a footer brings nothing
that the first copy did not.

Removing more does not keep helping, though. In the walk above, adding **Drop near-duplicates** made
the score slightly worse in this run, and adding **Minimum line length** moved it by too little to
read. Neither one clearly helped, even though both delete lines that you would call junk if you
read them.

### When a change is too small to read

Now compare what a **Minimum line length** of 20 does in two different setups. With
**Drop near-duplicates** on, it moves the score from 2.312 to 2.316, which is slightly worse. With
near-duplicates off, the same setting moves the score from 2.300 to 2.296, which is slightly better.

In this one run, the same setting seems to help in one case and hurt in the other. These gaps are
only 0.004 nats, though, which Where it breaks shows is too small to trust on its own. So take this
as a warning, not a result: this lab cannot tell you whether a length floor of 20 helps.

The badge on **Drop exact duplicates** showed the firmer version of the same lesson. A filter is
always measured on whatever text the other filters left, so you cannot judge it by itself.

At 12, **Minimum line length** removes only `ok.` and `yes.`, the two fragments, and nothing else.
The shortest real sentence is 56 characters long, so the slider starts deleting real sentences at 57.

### Diversity is counted per character

**Distinct trigrams** counts how many different three-character runs the text contains. It drops
from 366 in the raw scrape to 285 after cleaning. You might guess that is because the cleaned text
is shorter, but it is not. With only **Drop exact duplicates** on, the text loses about a third of
its length and still has all 366, because every copy repeats runs the first one already had. The
drop comes from the footer lines. They hold runs like `cop` and `sub`, from `copyright` and
`subscribe`, that no real sentence uses, so removing those lines removes those runs for good.

**Repetition** is the share of all trigrams in the text that are repeats of ones already counted.
For example, `ha ha ha` has 6 trigrams once you count the spaces: `ha␣`, `a␣h`, `␣ha`, `ha␣`,
`a␣h` and `␣ha`, where ␣ stands for a space. Only 3 of them are different, so half are repeats and
its repetition is 50 percent.

Cleaning lowers **Repetition** from a **Raw repetition** of 60 percent to 45 percent. So the cleaned
text has fewer different sequences in total, yet repeats itself less.

The share of trigrams that are new rather than repeats is called **trigram diversity**, so it is the
flip side of **Repetition**: 45 percent repetition means 55 percent diversity. It measures how varied
the text is per character, not how much text there is.

### The split bar

At the defaults, the **train/validation/test split** bar on **Dataset statistics** reads `train 7`,
`val 2` and `test 1`. It is cut by whole lines, so no sentence lands in two slices.

The three held-out sentences on the chart are separate again, and the bar is only a picture of a
split, since no model trains or scores on it.

### A leak looks like progress

Picture a class where one question from Friday’s quiz gets posted in the group chat the night before.
Everyone gets that question right and the class average jumps, but the average now tells the teacher
less about what the class can do, not more.

The lab shows the model version of this, with one twist that makes the point even sharper. With the
leak on, the headline score got better, but the cleaned model got worse on the two test sentences
that were not copied. It scored 2.397 on them without the leak and 2.422 with it.

That is not wobble, because the same happens when training is rerun in eight different shuffled
orders. The lab does not show exactly why. The bigram cannot recite the copied sentence, but it has
now practised every letter pair in it, so its shared numbers lean a little toward those pairs.

That lean seems to have cost the other two sentences slightly. Either way, the better headline
number came from one copied answer, not from a better model.

### Ordinary overlaps are short; copies are long

Without the leak, the longest run of words that the cleaned training text shares with the test
sentences is two words long: “the morning”. Ordinary phrases overlap by a word or two, while a copied
sentence shares 10 words in a row.

That gap is why a check for 8 words in a row catches the copy and nothing else. A run of words in a
row is called an **n-gram**, where n stands for the number of words. Here the pieces are whole words,
while the trigrams earlier were three characters, so this check looks for shared 8-grams.

Without the leak, turn **Decontaminate** on and its badge reads −0 lines, because no ordinary line
shares 8 words with the test text.

### Recitation switches on quickly

When a model gives back a stretch of its training text after being handed the start of that stretch,
it is called **memorization**. In the probe, it switches on over a narrow range of context lengths.
Nothing is copied at 1 or 2 characters of context, and by 5 almost every character is.

Picture a friend who knows every line of one film by heart. Say one word, like “the”, and they cannot
tell which scene you mean, because dozens of lines contain it. Say eight words in a row, and only one
line in the whole film starts that way, so they can carry on word for word.

The probe works the same way, although it is only counting and understands nothing about the text.
It also counts characters, not words, so 8 characters is only a word or two. In text this small,
even that is enough to pin down one line.

Set the slider to 8 and read the note under each sample, which counts how many contexts have only
one possible next character. For the raw model, 541 of its 555 contexts are like that, so it has no
choice but to carry on with the text it was built from.

Set the slider back to 1 and the same note for the cleaned model reads 4 of 26. With one character
of context, most contexts have several possible next characters, so the samples wander instead of
copying.

### Repeated footers get recited more

Repeated lines carry more counts, so they get picked more often. At a context length of 8, footer
text makes up 21 percent of the raw model’s sample, and the raw scrape holds four copies of the
copyright line.

To see that the copies are what matter, switch **Strip boilerplate** off and leave
**Drop exact duplicates** on, so each footer survives exactly once. At a context length of 8, footer
text now makes up only 6 percent of the cleaned model’s sample.

Cleaning does not stop the cleaned model from copying its own sentences, though, because it copies
100 percent at 8 as well. Removing copies changes which text gets recited and how often, not whether
a model with enough context recites at all.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Both models really train, and both held-out scores are real, so the lab is not faking its results.
Its limits come from its small size and from the things you cannot adjust.

Twenty lines is nowhere near a real dataset, and the gaps between the good settings are only a few
thousandths of a nat. That is smaller than the wobble you would get from training on the same text
in a different order.

The order in which the model visits its training pairs is shuffled by a recipe that starts from a
fixed number, called a **seed**. The same seed always gives the same shuffle, like dealing a deck of
cards in the same order every time. The lab has no slider or switch to change it.

So every score you see comes from a single run, with no **error bar**, a measure of how much a
result would wobble if you ran it again.

Behind the scenes, the people who built the lab trained both models again with every seed from 1 to
8. Across those runs, the gap between the raw and cleaned scores always stays between 0.065 and
0.085, so you can trust that cleaning helped.

The gap between 2.292 and 2.300 points one way at some seeds and the other way at others, so you
cannot say which of those two settings is really better.

Picture two sprinters who finish a hundredth of a second apart, then swap places when they race
again. That tiny gap tells you nothing about who is faster. A gap counts as evidence only when it is
bigger than the usual wobble, and the gap between the two best settings here is not.

The lab’s filters and readouts are also cruder than they look, in several ways:

- The 20 lines are a fixed teaching page, not a real crawl, and you cannot paste in a page of your
  own.
- The boilerplate filter is a list of five English phrases, so it would miss a footer worded any
  other way.
- The near-duplicate test compares single short lines, while real near-duplicate checks compare
  whole documents that can be thousands of words long. Its 0.7 cut-off is also fixed, with no
  control to change it.
- That test compares every pair of lines directly, which works only because there are 20 of them.
  The **MinHash** number is computed for real, but the filter itself does not use it.
- **Trigram diversity** counts letter sequences, so it calls a page varied for using many of them,
  not for covering many topics.
- The split bar on **Dataset statistics** never trains or scores a model, so the only held-out score
  on the page is the three-sentence passage on the chart.

The model on the chart predicts from one character, so it cannot recite long passages. All the
chart can show is the model’s limited room being spent on the wrong patterns.

The **Regurgitation probe** uses a table of counts built from only 516 to 899 characters. In so
little text, almost every long context is always followed by the same character, so reciting is
certain. A real model is far larger, picks up general patterns where this table cannot, and
memorizes only part of what it was trained on.

The probe also starts each sample from the opening characters of its training text. That mirrors
how an attempt to pull text out of a real model starts from a known beginning. In this tiny table,
starting from another point inside the training text would copy about as much, because almost every
long context has only one continuation. A prompt that is not taken from the training text at all
gives a model less to copy, so it usually copies less.

The probe’s own seed is fixed as well, so each context length always prints the same text.

**A common mix-up:** it is tempting to think that deleting data is what helped, but it is not.
Removing about 40 percent of this scrape improved the score, but **Drop near-duplicates** removes
even more and makes the score worse. Smaller is not automatically better, and neither is less
repeated: the `harbour` line is a near-repeat, yet removing it did not help here. What tells you
whether a change helped is the held-out score, not the character count and not which filter sounds
more responsible.

### Where real pipelines struggle

Real pipelines face problems that this lab is too small to show, and each one is a place where the
real mechanism can fail.

**Finding copies at web scale:** checking for exact copies with hashes still works across the whole
web, but it misses near-copies. Nobody can compare every pair among billions of documents, so
near-copy search uses MinHash, or a similar method called SimHash, along with a sorting trick called
locality-sensitive hashing.

That trick only compares documents whose signatures land in the same group, which saves most of the
work. Even so, near-copy search is one of the most expensive steps in the whole pipeline.

**Judging quality:** real boilerplate removal uses a program trained to pick the main text out of a
page, not a short list of phrases. Quality filtering usually means a **classifier**, a program that
sorts documents into groups.

A classifier scores each document by how much it looks like a reference collection, so whatever that
collection leans toward, the filter leans toward too. If the reference is mostly one style of
writing, other styles get pushed toward the bottom.

Separate steps handle which language a page is in, personal data, licences and toxic text. Each of
these is a policy decision as much as a technical one.

**Keeping test text out:** teams search for long runs of words shared with **benchmarks**, the
standard public tests used to compare models, and GPT-3’s report used runs 13 words long. Leaks
still happen, because benchmark questions get quoted in posts that the next crawl collects, and
reworded copies slip past a search for exact runs. In practice, a held-out set stays held out only
until someone publishes it.

**Counting in tokens:** near-copy and contamination checks run on text that has been tidied up first,
for example by making it all lowercase. Real models split text into tokens in different ways: some
use single characters like this lab, while others use whole words or pieces of words, such as `harb`
plus `our`.

So the same page can count as 900 tokens with one method and 200 with another. A funnel reported in
tokens only makes sense for the method that counted them.

**Memorizing a single copy:** removing copies lowers how often a model recites its training text,
but a large model can still memorize text that appears only once. The next section shows why that
matters for names, phone numbers and email addresses.

## Going deeper

### Choosing what gets seen twice

Filtering decides what is allowed into a dataset, while the **data mixture** decides what share of
the training each source gets. Giving a small, high-quality source a few extra passes is normal
practice, so after deduplication removes the accidental copies, teams put some repetition back on
purpose. The rule is not “duplicates are bad” but “you should be the one choosing which text gets
seen twice”.

Picture making a road-trip playlist: first you delete the songs that got added three times by
accident, and then you choose to play your two favourites twice. The second step is still
repetition, but it is repetition you chose, and that is the difference between an accidental
duplicate and a mixture.

### What a held-out score settles

**Held-out loss** answers one question well: did this change help the model predict text like the
held-out sample? It cannot tell you whether that sample looks like the text you actually care about.

A filter that strips out blocks of computer code might look fine when you score on ordinary sentences
and still ruin the model’s results on a coding test. That is why real pipelines score against
several held-out sets and expect them to disagree.

### Why the split has three parts

You check the validation slice again and again while you tune your choices, so it slowly stops being
truly held out. Picture trying 50 different filter settings and keeping whichever scores best on the
validation slice.

Part of the winner’s lead is luck that happens to suit those few sentences, like the sprinters’
wobble. The more settings you try, the more your pick is tuned to the validation slice itself, so its
score starts to flatter you. That is why the test slice stays untouched until one final check.

Cutting the **train/validation/test split** by whole document rather than by sentence is what makes
the whole thing work. Two sentences from the same page share so much wording that splitting a page
between training and testing would leak one into the other.

### Memorization and regurgitation

**Memorization** is a model giving back a stretch of its training text when it is handed the start
of that stretch. Carlini and colleagues measured it across several families of models and found it
grows steadily with three things:

- the model’s capacity, meaning how big it is;
- how many times an example was duplicated in its training data;
- how much context the prompt supplies, the prompt being the text you hand the model to start it
  off.

Each time one of these doubles, the amount memorized rises by roughly the same step. The probe’s
slider is closest to the third one, though raising it also makes the table bigger, so it changes the
model’s size as well.

Of the three, duplication is the one that the people building a dataset can control. Lee and
colleagues found that over 1 percent of what models wrote without any prompt was copied word for
word from their training data. These were models trained on standard datasets, and in C4 the team
found one 61-word sentence repeated over 60,000 times.

Training on their deduplicated data produced models that gave out memorized text ten times less
often, which is a big improvement but not the whole answer.

In another study, Carlini and colleagues got GPT-2 to write out hundreds of word-for-word passages
from its training text, including names, phone numbers and email addresses. Each of those passages
appeared in only one document, and the team also found that larger models were more vulnerable.

### Data governance: personal data, licences, consent

Three questions about a dataset cannot be answered by reading its text: may this person’s details be
used, may this text be reused, and did its owners agree? So no filter in this lab can answer them.
Keeping records and rules about questions like these, such as where each document came from and what
may be done with it, is called **data governance**.

- **Personal data** is text that identifies a person, such as a name, phone number or email
  address. Spotting it is a detection problem that a filter can help with, while deciding whether
  it may be used is a legal one. The GPT-2 result above shows why even one appearance matters.
- **Licences** are the rules that say who may reuse a piece of text, and how. An audit checked more
  than 1,800 text datasets on the websites that host them. It found licence information missing in
  over 70 percent of cases. In a separate count, it found licences labelled wrongly in over 50
  percent, so the two figures measure different things and do not add up to one whole.
- **Consent** is about whether the owners of a text agreed to its use. Websites state their wishes
  in their terms of service and in a file called `robots.txt`, which tells automatic collectors what
  they may and may not take. A 2024 audit of 14,000 web domains, which are website names, found
  these restrictions rose sharply in a single year. If they were respected, over 5 percent of all
  the text in C4 would now be fully blocked by `robots.txt` files. The audit also found that 45
  percent of C4 is restricted by the sites’ terms of service.

Rules are arriving, too, and one of them is a European Union law called the EU AI Act. It covers
models built for many different tasks, called general-purpose AI models, and it asks their makers to
keep a policy for following EU copyright law.

Some rights holders, meaning the people who own the copyright in a piece of writing, have stated
that their work may not be used this way. This is called reserving their rights. The makers’ policy has to
cover these cases, and the law also asks them to publish a detailed enough summary of what they
trained on.

Both of these duties need a record kept alongside the data, not a better filter.

### Toy versus real

- **Scale:** the lab’s scrape is 20 lines and 899 characters, and its most repeated line, the
  copyright footer, appears four times. Lee and colleagues found one 61-word sentence repeated over
  60,000 times in C4.
- **Overlap checks:** the lab flags an 8-word run shared with the test text, while GPT-3’s report
  flagged any test example that shared a 13-word run with its training data. Lee and colleagues
  also found that over 4 percent of the validation sets of standard datasets overlapped with their
  training data.
- **Reciting:** the probe copies everything at 8 characters of context because its text is tiny. In
  the models Lee and colleagues trained, over 1 percent of what they wrote without a prompt was
  copied word for word. Carlini and colleagues also found that memorization grows as models get
  bigger, so a real model’s size affects how much it recites.
- **What real pipelines add:** language detection, trained quality classifiers, detection of personal
  data, records of licences and opt-outs, and a log of why each document was dropped.
