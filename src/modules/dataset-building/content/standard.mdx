## The big picture

This module is about the text a model trains on: building it from a web scrape by removing site
boilerplate, copies, and evaluation text, then checking whether the result is better. The
question it answers is how to tell a better dataset from one that is merely smaller.

*Anatomy of a training run* followed one character model from batch to checkpoint. Here that loop
stays fixed and only the training text changes, so **held-out loss**, cross-entropy on text that
never entered training, is the verdict on every change. The last part asks when a model recites
its training text, and which questions about a dataset no filter can answer.

### Where it shows up

The first three are pipeline stages that run on real crawls at far larger scale, and the lab has
small versions of them. Mixture and governance are decisions and records kept around the data,
not filters, and have no control here.

- **Deduplication.** Exact hashing first, then MinHash signatures, short fingerprints whose
  agreement estimates how much two documents overlap, to find near-copies at web scale.
- **Filtering.** Trained boilerplate extractors, quality filtering that usually uses a
  classifier, and further stages for language, personal data, licensing, and toxicity.
- **Decontamination.** Training documents that share a long word n-gram with a benchmark item are
  removed before training.
- **Mixture and governance.** A **data mixture** sets each source's share of the training budget,
  and the EU AI Act asks providers of general-purpose AI models to publish a summary of their
  training content.

### Why it matters

Raw scraped text is full of site furniture and copies, and a model with a fixed parameter budget
spends part of it fitting them. Yet a filter that deletes junk can still cost accuracy, so a
dataset's size says nothing on its own about its quality. Without held-out text kept out of
training, no dataset change has a verdict you can trust.

Duplication also shapes what a model recites, since memorization grows with the number of times
an example was duplicated. Deduplication is not enough, though. Text seen once can still be
extracted, and whether a dataset is allowed to hold a person's details, a licensed work, or an
opted-out site's pages is not visible in the text, so no filter can settle it.

### What you will be able to explain

- The failure modes a cleaning pipeline removes: boilerplate, exact repeats, near-duplicates,
  low-signal fragments, and text that overlaps an evaluation set, and which filter catches each.
- Why held-out text must stay separate from every training set, and what **leakage**, evaluation
  text inside training data, does to a held-out score.
- How to judge a dataset change by its validation loss rather than its size, and when a gap is
  too small to resolve.
- When a model recites its training text verbatim, and why personal data, licences, and consent
  need checks that deduplication does not give.

### What the lab shows

The scrape is fixed: 20 hard-coded lines, each given one verdict, which **Show** filters to
**All lines** or **Kept only**. **Cleaning stages** holds four switches, **Strip boilerplate**,
**Drop exact duplicates**, **Drop near-duplicates**, and **Decontaminate**, plus a **Minimum line
length** slider. Each change updates the verdicts, the **Survival funnel**, and **Dataset
statistics**.

The comparison is measured. The same character bigram, which predicts each character from the
one before it, is trained for 25 epochs on the raw scrape and again on the kept lines, and both
are scored on three held-out sentences. **Raw held-out** and **Cleaned held-out** are real
cross-entropy, and **Difference** is their gap, all from one fixed shuffle seed, so none carries
an error bar.

The key experiment: find which filters lower **Cleaned held-out**, then press **Leak a held-out
sentence into the scrape** and watch the score improve for the wrong reason until
**Decontaminate** removes the copy.

The **Regurgitation probe** swaps in two tables of counts instead of trained networks, one built
from the raw scrape and one from the kept lines, and **Context length** sets how many characters
each conditions on. Each model writes one fixed-seed, 300-character sample that starts from its
training text's opening characters, and **Copied verbatim** reports how much of that sample sits
in runs of 16 or more characters copied from the training text.

Some parts are simplified. Boilerplate detection is five hard-coded phrases, the near-duplicate
stage compares character-trigram sets exactly while the MinHash note only estimates that
similarity, and the train/validation/test split bar is a display that neither trains nor scores a
model.

## What it is

A cleaning pipeline is a sequence of filters, each deleting documents by a stated rule. This
lab holds 20 lines from a small scrape. Every line gets exactly one verdict — kept,
boilerplate, exact repeat, near-duplicate, or too short — and **Show** switches between
**All lines** and **Kept only** so you can read the verdicts before trusting them.

The scrape carries four distinct kinds of junk, all of which occur in real crawls:

- **Boilerplate**: copyright, subscribe, and share lines that belong to the site rather than to
  any document. Nine of the 20 lines are this.
- An exact repeat of a real sentence, which **exact deduplication** catches.
- A spelling variant — one line writes `harbour` where an earlier line writes `harbor`. Same
  content, different bytes, so only a **near-duplicate** test finds it.
- Two fragments, `ok.` and `yes.`, carrying almost no signal for their length.

Then the lab does the part that arguments about data usually skip. It trains the same character
bigram model twice — once on all 20 lines joined, once on the kept lines — for 25 epochs each,
and scores both on 194 characters of text in neither training set. Those scores are
**Raw held-out** and **Cleaned held-out**, and **Difference** is the only claim it makes. The
**Survival funnel** counts what each stage removed, in lines and in encoded characters.

A last card, **Regurgitation probe**, changes the model. It builds a table of counts from the
raw scrape and from the cleaned set, lets each write 300 characters, and measures how much of
the output is a verbatim copy. The bigram that the rest of the track trains cannot do that, and
the probe shows what changes when the model sees more than one character of context.

## Why it’s here

Repetition is not neutral. Every training pair produces a gradient, so a line appearing four
times pulls the weights four times as hard. The model has 900 parameters to spend, and capacity
spent predicting `all rights reserved` is capacity not spent on anything else. That is the
mechanism behind **data quality**, a phrase that means nothing on its own.

Held-out text is here for a second reason: **leakage**. If evaluation text also sits in the
training data, the score measures recall of a memorized string rather than generalization, and
it improves without the model improving. The passage scored here is outside both training sets
on purpose, which is why the comparison carries information at all — until you press
**Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape**.

## How to play with it

Set **Show** to **All lines** and read all 20 verdicts before touching a filter. Then walk the
settings and record **Cleaned held-out** at each stop:

- All filters off: 20 lines, 899 **Characters**, 2.365 nats per token. The curves lie on top of
  each other and **Difference** reads +0.000. That is the control condition.
- **Strip boilerplate** only: 11 lines, 580 characters, 2.306.
- Boilerplate plus **Drop exact duplicates**, the defaults: 10 lines, 516 characters, 2.300,
  and **Difference** reads −0.065.
- Add **Drop near-duplicates**: 9 lines, 451 characters, 2.312. Worse.
- Add **Minimum line length** at 20 chars: 2.316. Worse again.
- Now turn **Strip boilerplate** off and leave only **Drop exact duplicates**: 13 lines, 623
  characters, 2.292 — the best score available in this lab.

That last result is not a trick. The repeated boilerplate lines are byte-identical, so
**exact deduplication** already removes all but one copy of each. The extra 107 characters are those
three surviving footer lines; at one copy each, their ordinary English letter pairs cost the model
nothing measurable here.

Finally, return to the defaults and press **Leak a held-out sentence into the scrape**. It
copies "the fog returns in the morning and the pattern repeats." into the page. **Cleaned
held-out** improves from 2.300 to 2.245, and the contamination check flags a 10-word run shared
with the held-out text. Turn on **Decontaminate** and the score returns to 2.300.

Last, open **Regurgitation probe** and drag **Context length** from 1 to 8. At 1 character,
**Copied verbatim** reads 0 percent for both models and the samples are gibberish. At 3 the raw
model copies 52 percent of its sample and the cleaned one 39 percent. At 4 it is 98 and 93
percent, and at 8 both read 100 percent.

The raw sample at 8 is also 21 percent footer text, the `copyright` and `subscribe` lines; the
cleaned sample has none.

## What to notice

- Watch the badge on **Drop exact duplicates**. With **Strip boilerplate** on it reads −1
  lines; with boilerplate off it reads −7. The filters overlap heavily, so neither one's value
  can be judged in isolation.
- The **Survival funnel** reads 20, 11, 11, 10, 10, 10 lines and 899 to 516 characters at the
  defaults. Boilerplate removes 9 of 20 lines but only 319 characters: counting documents and
  counting tokens tell different stories.
- Cleaning removes about 40 percent of the characters and the held-out score improves: size helps
  only when the added text carries new signal. But the direction is not automatic.
  **Drop near-duplicates** and **Minimum line length** each cost a little accuracy here, even
  though both remove text a human would call junk.
- The filters are not independent. **Minimum line length** at 20 makes the score slightly worse
  when **Drop near-duplicates** is on (2.312 to 2.316) and slightly better when it is off
  (2.300 to 2.296). A filter's effect depends on which others are already running.
- **Distinct trigrams** falls from 366 raw to 285 cleaned while **Repetition** falls from a
  **Raw repetition** of 60 percent to 45 percent. Fewer distinct sequences and less repetition
  at once: **trigram diversity** measures signal per character, not signal in total.
- The **train/validation/test split** bar reads 7 / 2 / 1 lines at the defaults, drawn by line
  so no sentence lands in two slices. The scored held-out passage is separate again.
- **Leakage** looks like progress. With the leak on, the cleaned model's loss on the two held-out
  sentences that were not copied gets worse, 2.397 to 2.422, while the headline score improves.
  The same happens at every seed from 1 to 8.
- Without the leak, the longest word run shared with the held-out text is two words, "the
  morning". Ordinary phrases are short and a copied sentence is long, which is why an 8-word
  **decontamination** check catches the leak and nothing else.
- **Memorization** switches on over a narrow range of context. Nothing is copied at 1 or 2
  characters of context, and at 5 almost every character is. Of the contexts the raw model has
  seen at length 8, 541 of 555 have only one possible next character, so it has no choice but to
  continue the text it saw.
- The duplicated footer is what the raw model recites first. Repeated lines carry more counts, so
  they are sampled more often: 21 percent of the raw sample at length 8, against none of the
  cleaned one. But cleaning does not stop the cleaned model copying its own sentences.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Both models are trained for real and both scores are real cross-entropy on text
neither model saw. The limits are size and what is not adjustable. Twenty lines is not a corpus,
and the gaps between the good settings are a few thousandths of a nat — smaller than the spread
a different shuffle order would produce.

The shuffle seed is fixed and has no control, so every number arrives without an error bar.
Across seeds 1 to 8 the raw-to-cleaned gap ranges from 0.065 to 0.085, so treat it as real; the
gap between 2.292 and 2.300 changes sign between seeds, so treat it as unresolved.

The filters are cruder than they look: boilerplate detection is five hard-coded substrings, and
the near-duplicate test is exact character-trigram Jaccard at 0.7 applied to single lines rather
than documents. The **MinHash** note computes a real 64-hash estimate of the harbour pair (0.922
against an exact 0.915), but the stage itself compares sets directly, which only works because
there are twenty lines.

The validation chart's model is a bigram over 30 characters, so it cannot show verbatim
regurgitation of long passages. It can only show capacity being spent in the wrong place. The
**Regurgitation probe** uses a different model for that: a table of counts over 516 to 899
characters, where almost every long context is unique, so reciting is guaranteed. A real model
generalizes where this table cannot, and memorizes only part of what it saw.

**In real systems.** Exact hashing still runs at web scale, but it misses near-copies, and
comparing every pair of billions of documents is impossible. So near-duplicate search uses
MinHash or SimHash signatures with locality-sensitive hashing, which only compares documents
whose signatures collide, and it is one of the most expensive stages in the pipeline.

Boilerplate removal is a trained extractor, not a substring list. Quality filtering usually
means a classifier scored against a reference corpus, which imports that reference's biases.
Separate stages handle language identification, personal data, licensing, and toxicity, each a
policy decision as much as an engineering one.

**Leakage** is harder there too. Benchmark contamination is checked by n-gram overlap against
every evaluation set (GPT-3's report used 13-grams) and still happens, because benchmark items
get quoted in posts that the next crawl collects, and paraphrases slip past exact n-gram
matching. A held-out set is held out until someone publishes it.

**Tokenization** interacts with all of this. Near-duplicate and contamination checks run on
normalized text, and the same page can yield very different token counts under different
tokenizers, so a funnel reported in tokens is specific to the tokenizer that counted it.

## Going deeper

**Mixture weights are the other half of the job.** Filtering decides what is admissible; the
**data mixture** decides what fraction of the training budget each source receives. Upsampling a
small high-quality source a few times is standard practice, so controlled duplication gets
reintroduced deliberately after deduplication removed the accidental kind. The rule is not
"duplicates are bad" but "you should be the one choosing which text gets seen twice".

**What a held-out score settles.** **Held-out loss** answers one question well: did this
dataset change help the model predict text like the held-out sample? It cannot tell you whether
that sample is the text you care about. A step that strips code blocks looks fine on prose and
disastrous on a coding benchmark, which is why production pipelines score against several
held-out sets and expect them to disagree.

**Why the split has three parts.** Validation is touched repeatedly while you tune, so it
slowly stops being held out — every decision fits it a little. The test slice is what you keep
for a single measurement at the end. Drawing the **train/validation/test split** by document
rather than by sentence is what makes any of it work, because two sentences from one page share
enough phrasing to leak across a sentence-level split.

### Memorization and regurgitation

**Memorization** is a model reproducing a stretch of its training text when given a prefix of
it. Carlini and colleagues measured it across model families and found it grows log-linearly
with model capacity, with the number of times an example was duplicated, and with the number of
tokens of context used to prompt the model (arXiv 2202.07646). The probe isolates the third.

Duplication is the part a pipeline controls. Lee and colleagues found that over 1 percent of the
unprompted output of language models trained on standard datasets was copied verbatim from the
training data, and that one 61-word sentence was repeated over 60,000 times in C4. Their
deduplication produced models that emitted memorized text ten times less frequently (arXiv
2107.06499).

Deduplication is not enough. Carlini and colleagues extracted hundreds of verbatim sequences from
GPT-2, including names, phone numbers, and email addresses, even though each sequence appeared in
just one document, and they found larger models more vulnerable (arXiv 2012.07805).

### Data governance: personal data, licences, consent

Three questions about a dataset cannot be answered by reading its text, so no filter in this lab
can answer them.

- **Personal data.** Whether a document names a person is a detection problem, and whether it may
  be used is a legal one. The extraction result above is why a single occurrence still matters.
- **Licences.** An audit of more than 1,800 text datasets found licence information omitted in
  over 70 percent of cases on the hosting sites it examined, and miscategorized in over 50
  percent (Longpre et al., arXiv 2310.16787).
- **Consent.** Websites state their preferences in terms of service and `robots.txt`. A 2024 audit
  of 14,000 web domains found that restrictions rose enough in one year that, if respected, over
  5 percent of all tokens in C4 are fully restricted, and 45 percent by terms of service
  (Longpre et al., arXiv 2407.14933).

Rules are arriving. Article 53(1) of the EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, asks providers of
general-purpose AI models to keep a policy for complying with Union copyright law, including
rights reservations, and to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for
training. Each of these needs a record kept with the data, not a better filter.

### Toy versus real

- **Scale.** The lab's scrape is 20 lines and 899 characters, and its most repeated line is the
  copyright footer, four times. Lee et al. found one 61-word sentence repeated over 60,000 times
  in C4.
- **Overlap checks.** The lab flags an 8-word run shared with the held-out text. GPT-3's report
  flagged any test example sharing a 13-gram with its pretraining data (Brown et al., arXiv
  2005.14165). Lee et al. found train-test overlap affecting over 4 percent of the validation
  sets of standard datasets.
- **Recitation.** The probe copies everything at 8 characters of context because the text is
  tiny. In the models Lee et al. trained, over 1 percent of unprompted output was copied
  verbatim, and Carlini et al. found memorization grows with model capacity.
- **What real pipelines add.** Language identification, trained quality classifiers, personal-data
  detection, licence and opt-out records, and a log of why each document was dropped.
