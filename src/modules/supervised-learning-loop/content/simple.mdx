## The big picture

This module is about how a program learns a rule from examples that already come with the right answers. The lab gives you a small table of sixteen made-up apartments. For each one you know the rooms and the color. You also know whether it is near a park and whether its price is high or low. The goal is a rule that can guess the price of an apartment it has never seen.

Learning from examples that carry their answers is called **supervised learning**. The answers guide the learning the way an answer key helps a student check homework. Getting from a table to a rule you can trust takes more than one step, though. It is a loop of separate stages, and this lab lets you run them in order on one problem:

1. Turn each row of the table into a short list of numbers, because a rule can only do arithmetic. This stage is called encoding.
2. Hide four of the rows so they can serve as a fair check at the end. The lab calls the hidden rows the holdout, and this stage is called the split.
3. Pick the kind of rule you will search through, from a straight line to a chain of yes-or-no questions. This is the family of rules.
4. Pick a way to measure how wrong a rule is. This mistake score is called the loss, and lower is better.
5. Search for the rule with the lowest loss you can find on the rows you kept. Adjusting a rule to match those rows as well as it can is called fitting it, or the fit.
6. Score the finished rule on the four hidden rows without changing it at all.

### Where you have met it

You have met this loop whenever an app makes a guess it learned from examples with answers. An email filter learns from messages that people marked as spam. A photo app learns to spot faces from pictures that people labeled. The rule a program like that learns is often called a **model**.

Behind each of those models, someone made five choices. They chose how to turn the data into numbers and which rows to hide. They also chose what kind of rule to allow, what counts as a mistake, and how to search. Later labs keep these same stages. The rule grows into a stack of layers with far more numbers to adjust.

### Why it matters

A rule can get almost every practice example right and still fail on new ones. Think of a student who memorizes one practice sheet and then struggles on the real test. Without hidden rows, you could not tell learning from memorizing, because both look alike on the practice rows.

Even a fair check has a limit. Hidden rows only tell you how the rule does on rows made the same way as the training rows. If the world that makes the rows changes, a good rule can start getting most answers wrong. The rule itself has not changed at all.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- The stages of the loop in order. They are encoding, the split, the family of rules, the loss, the search and a reading on hidden rows.
- Why you should pick the hidden rows by shuffling. Also what the training, validation and test piles are each for.
- How changing only one stage can move the reading on the hidden rows. The stage might be the encoding, the algorithm or the loss, and every other stage stays the same.
- Why a mistake score can fall on the practice rows while it climbs on the hidden rows. It means the rule has so much freedom that it memorizes.
- Why hidden rows from a different source can wreck an unchanged rule. A reading on hidden rows only speaks for rows like the training rows.

## What it is

### One table, one question

Each of the sixteen apartments has a name, such as Alder 12 or Rowan 9. It also has three facts. It has 1 to 4 rooms and a color of red, blue or green. It also has a yes or no for whether it sits near a park. Each apartment carries a price **label**, which is the right answer the rule should learn. A label of 1 means high priced and 0 means low priced. The lab's tables show it in the column marked y.

The lab's authors chose a pattern for the labels. An apartment is high priced if it has 3 or more rooms, or 2 rooms and a park. Half the table, 8 of the 16 apartments, ends up high priced, and the other half ends up low. One apartment breaks the pattern on purpose. Rowan 9 has 3 rooms and a park but is labeled low. This planted exception shows what happens when a rule memorizes a row instead of following the pattern.

### Practice questions and a mock exam

Here is a picture to carry through the whole lesson. Imagine the sixteen apartments as sixteen practice questions for a test, each with its answer printed on the back. Before you study, you put four of them aside face down as a mock exam. You promise not to peek.

You study only the other twelve. First you decide how to copy each question into your notes. You also decide what kind of rule of thumb to allow. Then you check your answers against the backs of the cards. You adjust your rule until you get as few wrong as you can. At the end you turn over the four hidden questions and mark them, without changing your rule. That mark is your fairest guess at how you will do on the real test.

Each part of that picture is one stage of the loop. The rest of this section walks through them in order. The picture stops matching in two places. A student understands what the questions are about, but the fit does not. It adjusts a handful of numbers, or picks a few yes-or-no questions, until a mistake score goes down. It has no idea what an apartment is. A sensible student would also study the mock exam mistakes afterward. In this loop, the hidden rows never go back into the fit. Once they are used for learning, they stop being a fair check.

### Encode: turning a row into numbers

A rule can only do arithmetic. So the first stage turns each table row into a short list of numbers. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**, which the lab writes as x. Each number in it is called a **feature**. Choosing which features to include, and how to write each one as a number, is called the **encoding**.

The **Encoding** switch has three settings. Alder 12 has 2 rooms, is red and is near a park, so it shows how each setting works:

- `rooms + park` keeps two numbers and drops color. Alder 12 becomes (2, 1), where the 1 means "yes, near a park".
- `integer codes` puts color in the middle as one number. Red is 1, blue is 2 and green is 3. Alder 12 becomes (2, 1, 1). That means 2 rooms, color code 1 for red, and 1 for the park.
- `one-hot` gives each color its own yes-or-no slot, placed between rooms and park in the order red, blue, green. Alder 12 becomes (2, 1, 0, 0, 1). That means 2 rooms, a 1 in the red slot, 0 for blue and green, and the park's 1.

The lab's authors made up the red, blue, green order in `integer codes`, and nobody measured it. That matters because a rule does arithmetic with the code. To the rule, blue's 2 sits halfway between red's 1 and green's 3. Colors have no such order, so the encoding has invented a pattern. The `one-hot` setting avoids this by giving each color its own yes or no.

Encoding decides what a rule can even see. With `rooms + park`, color is invisible, so no rule can use it, whether it would help or hurt.

### Split: hiding four rows

The second stage divides the rows into two piles. Twelve apartments form the **training set**, which is the pile the rule may learn from. The other four form the **holdout**. These rows stay out of the fit completely and get scored only at the end. In this lab the holdout is Laurel 2, Maple 10, Oak 14 and Pine 6.

Both piles keep the table's even mix. The training set has 6 high-priced and 6 low-priced apartments, and the holdout has 2 of each. On the **Decision view** card, training apartments are filled circles and holdout apartments are open circles. Click the **Split** chip on the **Supervised loop** card. The readout **In the fit?** then says yes or no for the apartment you have selected.

The rule for the holdout is strict. No step of the search and no question in a tree may ever look at a holdout row. Its score is a reading taken after the fit, and the fit cannot aim at it.

### Hypothesis: choosing a family of rules

Next you choose what kind of rule to search through. The whole family of rules you are willing to consider is called the **hypothesis class**. The **Algorithm** switch picks one of three:

- `linear` gives each feature a **weight**, which is a number the feature gets multiplied by. Then it adds up the results with one extra number called the **bias**. The lab writes this score as `w·x+b`, where w is the list of weights and b is the bias. With **Loss** on `MSE`, explained a little further on, the rule says high priced when that score reaches 0.5.
- `logistic` works out the same kind of score and then squashes it with a curve called the **sigmoid**, written σ. The sigmoid turns any score into a chance between 0 and 1. A score of 0 becomes exactly 0.5. Large positive scores come out close to 1, and large negative scores come out close to 0.
- `tree` asks a short chain of yes-or-no questions, and each question is about one feature. One example is whether the rooms are below 1.5. The answers lead to a final group called a **leaf**. The chance of high price there is the share of training apartments in that leaf that are high priced.

The lab writes the logistic rule as `P(y=1|x) = σ(w·x+b)`. Here `P(y=1|x)` stands for the chance that the label y is 1, or high priced, for vector x. Changing the hypothesis class gives you a different search over the same table. It does not give you a different table.

The place where a rule switches from saying low to saying high is called the **decision boundary**. On the **Decision view** card, rooms run from left to right and park runs from bottom to top. Park is only ever yes or no, so the apartments sit on two flat rails. "No park" runs along the bottom and "park" runs along the top.

For `logistic`, the boundary is the solid line where the chance is exactly 50 percent. The shading shows the chance everywhere else on the map. A tree draws dashed cuts instead. Each cut runs straight across or straight up and down, because each question covers one feature.

Here is a boundary you can read. Use the settings the lab opens on: `rooms + park`, `logistic` and `log-loss`. Run the fit to its last step. The solid line crosses the top rail, where apartments have a park, at about 1.8 rooms. It crosses the bottom rail at about 2.5 rooms. In words, 2 rooms is enough for the high side when there is a park. Without a park, an apartment needs 3. That is the same pattern the labels were written with. The fit found it from twelve rows and a loss, and nobody drew the line by hand.

### Fit, part one: a score for mistakes

To search for a good rule, you need one number for how wrong it is on the training rows. That number is called the **loss**, and the fit's whole job is to push it down. The **Loss** switch offers two kinds.

The `MSE` setting, short for mean squared error, measures how far each prediction lands from the right answer. It squares that miss and averages the results over the rows. Squaring makes big misses cost far more than small ones:

- A miss of 0.2 costs 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.04.
- A miss of 0.8 costs 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64, which is sixteen times as much.

The `log-loss` setting looks only at the chance the rule gave to the right answer. It charges a penalty that grows faster and faster as that chance shrinks. This penalty is called **log-loss**. Its name comes from logarithms, a kind of math you do not need here. The lab works it out for you. What matters is the shape of the penalty. You can see it on real rows from the lab's opening fit:

- **Juniper 6:** This apartment is high priced and got a 91 percent chance of high. Its penalty is about 0.10.
- **Alder 12:** The rule gave its right answer a 55 percent chance, so its penalty is about 0.59.
- **A coin-flip guess:** Giving every row a 50 percent chance costs about 0.69 per row.
- **Rowan 9:** Its right answer, low, got only about 22 percent, so its penalty is about 1.49.
- **A chance near 0 percent:** Almost 0 percent on the right answer would cost an endless amount. The lab caps the penalty at about 21 per row.

The coin-flip value of 0.69 is worth remembering. A log-loss above it means the rule does worse than answering 0.5 for every row. Answering 0.5 every time is the same as having no rule at all.

### Fit, part two: searching for the lowest loss

With a loss chosen, the search can begin. The process that hunts through the hypothesis class for the lowest training loss it can find is called **optimization**. Running it is the fit.

For `linear` and `logistic`, the lab starts with every weight and the bias at 0. With `logistic`, a score of 0 gives every apartment the same 0.5 chance. So the training loss starts at the coin-flip value of 0.69. At each step, the lab looks at all twelve training rows at once. It works out which way to nudge each weight so the loss falls fastest. The lab finds that direction from the **gradient**. It measures how fast the loss would change as each weight moves. For that reason, each nudge is called a gradient step.

The **Fit step** slider runs from 0 to 48 and lets you scrub through those steps. At the opening settings, the training loss is 0.69 before the first step. Ten steps later it has dropped to about 0.57, and by the last step it reaches 0.45. It is still creeping down a tiny amount each step there, but the lab stops after 48 steps.

A tree searches in a different way. It takes no gradient steps and grows one level of questions at a time. At each level it picks the question that best separates high from low among the training rows. It judges this by a score of how mixed each group is. With **Algorithm** on `tree`, the **Fit step** slider is relabeled **Grown depth**. Dragging it down also lowers **Tree depth**, so use the **Tree depth** slider to go back up.

### Worked example: one apartment, start to finish

The **Worked example** card shows the whole calculation for the apartment you have selected. It uses the same arithmetic that the loss curve averages. At the opening settings with Alder 12 selected, the card shows three numbers the fit settled on:

- **w_rooms:** This is the weight that the number of rooms gets multiplied by, and it is 1.031.
- **w_park:** This is the weight that the park answer gets multiplied by, and it is 0.709.
- **b:** This is the bias that gets added on at the end, and it is −2.561.

Alder 12's vector is (2, 1), so its score is `2 × 1.031 + 1 × 0.709 − 2.561`, which comes to 0.210. The sigmoid squashes that small positive score into a chance of 0.552. That is above 0.5, so the rule calls Alder 12 high priced. It is only about 55 percent sure, though. Alder 12 really is high priced, so its log-loss is 0.594. The card labels this number ℓ.

Now click Rowan 9 in the apartment table. Its vector, (3, 1), gives a higher score and a 0.776 chance of being high priced. Rowan 9 is labeled low, so the chance on its right answer is 1 − 0.776, or about 22 percent. Its penalty comes to 1.494, the largest of all twelve training rows. That makes sense for the one row that breaks the pattern.

Then click Laurel 2, a holdout row with 2 rooms and no park. It gets a 0.378 chance of high, so the rule correctly calls it low. Its penalty is 0.474. The card adds a note that the fit scored this row afterward and never used it.

Averaging ℓ over the twelve training rows gives **L train**, which is 0.45 here. Averaging it over the four holdout rows gives **L holdout**, which is 0.31.

### Holdout: read the score, never fit it

The last stage reads the finished rule's score on the four holdout rows. What you care about is how the rule does on rows it has never seen. Doing well on new rows from the same source as the training rows is called **generalization**. The holdout score is the only estimate of it this lab can compute. A small training loss says nothing about new rows on its own.

The **Loss over the fit** card gives you **L holdout**, the average log-loss on the four hidden rows. It also gives you **Holdout acc**, the share of those rows the rule gets right. At the opening settings, **Holdout acc** reads 100 percent, which is 4 of 4. **Train acc** reads 92 percent. The rule gets 11 of the 12 training rows right and misses Rowan 9.

You may notice that the holdout line on the chart ends lower than the training line. That can look backwards. The main reason is Rowan 9, the one planted exception in the training rows. It costs 1.49 on its own. All four holdout rows follow the usual pattern. A smaller reason is that two training rows sit close to the boundary. Alder 12 and Holly 3 cost 0.59 each. No holdout row sits that close. Together, these make the holdout the easier of the two piles in this table.

### Why the split is random, and what each slice is for

This lab cuts its table by hand. The first twelve rows are for training and the last four are held out. A real project does it differently. It shuffles the rows like a deck of cards and then cuts. Nobody picks which rows land in the holdout. This is called a **random split**.

A starting number called a **seed** drives the shuffle, and the same seed always gives the same shuffle. It works like a game that rebuilds the same random level when you type in the same code. So anyone can repeat your split and check your work.

Cutting by order is risky. If the table were sorted by price, the holdout would be the four most expensive apartments. Its score would describe only that corner. If it were sorted by date, the holdout would hold only the newest rows. Shuffling mixes old and new, cheap and expensive, so no one kind of row fills the holdout.

A shuffle is safer but not perfect. There are 1,820 ways to pick four hidden rows out of sixteen. A fair shuffle makes each one equally likely:

- **140 of them:** In about one shuffle in every thirteen, all four hidden rows have the same price label. That holdout could only test high-priced apartments, or only low-priced ones, so it says nothing about the other kind.
- **784 of them:** In 43.1 percent of the draws, the holdout has two high and two low. This lab's does too.
- **896 of them:** In the other 49.2 percent, it has three of one kind and one of the other.

Dealing the cards so that each pile keeps the same mix of high and low is called stratifying. It removes those unlucky all-one-label draws. A tiny holdout has another weakness. With four rows, each one is worth a quarter of the score. So holdout accuracy can only move in jumps of 25 points.

Real projects usually keep three piles instead of two, because there are three different jobs to do:

- **Training:** This is the pile the rule learns from.
- **Validation:** You look at this pile while you choose settings, such as the encoding or the tree depth. It is called the **validation set**.
- **Test:** This pile is the final exam. You score it once at the very end, after every choice has been made. It is called the **test set**.

Picture a class that sits the same mock exam every Friday and changes how it studies after each result. By the end of term the class scores well on that mock exam. Part of the reason is that every change was picked to suit those exact questions. So the mock score ends up rosier than the real exam is likely to be.

This lab has only a training pile and a holdout, and you will choose settings by watching the holdout. That quietly turns it into a validation set, so its scores are a little optimistic. A real project would keep a separate test set that no choice has ever seen.

### A holdout from a different process

A holdout only tells you about rows made by the same process as the training rows. That means the same kinds of apartments and the same rule linking them to price. The **Holdout drawn from** switch on the **Train versus holdout** card lets you break that on purpose.

Set it to `a different process`. Quay 3, Reed 5, Slate 7 and Tarn 11 then replace the four holdout rows. They come from a market where small apartments are the expensive ones. They are high priced when rooms ≤ 2, which is the opposite of the usual rule.

The fit never saw any holdout rows, so it stays exactly as it was. **L train** stays at 0.45. Only the four rows it is scored on are different. At the opening settings, **Holdout acc** falls from 100 percent to 25 percent. The rule now gets only 1 of the 4 rows right. The **Holdout accuracy change** readout shows this as `-75 pts`.

Meanwhile **L holdout** rises from 0.31 to 1.39. The readout **Holdout loss, different ÷ same** shows that this is 4.5 times as high. The new loss is also worse than 0.69, the coin-flip value. On these rows the rule does worse than having no rule at all. It still gets Reed 5 right, which has 2 rooms and a park. The old rule and the new one happen to agree there.

This is called **distribution shift**. It means the process behind the rows you score has moved away from the one behind the training rows. Here the rule linking rooms to price flipped, which is about as extreme as shift gets. The fit did not get worse. The world it is scored on changed underneath it. A holdout drawn from the old process can never see that coming.

Picture someone who spent years guessing apartment prices in a town where bigger always meant pricier. They got very good at it. Then they move to a market where small apartments are the expensive ones. Their skill has not faded at all, yet most of their guesses are now wrong. No amount of re-checking their old town's prices could have warned them. The comparison stops matching in one place. A person notices the new market and adjusts. A fitted rule keeps using the old pattern until someone refits it.

## Why it’s here

The earlier labs in this group each handed you one piece of the loop. You met the idea of choosing a kind of rule and the arithmetic of a weighted score like `w·x+b`. You also met a table row turned into numbers. And you met eight older ways for a rule to draw borders between groups. None of them ran one labeled problem from a raw row to a final check. So "the model learned" had nothing solid to point at. This lab joins the pieces, and joining them exposes two problems that no single piece shows on its own.

### A training score cannot tell learning from memorizing

Every fit is built to push its training loss down. A small training loss shows only that the search worked on those twelve rows. It says nothing about any other rows.

How flexible a family of rules is, meaning how many shapes it can bend into, is called its **capacity**. A tree with more levels of questions has more capacity. Each extra level slices the training rows into smaller groups. With enough levels, even a strange row can end up in a group of its own.

Picture a classmate who notices that question 9 on the practice sheet is a trick question. Its answer is "no", and she remembers it by its blue diagram. Now she scores 100 percent on that sheet. Then the mock exam has a different question with a blue diagram whose answer is "yes". She writes "no" with complete confidence.

A deep tree does the same thing with Rowan 9 when color is part of the encoding. At depth 5, meaning five levels of questions, one of its questions is about color. The answers put Rowan 9 in a small group of its own: blue apartments with 3 rooms. None of its questions asks about the park. So Maple 10, a blue 3-room holdout apartment, lands in that same group.

The only training apartment in that group is Rowan 9, which is low. So the tree gives Maple 10 a 0 percent chance of being high priced. Maple 10 really is high priced. The training loss drops to zero while the holdout loss shoots up. Only the holdout can show you that.

### When a number moves, which choice moved it?

The loop has several separate knobs: **Encoding**, **Algorithm**, **Loss** and **Tree depth**. Suppose you changed three of them at once and the holdout score improved. You could not say which change helped, and two of them might even have cancelled each other out. Keeping the stages separate lets you change exactly one thing and watch which numbers move.

That matters because some changes help on the training rows and hurt everywhere else. Picture a friend who notices something about this term's practice sheets. Almost every question printed in red ink had the answer "true". She starts answering "true" to anything in red. Her practice scores go up. The pattern was a coincidence of those sheets, though, so on the mock exam it costs her points.

Color plays the same trick in this table. Four of the five red training apartments are high priced, including every red one with 2 or more rooms. Neither red holdout apartment is high priced. A rule that can see color picks up a cue that only holds on the training rows. To catch it, change the encoding alone and watch what the holdout score does.

## How to play with it

The lab's seven steps walk through the loop in order, and each part below matches one of them. Each part starts by naming the settings it needs, so your numbers match the ones written here. Under the sliders on the **Supervised loop** card, a short note names the last setting you changed. **Fit step** does not count. It also says what that change could move.

### Encode one row

Click **Alder 12** in the apartment table on the **Train versus holdout** card. The worked example may have left another apartment selected. Next click the **Encode** chip on the **Supervised loop** card. The panel below the chips now shows Alder 12's vector. Set **Encoding** to `rooms + park`, then to `integer codes`. Watch Alder 12's vector grow from two numbers to three as color joins it.

### Read the split

Click the **Split** chip. Then, in the table on the **Train versus holdout** card, click **Rowan 9** and after that **Laurel 2**. The **In the fit?** readout changes from yes to no, because only training rows ever enter the fit.

### Choose a hypothesis

Click the **Hypothesis** chip and set **Encoding** back to `rooms + park`. Leave **Loss** on `log-loss` and **Tree depth** at 2, where the lab opens. Then set **Algorithm** to `linear`, then `logistic`, then `tree`, and watch the **Decision view**. The solid straight line gives way to dashed cuts that run only straight across or straight up and down.

Each time, read **L holdout** and **Holdout acc** on the **Loss over the fit** card. Both `linear` and `logistic` read 0.31 and 100 percent. The `tree` reads 0.35 and 75 percent, because it gets one of the four hidden apartments wrong. Only the family of rules changed, yet the holdout reading moved.

One thing to expect here: `linear` and `logistic` land on exactly the same fit. Log-loss needs a chance between 0 and 1. So with **Loss** on `log-loss`, the lab squashes the `linear` score with the same sigmoid that `logistic` uses. Switch **Loss** to `MSE` if you want to see `linear` as a family of its own.

### Scrub the fit

Set **Encoding** to `rooms + park`, **Algorithm** to `logistic` and **Loss** to `log-loss`. Then click the **Fit** chip, and drag **Fit step** down to 0 and slowly back up to 48. Watch the **Loss over the fit** card as you go. The solid line is the training loss, and the dashed line is the holdout loss. Both start at 0.69, the coin-flip value, and both fall as the weights move. They end at 0.45 for training and 0.31 for the holdout. On the **Decision view**, the boundary line first shows up in the bottom-left corner after two steps. Then it sweeps to the right and settles into place.

### Swap one stage

**Try this:** Leave **Algorithm** on `logistic` and **Loss** on `log-loss`. Switch only **Encoding** from `rooms + park` to `one-hot`, then back again. **L train** drops from 0.45 to 0.26, which looks like progress. But **L holdout** climbs from 0.31 to 0.75. Adding color let the rule lean on color cues that hold only on the training rows. Red looks expensive there, so Laurel 2 gets called high priced. Blue looks cheap, since three of the four blue training apartments are low, Rowan 9 among them. So Maple 10 gets called low. **Holdout acc** drops from 100 percent to 50 percent. If you try `integer codes` as well, it lands between the other two settings on both loss numbers.

Then set **Encoding** back to `rooms + park` and switch only **Loss** from `log-loss` to `MSE`. Both loss numbers change, but they cannot tell you which setting is better. The loss is both the target the fit aims at and the ruler you read the result with. Switching it changes the fit and the ruler together. Comparing the two numbers is like asking whether 8 points in one board game beats 120 points in another.

To compare the two loss settings fairly, read **Holdout acc**, which counts right answers the same way under both. On `rooms + park` it stays at 100 percent either way. Now set **Encoding** to `one-hot` and switch **Loss** between `log-loss` and `MSE` again. **Holdout acc** drops from 50 percent to 25 percent. Changing only the loss made the rule miss one more hidden apartment.

### Raise tree depth

Set **Loss** back to `log-loss`, **Encoding** to `integer codes` and **Algorithm** to `tree`. Then move **Tree depth** from 1 to 5. **L train** falls all the way to 0, while **L holdout** climbs from 0.48 to about 10.

The holdout loss does not rise smoothly. It jumps at depth 2, when the tree starts asking about color. Every red training apartment with 2 or more rooms is high priced. So the tree calls Laurel 2 high with full confidence, and it is wrong. The loss jumps again at depth 5. The tree fences off Rowan 9 and drags Maple 10 into the same group.

Click the **Holdout** chip to see the **Gap** readout, which is the holdout loss minus the training loss. Notice how large it gets as extra depth lets the tree memorize quirks that only the training rows have.

Now switch **Encoding** to `rooms + park` and try the same depths again. This time the training loss stops falling while it is still above 0. Rowan 9 and Cedar 9 share the same vector, (3, 1), yet carry different labels. So no question about rooms or park can pull them apart. By depth 5 the holdout loss is lower than it was at depth 1, not higher.

### Shift the holdout

Go back to `rooms + park`, `logistic` and `log-loss`. Then, on the **Train versus holdout** card, switch **Holdout drawn from** between `the same process` and `a different process`. The small table under the apartment list scores the same fit on both holdouts side by side. Its columns are **L holdout**, **Holdout acc** and **Rows right**. Below it, **L train (same in both)** never moves, because the fit itself never changes.

## What to notice

- Filled circles on the **Decision view** were used in the fit, and open circles were not. Suppose an open circle lands on the correct side of the line. Then the rule got that apartment right without ever seeing it, which is exactly what the holdout is for.
- The shading and the line are the actual fitted rule. The lab works it out on a 28 by 28 grid of points across the map. They are not a drawing. When color is in the encoding, the map can show only one color at a time. So it shows the rule at the color of the apartment you selected.
- Changing one stage at a time is the whole point. The clearest case is switching only **Encoding** to `one-hot` with `logistic` and `log-loss`. Training loss drops from 0.45 to 0.26, while holdout loss rises from 0.31 to 0.75.
- Color tracks price only on the training rows. Of the 5 red training apartments, 4 are high priced, and neither red holdout apartment is.
- Switching only **Loss** changes the fit and the ruler together. A holdout MSE and a holdout log-loss sit on different scales, so you cannot rank them against each other.
- On `integer codes`, a depth-5 tree takes training loss to 0. Meanwhile holdout log-loss climbs from 0.48 to about 10. That is extra capacity memorizing quirks of the training rows.
- The jump to about 10 comes from two holdout rows that the tree gets wrong with total confidence. From depth 2 on, Laurel 2 lands with the red apartments that have 2 or more rooms. Those are all high priced in training, so the tree marks it high when it is low.
- Maple 10 is blue with 3 rooms, so it lands in the same leaf as Rowan 9. The tree marks it low when it is high. Each of those two rows costs about 21, the lab's cap, while the other two cost almost nothing. The average over four rows comes out near 10.
- On `rooms + park`, Rowan 9 and Cedar 9 share the vector (3, 1) but have different labels. So no depth can memorize the exception. Training loss stalls above 0, because capacity cannot memorize what the encoding cannot tell apart.
- Switching **Holdout drawn from** to `a different process` leaves the fit and **L train** alone. Yet holdout accuracy drops from 100 percent to 25 percent. The fit did not get worse. The rows it is scored on stopped following its rule.
- Every holdout reading counts four rows, so one row moves accuracy by 25 points. Even a holdout from the right process gives only a rough estimate.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- The authors wrote the sixteen apartments by hand, and the split too. It takes the last four rows with no shuffle at all.
- The lab has no validation pile, no test pile and no neural network. It shows the loop at its smallest.
- Park is only yes or no, so the apartments sit on two flat rails on the map. The picture nudges apartments that share a spot apart so you can click them. The fit always uses their exact numbers.
- The search is short and exact. It takes 48 steps that each use all twelve training rows at once. That is nothing like the training run behind a big model.
- The color order in `integer codes` is invented. When color is in the encoding, the map shows only one slice of the rule at a time.
- The different-process rows are one extreme case, with four invented rows whose price rule is reversed. Real shift is usually partial and slow. It can show up in the inputs, in the labels, or in the rule that links them.

### Where the real loop breaks

In a real project the same stages are all still there. The holdout has to stay out of every choice, not only out of the search. Say you use the holdout's prices to decide which colors count as expensive. Or say you decide when to stop training by watching the holdout curve. Either way, holdout information has slipped into the fit. That is called leakage, and it is the subject of the next lab.

A small holdout gives only a rough estimate of generalization, even when you draw it fairly. A different draw of four rows could give a very different reading. And no holdout can warn you about a change that happens after you drew it. It came from the old process. The only way to catch that kind of shift is to keep watching new data as it comes in.

**A common mix-up:** It is tempting to treat a tiny training loss as proof that a model works. Training loss only reads the rows the rule was fitted to. Holdout loss is the first estimate this lab gives for new rows. The two can even move in opposite directions, as the deep tree showed. The second mix-up runs the other way. A good holdout score is not a promise about the future. It only speaks for rows made like the training rows.

## Going deeper

- **Least squares:** With `linear` and `MSE`, the **Loss over the fit** card adds a dotted line. It is labeled least squares (train). That line is the training loss of a formula that jumps straight to the best weights in one go. The gradient steps start at 0 and creep toward the dotted line. After 48 small steps they are still slightly above it. So the weights on the **Worked example** card are the stepped ones, not the formula's.
- **One-hot and a tiny extra:** With `one-hot`, the three color slots always add up to 1. That formula then cannot decide how much score to give the colors and how much to give the bias. The lab adds a tiny amount, 0.0001 (written 10⁻⁴), so the formula can always be solved.
- **Step size:** On the **Fit** stage, the note shows the step size η. This is the fraction of the gradient that each step moves by. It is 0.85 at the opening settings. The ‖∇‖ readout beside it is the size of the gradient, meaning how steeply the training loss still slopes downhill. It shrinks as the fit settles.
- **Tree questions:** A tree picks the question that best cuts the mixing of high and low in each group. With **Loss** on `log-loss`, it measures mixing with a score called entropy. With `MSE`, it uses one called Gini. Both scores read zero for a group that is all one label.
- **Kinds of shift:** Distribution shift can come from new inputs, new labels, or a new rule linking the two. The different-process rows here are the last kind, which is often called concept shift.
- **The next lab:** How we know a model works takes the holdout reading apart with a confusion matrix. That is a table of which answers were right and which were wrong. It also shows how a leaked holdout lies. And it compares made-up learning curves with the holdout curve you have met here.
