## The big picture

This module is about what a benchmark score can and cannot settle in a deployment decision, and
who bears the costs it never records, from past students and contractors to readers and patients.
The lab lays out competing claims and leaves them unscored. It does not decide whether any
deployment is justified.

It builds on **The ecosystem** and adds two calculations that put numbers on what a score leaves
out. **Disaggregated evaluation**, reporting a metric separately for each group of people, shows
whom an average hides. A compute estimate separates **training compute**, paid once per run, from
**inference compute**, paid on every request served.

### Where it shows up

- **Benchmarks and leaderboards.** A headline accuracy averages over the test set's population.
  Group-fairness criteria such as **equal opportunity** and **equalized odds** (Hardt, Price, and
  Srebro, 2016) compare error rates group by group.
- **Regulation.** The EU AI Act is **risk-based regulation**, scaling duties with the harm a use
  could cause. Its high-risk list includes emergency healthcare patient triage and AI that
  evaluates learning outcomes in education, and Article 50 asks for machine-readable marks on
  generated content, a form of **provenance**.
- **Energy reporting.** Published energy figures for training often cover only the final run and
  not the experiments before it. The lab converts its estimate of arithmetic operations to
  energy with one assumed utilization.

### Why it matters

- **An average can hide a group.** In the lab's synthetic triage model, accuracy falls about 3
  points as group B goes from 10% to 50% of patients, while missed urgent cases per 1,000 double,
  mostly in group B. A single accuracy cannot show where the misses fall; per-group reporting can.
- **Some costs never reach a score.** Training text taken without **consent**, labeling
  **labor**, and the energy of serving are costs no score records. Most fall on people who did not
  choose the deployment, which makes them **externalities**. A real person's likeness used without
  their say is only partly measurable, and only by a benchmark whose prompts ask for real people.
- **Serving is the recurring bill.** Counting only training misses compute paid on every request,
  and at high volume serving dominates.
- **A rule is not a verdict.** Meeting a risk-based rule's duties still leaves open whether a
  deployment is justified.

Nothing here runs a trained model or measures a real system. Each card is authored text, fixed
synthetic rates, or closed-form arithmetic.

**Tension explorer**, **Claim** steps through them one at a time.

**Who the benchmark represents** evaluates a synthetic triage model with invented, fixed group
rates. The key experiment moves **Group B share of patients** from 10% to 50%: accuracy barely
moves, **Missed urgent per 1,000** climbs, and the **Equal-opportunity gap** and
**False-positive gap** stay put.

**Training versus serving** estimates floating-point operations (FLOP) for a dense model in closed

day cumulative serving compute matches one training run, does not move when **Parameters** changes.
The card's note converts compute to energy in MWh, assuming 40% of an A100's peak throughput and
400 W per GPU.

## What it is

A deployment decision has claims for it and claims against it, and they are borne by different
people. This lab lays out four fictional deployments, each with five authored claims tagged by theme,
by who bears them, and by whether any benchmark number could speak to them. One is an image generator,
with claims about consent, likeness, and **provenance**. The lab never reduces them to a score.

Two cards then compute what a leaderboard leaves out.

### Who the benchmark represents

A single accuracy is an average over whoever was in the test set. The synthetic triage model here is
94.0% accurate on group A and 85.4% on group B, whose notes the labels under-represent. It misses 10%
of group A's urgent cases and 45% of group B's.

On a holdout with 10% group B it scores 93.1%. Move the population to 50% group B and accuracy falls
only to 89.7%, while missed urgent cases per 1,000 patients go from 27 to 55. That gap is what
**disaggregated evaluation** exists to show, and what **distribution shift** hides.

The gap has a name. The model flags 90% of group A's urgent cases and 55% of group B's, a
true-positive-rate gap of 35 points. **Equal opportunity** (Hardt, Price, and Srebro, 2016) asks for
equal true-positive rates, so the model is far from it.

**Equalized odds** also asks for equal false-positive rates. Those are 5% and 7%, only 2 points apart,
so the model fails equalized odds almost entirely because of the first gap. Neither gap moves with the
group mix.

### Training versus serving

**Training compute** for a dense transformer is about `6·N·D` operations for N parameters and D
training tokens. **Inference compute** is about `2·N` per token served. One is paid once; the other is
paid on every request.

They are equal after `3·D ÷ tokens served per day` days, so model size cancels out. With 2T training
tokens and 10B tokens served a day that is 600 days; at 1T tokens a day it is 6.

## Why it’s here

Leaderboards answer harnesses. Misinformation, **dual use**, and **accountability** do not sit on
those axes. The extractor that fills in an intake form can also compile a dossier, and no score says
who answers for a wrong output. The ecosystem lab showed that a release is a bundle of parts; this
lab asks who pays for each part, and who is left out of the measurement.

## What to notice

- In each deployment at most one claim is fully on a leaderboard, and in three of the four an Against
  line already answers it. On `Image generator` the only benchmark claim is prompt match; consent, likeness,
  and provenance are not.
- The costs fall on people other than the buyer: past students, contractors, writers, readers, patients.
- At the default 30% group B, group B is 30% of patients and 66% of missed urgent cases.
- The **Equal-opportunity gap** stays at 35 points and the **False-positive gap** at 2 at every share.
  Both are properties of the model, not of who walks in.
- Training energy is a one-off; serving energy scales with use. At high volume, serving dominates.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The deployments, claims, and tags are authored teaching sentences, not findings. The
triage model's group rates are invented and fixed, and only two fairness criteria are named; others
exist, such as demographic parity. The compute arithmetic assumes a dense model and
one utilization figure. It ignores long-context attention cost, sparse mixture-of-experts models,
cooling, and the energy source.

**In the real mechanism.** A benchmark still cannot finish the question. **Externalities** stay off the
loss curve, a public URL is not consent, and a model card is not accountability by itself. Published
energy figures often cover only the final training run and not the experiments before it.

## Going deeper

### Rules and frameworks, checked 30 September 2026

Regulation and standards now name deployments like these. Three are worth knowing by name. They set duties
and processes; none decides whether a deployment is justified.

- **EU AI Act, risk levels.** It is **risk-based regulation**. The European Commission describes four
  levels: unacceptable risk (banned), high risk, transparency risk, and minimal risk. Annex III lists
  high-risk uses, including emergency healthcare patient triage and AI that evaluates learning outcomes
  in education.
- **EU AI Act, transparency.** Article 50 asks providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image,
  video, or text to mark outputs so they are machine-readable and detectable as generated, and deployers
  of deepfakes to disclose them. AI-generated text published to inform the public must be disclosed
  unless a person has reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility.
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework.** AI RMF 1.0, released in January 2023, is voluntary and organized
  around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. A Generative AI Profile followed in July 2024.
- **ISO/IEC 42001.** Published in 2023, it is a management-system standard for AI: requirements for how an
  organization sets up and keeps improving its own AI governance. It is about processes, not any one
  model's behavior.

The schedule is the part that moves. As the Commission's AI Act timeline read on 30 September 2026,
prohibitions applied from 2 February 2025, rules for general-purpose AI from 2 August 2025, most other
rules including Article 50 from 2 August 2026, and Annex III high-risk rules from 2 December 2027, a date
moved later by an amendment, the Digital Omnibus on AI. Check the current text before relying on a date
here.
