## The big picture

News stories often say that a new AI model got the top score on a test. It is tempting to hear something bigger, as if the model must be good and people should use it. This module is about the gap between those two ideas. It is also about the people who end up standing in that gap.

Putting an AI system to work in a real place, for real people, is called a **deployment**. A school might deploy a tutor, and a clinic might deploy a tool that helps sort patients. Every deployment brings benefits and costs, and they rarely land on the same people. A test score can measure a few of them, but most never show up in any score.

### Where you have met it

AI models are compared using fixed tests called **benchmarks**. Their scores are often ranked in public tables called **leaderboards**. When you read that one chatbot beat another on a test, you are reading a benchmark result.

You have also met the costs in this module, even if nobody pointed them out. Chatbots and image generators learn from huge piles of writing and pictures. Much of that was gathered without asking the people who made it.

Every answer a chatbot gives uses computing power, and that cost comes back each time someone asks a question. An image generator can also make a realistic picture of a real person who never agreed to it.

Lawmakers have started to respond to problems like these. The European Union's AI Act keeps a list of high-risk uses of AI. Those uses must follow extra rules. The list includes AI that sorts emergency patients. It also includes AI that judges how well students are learning.

### Why it matters

If a top score ends the argument, you miss everything the score never looked at. A test can leave out some of the people who will use a system. The score can still look fine while those people are failed far more often. Nothing in that single number warns you.

The people who carry the costs are often not the people who chose the system or paid for it. Students whose old essays were used never get a line on a leaderboard. Neither do workers who labeled data by hand or readers who meet a confident mistake. Nor do patients a test left out. Without a deliberate search for these people, their costs stay out of sight.

None of this means tests are useless. A benchmark tells you something real about the one question it asks. This module helps you see how far that answer reaches and where it stops.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- At least two costs that never appear on a leaderboard, and who pays for each one. Examples are writing used without permission and the hidden work of data labelers.
- Why a test score cannot settle whether a use of AI is right. Look at who the test included and who it left out.
- How the computing needed to train a model once compares with the computing needed to answer people daily. You should also see why the daily cost can catch up.
- Which fairness rule the lab's patient-sorting model breaks, and how a risk-based law treats a case like this. The law does not decide whether a deployment is justified.

## What it is

### A debate with no judge

Picture a class debate about whether your school should start using an AI tutor. One side says a tutor that never gets tired could help students in the evening, after the building closes. The other side asks whose essays the tutor learned from, and whether anyone asked those students first.

Now give the debate an unusual rule: there is no judge, and nobody keeps score. Each speaker makes one point. A note beside it says who would gain or pay if that point is right. That is how this lab works. Its second card is called **Tension explorer** because it lays out arguments that pull against each other.

The comparison stops matching in two places. In a real debate, both teams choose to show up and speak for themselves. Many people who bear these costs were never in the room. Think of the students whose old essays were used. A debate also ends with a winner. This one stays open, because the lab cannot decide for the people affected.

### Four deployments, five claims each

The lab offers four made-up deployments, and you choose one with the **Deployment** control. They are `School tutor`, `Newsroom draft`, `Clinic intake` and `Image generator`. Each one comes with five short arguments, which the lab calls **claims**. Some claims argue for the deployment and some argue against it. The card sorts them into two columns, headed For and Against.

Every claim carries three tags. You can read them in **Tension explorer** once you pick that claim.

- **Theme:** This tag names what the claim is about, such as access, consent, or energy and labor.
- **Who bears it:** This tag names who carries that benefit or cost, such as past students or contractors.
- **Can a benchmark measure it?:** This tag answers yes, partly or no, and then gives a one-line reason.

Below the two columns, the **Deployment** card shows four readouts. **For claims** and **Against claims** count how many claims the lesson writers put on each side. That is not the same as counting votes. **A benchmark speaks to** counts how many claims a test score could cover fully, and how many partly.

The last readout, **Score**, always says none. Nothing on the page weights the claims or adds them into a total.

### Reading one claim, step by step

Set **Deployment** to `School tutor`, then use the **Claim** control to pick `2 Against`. The claim reads: “The essays used to train it were collected without those students' consent.” Its **Theme** is consent, and **Who bears it** says past students. **Can a benchmark measure it?** says no, with the reason “no leaderboard measures this”.

Read those three tags together as one complete thought. The cost is that people's writing was used without their permission. Students who wrote essays in earlier years pay it. No test score could ever show that cost. However well the tutor scores, those essays were still used without asking.

Now step to `3 For`: “A published harness shows higher homework completion.” A **harness** is the setup that runs a test on a model and records its score. So this claim rests on a real number. Its **Theme** is measured benefit, and **Who bears it** says district. **Can a benchmark measure it?** says yes, with the reason “a benchmark number exists for this”. The district is the group of schools that would pay for the tutor.

Claim `4 Against` answers it right away. It reads “Completion is not learning, and the harness does not represent the district that would pay.” In other words, finishing more homework does not prove that students learned more. The students in the test may also differ from the students in this district.

Its tag says partly, because a test could speak to this only in some cases. It would have to report results for each group of students separately. It could also test students like the ones who will use the tutor. Or it could be built for this exact case.

### The costs no leaderboard counts

The first goal of this lesson is to name costs that never show up in a test score. The lab's claims raise several of them. Each one below starts in everyday words before giving the name the lab's glossary uses.

#### Writing and pictures used without asking

The examples a model learns from are called its **training data**. For chatbots and image generators, much of that data came from the web. Nobody asked the people who wrote it or appear in it. Permission to use someone's work or likeness is called **consent**. A page being public on the web does not mean its author gave consent.

Picture a made-up artist who posts drawings on a public art site so that friends can see them. Later a company copies those drawings and a huge pile of other pictures to train an image generator. The drawings were public, but nobody asked the artist. No score the generator ever earns will mention them.

The company may attach a license to the finished model. A license is a set of rules for how other people may use it. That license covers the model itself. It cannot give permission on behalf of the people whose work went in.

#### Hidden human work

Behind every polished AI product are people doing slow, careful work by hand. Some label examples with the right answers. Others try to make the model misbehave so its problems can be found and fixed, which is called red-teaming. Others review harmful material so that users never see it, which is called content moderation. Even the data that teaches a model to follow instructions is made this way.

All of this is **labor**, and it is often low-paid and invisible behind the product. In the School tutor list, claim 5 says the labeling work “was billed to contractors”. Contractors are outside workers hired for the job.

#### Energy

Training a model takes a huge amount of electricity once. Answering people takes more electricity every day after that. The same School tutor claim says “Serving energy is billed to the school”. Here, serving means running the model to answer people. The **Training versus serving** card, covered later in this section, works out how those two costs compare.

#### Costs that land on people who had no say

Some costs are paid by people who never chose the deployment. The school pays its own electricity bill. But making that electricity can affect people far away who were never asked. The writers whose work was copied were never asked either. A person whose old task the system now does also pays a cost.

These costs are called **externalities**. They never show up in a price. They also stay off the loss curve, the chart that tracks a model's errors during training. Newsroom draft claim 4 says it directly. It reads “Cheap for the publisher is an externality for the people whose writing was scraped.” Scraped means copied off websites automatically by a program. The writers paid part of the cost of every cheap article without being asked.

#### Mistakes that sound sure of themselves

A model's writing can be smooth and convincing even when it is wrong. Newsroom draft claim 2 says “Fluent errors can reach readers in the desk's own voice.” The desk is the newsroom team that publishes the story. A mistake could go out sounding exactly like the newspaper itself. The theme is misinformation, which means false information that spreads. The people who bear it are readers.

#### Help that can also harm

A tool that pulls facts out of a patient's notes can fill in an intake form. That saves the staff time. The same tool could also gather everything about one patient into a single file. Someone could misuse that file. A skill that can help and harm like this is called **dual use**. The tool itself does not choose which way it gets used.

Clinic intake claim 5 is the lab's example. It reads “Dual use: the same extractor can compile a dossier on a patient.” An extractor is a tool that pulls facts out of text. A dossier is a detailed file about one person.

#### Faces and fake photos

An image generator can make a realistic picture of a real person without that person's say. The lab files this under the theme likeness, which means someone's face and appearance. A related problem is that readers may not be able to tell a generated picture from a real photograph.

A record of where a picture came from, and how it was made or changed, is called **provenance**. One example is a mark inside the image file that a computer can read, saying the image was generated. A mark like that helps only while it survives editing and someone checks it. It supports people's judgment instead of replacing it.

#### Nobody to answer for it

When something goes wrong, someone should be able to say who decided to use the system, and on whom. They should also say how people can get a mistake put right. Being able to answer those questions is called **accountability**.

Newsroom draft claim 5 raises it: “Accountability is unclear when a wrong date ships under a byline.” A byline is the line naming the reporter who wrote a story.

Picture a made-up school newspaper that starts drafting stories with an AI tool. A story about the spring concert goes out under a student reporter's name with the wrong date. Families turn up on the wrong night. Was that the reporter's fault, or the editor's? Was it the teacher who chose the tool, or the company that built it?

If nobody can answer, accountability is missing, and the families have nobody to complain to. A **model card** is a document describing a model and how it was tested. It does not answer that question by itself. It describes the model, not the people who decided to use it.

### Who the benchmark represents

Picture a made-up teacher who announces that the class averaged 88% on a test, which sounds like great news. Twenty students sat near the front and scored 95%. The other five sat at the back where they could not read the board, and they scored 60%. The average is true, but it hides the five students who were struggling. You only find them by splitting the results by group.

The **Who the benchmark represents** card applies the same idea to a made-up clinic. When a clinic is busy, staff sort patients by how urgent each case is. That way, staff see the most serious ones first. That sorting is called triage. The card's invented model reads each patient's notes and either flags the case as urgent or leaves it alone.

The card's small heading calls it a “synthetic triage model”. Synthetic means made up for the lab, not trained on real patients.

The patients belong to two groups, called A and B. In both groups, 1 patient in 5 has a truly urgent case. Group B stands for patients whose notes were under-represented in the labels. That means too few notes like theirs were among the examples that people had marked with the right answer.

The model learned what an urgent case looks like from those marked examples. So it learned much less about how urgent cases look in group B's notes.

#### Working out each group's accuracy

A model's **accuracy** is the share of all its calls, urgent or not, that turn out to be right. Take 1,000 patients from group A and follow them through the model, one step at a time.

- One patient in five is urgent, which makes 200 urgent patients in the group.
- The model flags 90% of these urgent patients, which means it catches 180 of them.
- The other 20 urgent patients slip through without a flag. These are the misses that matter most in a clinic.
- The other 800 patients are not urgent, and the model correctly leaves 95% of them alone.
- Those correct “not urgent” calls come to 760. Adding the 180 urgent patients it caught gives 940 correct calls.
- That gives group A an accuracy of 94.0%, because 940 out of 1,000 is 94%.

Now take 1,000 patients from group B, who also include 200 urgent cases.

- The model flags only 55% of the urgent ones, which means it catches 110 of them.
- That leaves 90 urgent patients it misses, which is nearly half of the urgent cases in the group.
- Of the 800 patients who are not urgent, the model correctly leaves 93% alone, which is 744 patients.
- Adding the 110 urgent patients it caught to those 744 gives 854 correct calls. So group B's accuracy is 85.4%.

The two accuracies, 94.0% and 85.4%, are less than 9 percentage points apart, since 94.0 minus 85.4 is 8.6. The gap between two percentages is counted in percentage points, often called points. A gap that small does not sound alarming, but the misses tell a very different story.

For group A, the model misses 10 urgent cases in every 100. For group B, it misses 45 in every 100. That is more than four times as often.

Accuracy hides most of that difference because urgent cases are only 1 patient in 5. Most of each group's score comes from the other four patients in five. The model handles those non-urgent patients about equally well for both groups.

#### The test the model passed

The model was tested on a **holdout**, a set of cases kept aside for testing. Those cases were not used to build the model. In that holdout, only 10% of the patients were in group B. Out of every 1,000 holdout patients, that makes 900 from group A and 100 from group B.

- Group A misses 20 urgent cases in every 1,000 of its patients, so its 900 patients produce 18 misses.
- Group B misses 90 in every 1,000, so its 100 patients add 9 more misses on top of those.
- That comes to 27 missed urgent cases per 1,000 patients, with an overall accuracy of 93.1%.

#### The clinic that will use it

Now suppose the clinic that will really use the model sees half of its patients from group B. Out of every 1,000 patients who walk through its doors, the numbers change.

- Group A's 500 patients produce 10 missed urgent cases. That is half of the 20 that a full 1,000 would produce.
- Group B's 500 patients produce 45, which is half of the 90 that a full 1,000 would produce.
- That comes to 55 missed urgent cases per 1,000 patients, with an overall accuracy of 89.7%.

Compare the two clinics side by side. Accuracy slipped only a few points, from 93.1% to 89.7%. Missed urgent cases per 1,000 patients went from 27 to 55, which is roughly double. Most of the extra misses landed on group B. In fact, 45 of the 55 misses, about 82%, are in group B.

The accuracy for a mix of patients is a **weighted average**. Each group's accuracy counts in proportion to how many patients come from that group. With half the patients from each group, it is the ordinary average of the two. Adding 94.0% and 85.4% and dividing by 2 gives 89.7%, which is what the card shows.

The holdout works the same way. Ninety percent of 94.0 plus 10% of 85.4 is 84.6 plus 8.5, which rounds to 93.1%.

So why does accuracy barely move while the misses double? Accuracy is a weighted average of two numbers that are already close, 94.0% and 85.4%. Missed cases per 1,000 are also a weighted average, but of 20 and 90, which are far apart. Shifting patients from group A to group B moves that average a long way, so the misses jump.

#### Splitting the score by group

Reporting a score separately for each group of people is called **disaggregated evaluation**. The card does this with its bars, under the headings “Patients per 1,000” and “Missed urgent cases per 1,000”. Each heading has one row for the holdout and one for the mix you chose. Group A is drawn as a solid bar. Group B is drawn with diagonal stripes, which the card calls hatched. A single accuracy number cannot show what those bars show.

#### When the test and the real world differ

Clinic intake claim 4 puts the problem in one line. It reads “The holdout is last year's clinic, not the patients who walk in tomorrow.” Suppose the people a model meets in real use differ from the people it was tested on. That difference is called **distribution shift**.

A shift like this does not announce itself, because the holdout score stays the same number it always was. Only a split by group shows that the errors have moved onto different people. More of them now fall on group B.

#### Beating a lazy guesser

Clinic intake claim 3 says “A documented harness beat a majority-class baseline on holdout notes.” A majority-class baseline is a lazy guesser that always gives the most common answer. Suppose 1 patient in 5 is urgent, as in the triage card. A guesser that always says “not urgent” is then right 80% of the time. It catches no urgent case at all.

Beating that guesser is worth knowing, but it does not tell you which patients the remaining misses fall on. Accuracy alone can look respectable while the cases that matter most slip through.

### Two fairness rules, and the one this model breaks

The triage card also gives the gap between the groups a name. To see it, you need two rates. The card shows both of them on its second row of readouts.

The **true-positive rate** is the share of truly urgent cases that the model flags. It is 90% for group A and 55% for group B. The card shows this in **True-positive rate, A / B** as “90% / 55%”. The gap between them is 90 minus 55, which is 35 points. The card shows it as **Equal-opportunity gap**.

A fairness rule called **equal opportunity** asks that the true-positive rate be the same for every group. In plain words, among the people who really need help, each group should be caught at the same rate. A gap of 35 points means the model is far from that. So equal opportunity is the fairness rule the triage gap breaks.

The **false-positive rate** is the share of non-urgent cases the model flags anyway, which are false alarms. It is 5% for group A and 7% for group B. So the **False-positive gap** is only 2 points.

A stricter rule called **equalized odds** asks for two things at once: equal true-positive rates and equal false-positive rates. The model fails equalized odds as well. That happens almost entirely because of the 35-point gap, since its false-alarm rates are close together.

Each rate is worked out inside one group, as a share of that group's own urgent or non-urgent patients. Moving the slider changes how many patients are in each group. It does not change how the model treats any one of them. In this lab each group's rates are fixed. So both gaps stay the same however you move **Group B share of patients**.

In real use, a group's rates can shift too, for example when its patients change. Accuracy and missed cases do move with the mix. They depend on how many patients come from each group.

Meeting these rules would not settle everything either, because the rates are checked against the labels people wrote. If those labels were wrong, a model could match them equally well for both groups. It would still be wrong for both. Passing a fairness rule also does not show that using the model is justified.

### Training versus serving

Most of the claims so far were about costs that no test number captures. This card looks at a cost that can be counted, but often is not counted in full.

A chatbot reads and writes text in small pieces called **tokens**. A token is often a whole word or part of one. Inside, a model is a huge list of adjustable numbers called **parameters**. Training is the process that sets them. A model with 7B parameters has 7 billion of them.

Making a model and using it are two separate jobs. **Training** means running the model over its training text while the process adjusts its parameters. This takes an enormous amount of arithmetic, paid once for each training run. **Serving** means running the finished model to answer people. Every token it reads or writes takes more arithmetic, paid again every day the model is in use.

The arithmetic for training is called **training compute**. The arithmetic for serving is called **inference compute**, because running a finished model to get answers is called inference. The lab counts both in **FLOP**, short for floating-point operations. One floating-point operation is one arithmetic step on numbers with decimals, such as a single multiplication.

#### The formulas the card shows

The note under the card's readouts gives three short formulas. They are rules of thumb, which means quick estimates that researchers use instead of exact counts. They say roughly how many arithmetic steps each parameter needs for each token. In them, N stands for the number of parameters, and D stands for the number of training tokens. The dot means “multiply”.

- Training takes about `6·N·D` FLOP, and that amount is paid once for each training run.
- Serving takes about `2·N` FLOP for each token, and that amount is paid again on every request.
- The two totals become equal after `3·D ÷ T` days of serving. Here, T is the number of tokens served per day. That day is the break-even point.

#### A worked example at the starting settings

The card starts with **Parameters** at 7B and **Training tokens** at 2T, which means 2 trillion. **Tokens served per day** starts at 10B, which means 10 billion tokens a day.

- For training, multiply the small numbers first: 6 × 7 × 2 is 84.
- A billion has 9 zeros and a trillion has 12, so multiplying by both adds 21 zeros. That makes 84 followed by 21 zeros.
- The readout writes big numbers with one digit before the decimal point. Now 84 is the same as 8.4 × 10, so writing 8.4 adds one more zero to the count.
- That is why the **Training, once** readout shows 8.4×10²² FLOP. The ×10²² part means “times 1 followed by 22 zeros”.
- For serving, 2 × 7 × 10 is 140, and the two billions add 9 zeros each. That makes 140 followed by 18 zeros.
- Next, 140 is 1.4 × 100, which adds two more zeros. So the **Serving, per day** readout shows 1.4×10²⁰ FLOP each day.
- For break-even, 3 × 2 trillion is 6 trillion, which is the same as 6,000 billion. Dividing 6,000 billion by 10 billion gives 600.
- So the **Days to break even** readout says 600 days at the starting settings.

At these settings, serving matches training's compute after 600 days. That is a little over a year and a half. After that day, serving has cost more than training did. The difference keeps growing every day the model stays in use.

The chart tells the same story. Its dashed line, “one training run”, stays flat because training is paid once. Its solid line, “serving, cumulative”, is a running total that climbs a little every day. The point where they cross is marked break-even. Its note reads “The guide marks day 600, where serving has used as much compute as training.”

#### Why model size cancels out

Training costs 6 × N × D. Serving costs 2 × N × T each day, where T is the tokens served per day. To find how many days of serving equal one training run, you divide the first amount by the second. N sits on both the top and the bottom of that division, so it cancels. Then 6 divided by 2 is 3, which leaves 3 × D ÷ T.

Picture a made-up class trip where the bus costs $30 per student, paid once. Lunch costs $10 per student each day, so lunch spending catches up with the bus fare after 3 days. That is true whether 20 students go or 200, because both costs grow with the number of students.

The number of students plays the same part as N, and it cancels for the same reason. The comparison stops matching in what the costs are made of, because compute is arithmetic and not money. The lab's break-even also depends on two settings, not two fixed prices. These are **Training tokens** and **Tokens served per day**.

**A common mix-up:** A bigger model costs more to train, so serving seems slower to catch up. But a bigger model also costs more to serve every token. A model with twice the parameters costs twice as much to train. It also costs twice as much to serve, so the two costs grow together. Move **Parameters** from 1B to 405B, and both **Training, once** and **Serving, per day** grow. **Days to break even** stays at 600 days.

#### Turning compute into energy

The card's note also turns compute into electricity, using two assumptions about the hardware. It assumes the work runs on A100s, a kind of computer chip built for this arithmetic. The chips run at 40% of their top speed and draw 400 watts each.

A watt measures how fast a device uses energy. A watt-hour is the energy used at one watt for one hour. A **megawatt-hour**, written MWh, is a million watt-hours. That is enough to keep a 100-watt light bulb lit for 10,000 hours.

With those assumptions, the default training run uses about 75 MWh for the chips alone. So 75 MWh would keep that bulb lit for 750,000 hours, which is more than 85 years.

Serving at the same efficiency uses about 0.12 MWh a day, which looks tiny next to the training figure. But it is paid every day, so after 600 days it matches the whole training run. The note adds that real decoding usually runs less efficiently than that. Decoding is the step where the model writes its answer.

At lower efficiency, the chips stay switched on longer for the same arithmetic. So they use more electricity. So serving's energy usually catches up even sooner than the compute chart suggests.

### What a risk-based law does, and what it leaves open

The last goal of this lesson is about how rules handle deployments like these. Think of the safety rules for a school science lab. A risky experiment needs goggles, gloves and a teacher watching, while reading a thermometer needs none of that. The rules grow stricter as the danger grows. They do not decide whether a particular experiment is worth doing.

Some laws treat AI in the same way. They give more duties to uses that could cause more harm, instead of giving every system identical rules. That approach is called **risk-based regulation**, and the main example is the European Union's AI Act.

The European Commission, which runs the EU's day-to-day work, describes four levels of risk under the Act.

- **Unacceptable risk:** This level covers uses that are banned altogether.
- **High risk:** This level covers uses that are allowed but bring extra duties with them.
- **Transparency risk:** This level brings duties to be open about AI, such as marking content that was generated.
- **Minimal risk:** This level covers uses that add no extra rules at all.

The Act sorts systems mostly by what they are used for, not by how they are built. Its high-risk list includes emergency healthcare patient triage, which is close to the lab's `Clinic intake`. It also includes AI that evaluates learning outcomes in education. Whether a system falls under that list depends on what it is meant to be used for.

A high score does not take a use off the list. The list is about the use, not the score. Landing on the list brings extra duties and processes that have to be followed.

What the law does not do is decide whether a given deployment is justified. A clinic could follow every duty and still face the lab's open questions. Who bears the costs? Who was left out of the benchmark?

For a use the law allows, such as triage, the law says how carefully to go ahead. It does not say whether this particular deployment should go ahead. Only the short list of banned uses gets a flat no.

That is where the goggles comparison stops matching, because the Act does ban a few uses outright. Triage is not one of them, so for the clinic the law works like the goggles rule.

## Why it’s here

Every test asks one particular question. Its score is an honest answer to that question and to nothing else. The Image generator shows this clearly. Its claim 3 says “A published benchmark rates its images above the previous model's for matching the prompt.” That is a real and useful measurement. It tells you how well the pictures match what people asked for.

The other claims about the same generator ask different questions. Were the training pictures used with the artists' consent? Can the tool fake a real person's face? Can readers tell its pictures from photographs? No amount of skill at matching prompts answers any of those.

Picture a made-up website that reviews phones only on battery life. A phone that scores 10 out of 10 really does have a great battery. But the score says nothing about the camera, or about how the workers who built the phone were treated. Nobody would read that number as the final word on buying the phone. Yet a top leaderboard score often gets read in exactly that way.

This lab follows on from **The ecosystem**, which showed that a model release is a bundle of separate parts. Those parts include the model's numbers, its code, information about its data, its license and its model card. A download, in other words, is not a finished product. This lab asks the next question. Who pays for each of those parts, and who gets left out when the system is measured?

The answer is often someone other than the buyer. You can see this by looking at who bears each claim across the four deployments.

- **`School tutor`:** The district gets the measured benefit. Past students bore the consent cost, contractors bore the labeling work, and the school pays the serving energy.
- **`Newsroom draft`:** The publisher pays for an API, which is a paid service. A program sends text to someone else's model and gets answers back. One more article looks cheap to the publisher, while scraped writers and misled readers carry the costs.
- **`Clinic intake`:** The clinic holds the measured benefit. Patients bear the representation gap, the distribution shift and the dual-use risk.
- **`Image generator`:** The vendor holds the benchmark claim, and small studios get the access benefit. The vendor is the company selling the tool. Illustrators, photographers, people depicted, readers and newsrooms carry the costs.

You might wonder why the lab does not weigh the claims and give each deployment a score. Any weighting would itself be a choice about whose costs count for more. That choice belongs to the people affected, not to a formula.

So the lab leaves every question open on purpose. Understanding what a score does and does not measure helps you ask better questions. It cannot choose the deployment for you.

## How to play with it

Work through the cards in order, because each step builds on the one before it.

### 1. Pick a deployment

Set **Deployment** to `School tutor`, and notice that **Score** reads none. **A benchmark speaks to** reads “1 of 5, 1 partly”. ## What to notice

- At most one claim per deployment is on a leaderboard, and `Newsroom draft` has none at all. Its **A benchmark speaks to** reads “0 of 5, 1 partly”. In the other three deployments, claim 4 is an Against line. It already answers the one benchmark claim beside it.

- The costs fall on people other than the buyer, such as past students, contractors, writers, readers and patients. Meanwhile the district, the publisher and the clinic pay for the system, and the vendor profits from selling it.
- One accuracy number is an average that can hide a group. At the starting 30%, group B is 30% of the patients but 66% of the missed urgent cases. Only a disaggregated evaluation shows that.
- The **Equal-opportunity gap** stays at 35 points and the **False-positive gap** stays at 2 points at every share. In this lab, the model's rates for each group fix both gaps. The mix of people who walk in does not.
- Training energy is paid once, while serving energy keeps growing with use. Say a model trained on 2 trillion tokens serves 100 billion tokens a day. It matches its training compute in 60 days, whatever its size.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- The four deployments, their twenty claims and their tags are teaching sentences written for this lesson. They are not findings from a survey or from interviews with the people affected. Nothing ranks them, and the counts are not votes.
- The triage model is invented, so nothing is trained and both groups' rates are made up and fixed. In real life, groups also differ in how often their cases are urgent. They do not differ only in how often a model makes mistakes.
- Only two fairness rules are named here, although others exist. One is demographic parity. It asks that each group be flagged at the same overall rate, urgent or not. Different rules can also disagree about the same model, so passing one does not mean passing the others.
- The compute is an estimate from formulas, not a measurement. The formulas assume a dense model, meaning one where every parameter works on every token. The estimate also counts one training run only. It leaves out the experiments and failed runs that came before it.
- The formulas also leave out the extra arithmetic needed for very long inputs. They skip models split into many smaller sections. In those models, each token uses only some of the parameters.
- The energy figure covers the chips only, so it ignores cooling and where the electricity comes from. The 40% speed and the 400 watts are assumptions, not readings from a real data center.
- How hard the chips really run depends on things like batching, which means handling many requests together. So the one fixed 40% figure is only a rough guess.

### Where the real thing fails

- A test score still cannot finish the question, however good the score gets. Externalities stay off the loss curve. A public web page is not consent. A model card is not accountability by itself.
- Published energy figures often cover only the final training run. They leave out the experiments and failed runs that came before it. Real energy use also depends on the hardware, how hard it runs, and how efficient the data center is. The way the local electricity is made matters too.
- A model tested on last year's patients can be used on a different group next year. Distribution shift and missing groups change who bears the errors, not only how many errors there are.
- A provenance mark helps only while it survives editing and someone checks it. Meeting a fairness rule says nothing about whether the labels were right. A risk-based law sets duties without deciding whether any given deployment is justified.

People often treat a top score as a finished ethics argument. The score answered the test, and that answer can be true and useful. It says nothing about who was left out of the test or who labeled the data. It also says nothing about whose work was used without asking, or who is accountable when it goes wrong.

## Going deeper

### Rules and frameworks, checked 30 September 2026

Laws and standards now name deployments like the ones in this lab. Three are worth knowing by name: the EU AI Act, the NIST framework and ISO/IEC 42001. All three set duties and processes without deciding whether a deployment is justified.

- **EU AI Act's risk levels:** These are the risk-based regulation described above. They run from banned uses down to uses with no extra rules. The list of high-risk uses sits in a part of the law called Annex III. It includes emergency healthcare patient triage and AI that evaluates learning outcomes in education.
- **EU AI Act's transparency rules:** These sit in a part of the law called Article 50. It asks the makers of tools that generate audio, images, video or text to mark the results. A computer should be able to detect that they were generated.
- **More from Article 50:** People who publish deepfakes have to say so. Deepfakes are realistic fake images, audio or video. AI-written text published to inform the public must be disclosed too. The exception is text that a person has reviewed and taken editorial responsibility for.
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework:** This comes from NIST, a US standards agency. It is voluntary, not a law. Released in January 2023, it is organized around four activities called Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. A companion guide for generative AI, called a profile, followed in July 2024. Generative AI means AI that makes new text, images or sound.
- **ISO/IEC 42001:** This standard, published in 2023, sets requirements for how an organization manages and improves its AI. It is international, and it is about an organization's processes, not about how any one model behaves.

The transparency rules connect to two of the lab's deployments. A provenance mark is the kind of mark Article 50 asks generator makers to add. The Newsroom draft, meanwhile, raises the question of who reviewed a story and takes responsibility for it.

The schedule is the part that keeps moving. The European Commission's timeline for the Act read like this on 30 September 2026. Its parts apply from these dates.

- The bans on unacceptable-risk uses applied from 2 February 2025.
- The rules for general-purpose AI models applied from 2 August 2025. These are large models built to be used for many different tasks.
- Most other rules, including Article 50, applied from 2 August 2026.
- The Annex III high-risk rules apply from 2 December 2027.

A change to the law called the Digital Omnibus on AI moved that last date later. Check the current text before relying on any of these dates, because one of them has already moved.

### More about the fairness rules

The names equal opportunity and equalized odds come from a 2016 research paper by Hardt, Price and Srebro. Both rules compare error rates group by group. That is why they can spot a problem that one overall accuracy number hides. Demographic parity is another rule from the same family of group-fairness rules. It asks a different question, so it can give a different verdict on the same model.

### More about the compute estimate

The formulas `6·N·D` and `2·N` are rules of thumb for dense models. Real systems can differ from them in several ways. Very long inputs add extra work beyond the formula. Models split into many smaller sections, called mixture-of-experts models, use only some of their parameters for each token.

The 40% figure for how hard the chips run is one fixed assumption. Real answering usually runs less efficiently than training, which brings the energy break-even sooner.
