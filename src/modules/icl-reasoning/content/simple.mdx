## The big picture

Here is a small puzzle. Look at these lines and decide which number belongs after the last arrow.

```
glim → 4
plint → 5
soravel → 7
florin → ?
```

Most people answer 6, because each number looks like the count of letters in the word before it. Nobody told you that rule. You pieced it together from three examples and then used it on a word you had never seen.

A language model, the kind of program behind a chatbot, can often do the same thing. Show it three or four examples of a made-up task and it will often do the task. Yet the settings it learned in training stay exactly the same while it reads them.

This module explains how that works. It also shows why a right answer does not always mean the model worked it out. It may have copied the answer from your message. Last, it shows what changes when a model gets to write out its steps before it answers.

### Where you have met it

Maybe you have shown a chatbot a couple of examples of the layout you wanted. If it followed them, you used this idea. This is why a prompt, the message you send to a model, can start a new job without training.

You may also have heard of reasoning models. These newer models write out a long chain of steps before they give an answer. The last part of this module looks at why writing steps down can change whether an answer is right. It also explains why you should check those steps rather than trust them blindly.

### Why it matters

Without this skill, every new task would need its own round of training. That would cost time, computing power and a large set of training examples for every single job. Prompting with a few examples is the cheapest way to point a model at something new. It would not work at all.

It also matters that you can tell what kind of success you are looking at. A model that repeats an answer from your message has not shown it can handle new cases. In the same way, a tidy list of written steps does not prove that those steps found the answer. Both mistakes are tempting when you judge a model, and this module helps you avoid them.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- How a model can pick up a task from the examples in your message. It treats them as clues, while the settings it learned in training stay the same.
- The difference between copying an answer that sits in the message and working out a rule. A real rule also fits a word the message never mentioned.
- How a scratchpad, a place to write progress down, lets a model follow a longer chain of steps. A model does a fixed amount of work per run. Each step in the chain needs the one before it. You will also see how many extra runs the scratchpad costs.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab has three cards, and each one is a small toy built by hand for this lesson:

- **Prompt as context:** This card shows the puzzle above with six possible rules beside it. You add examples one at a time and watch the rules that do not fit get crossed out. A chart shows how the possible answers are shared out.
- **Copy from context:** This card shows a copying trick that real models use. You watch it succeed on a word it has already seen. Then you watch the same trick fail on a new word.
- **Chain-of-thought scratchpad:** This card sets up a chain of lookups that is too long to finish in one go. You can switch on a scratchpad to see when writing steps down fixes the answer. You can also count the extra work it takes.

No real model runs in the lab, so every number you see comes from rules written for the lesson. That makes each result exact, so you can check it by hand. The section "Where it breaks" explains what real models do differently.

## What it is

### Three words you will need

A language model reads and writes text in small pieces called **tokens**. A token is a whole word or part of a word. It writes its reply one token at a time. A huge collection of numbers inside the model, called its **weights**, controls what it does with those tokens. The weights were adjusted again and again during training, and they stay fixed once people start using the model.

The text a model can see at any moment is called its **context**. That means your message plus everything earlier in the conversation. Keep this split in mind for the whole lesson. Changing the context changes what the model reads, but changing the weights changes the model itself.

### Learning without changing: in-context learning

Suppose you send a model a few examples of a task it was never trained on. The words are ones it has never seen. It will often carry on the pattern correctly for the next word. Its weights are exactly the same after it answers as before it read your examples. Nothing in the model was adjusted. This is called **in-context learning**. All of the "learning" lives in the context, and none of it reaches the weights.

This lesson's puzzle uses letter counting because you can check every answer by hand. Real models are actually weak at counting letters, because they see tokens rather than single letters. A real chatbot may well miscount florin. On tasks they can see clearly, though, a few examples often teach a pattern they were never trained on.

Nothing is kept afterwards, either, because the examples in the context were the only thing that changed. If you clear the conversation and ask about `florin` with no examples, the model is back where it started.

Three terms describe the different ways of asking a model to do a task:

- **Few-shot prompting:** This means giving a handful of examples, like the puzzle lines, where each "shot" is one example.
- **Demonstration:** This is each example pair in the prompt, such as `glim → 4`.
- **Zero-shot:** This means asking with an instruction and no examples. An example is "Count the letters in each word" and then `florin →`. Models trained to follow instructions handle zero-shot requests far better. Models without that training can still manage some tasks.

So how can a model do a task it was never trained on, with no change to its weights? During training it read an enormous amount of text. It became very good at continuing text in a way that fits what came before. Your examples narrow down what kind of text this is. So the best continuation is the one that carries on the pattern. Carrying on the pattern and doing the task turn out to be the same thing.

### A game of guess-my-rule

Picture a friend who starts a guessing game with you without telling you the rule. "Glim gives 4, plint gives 5, soravel gives 7," they say, "so what does florin give?" You do not study, and you do not become a different person while you play. Instead, you use each clue to throw out the guesses that do not fit. Then you answer with whatever rules are left.

The lab plays this game out in the open, so you can watch each guess get thrown out. The game is a way to picture the examples as clues. It is not a picture of what happens inside a real model. "Where it breaks" explains where the two stop matching.

### The examples are clues: task inference

The **Prompt as context** card lists six rules that someone might mean by `glim → 4`. They are "count the letters", "count distinct letters", "consonants + 1", "vowels + 3", "count consonants" and "count vowels". Here "distinct" means that each different letter is counted only once. The vowels are a, e, i, o and u. Each rule is a real calculation. The card runs it on every example word and marks the result with a ✓ or an ✗.

Watch what the first example, `glim → 4`, does to the list. Glim has four letters and none repeats, so "count the letters" and "count distinct letters" both give 4. It has three consonants and one vowel, so "consonants + 1" and "vowels + 3" also come to 4. Only "count consonants", which gives 3, and "count vowels", which gives 1, miss the answer. Those two rules are out.

The next two examples keep cutting the list down, one rule each. Plint has only one vowel, so "vowels + 3" gives 4 instead of 5 and drops out. Soravel has four consonants, so "consonants + 1" gives 5 instead of 7 and drops out as well. That leaves two rules standing: "count the letters" and "count distinct letters".

Treating the examples as evidence about which task you mean is called **task inference**. You keep only the rules that fit. Inference means reaching a conclusion from evidence, which is exactly what you do in the guessing game.

Not every example earns its place, though. The fourth example, `trelix → 6`, crosses out nothing at all. Trelix has no repeated letters, so both of the surviving rules already give 6 for it. A good example is one that rules something out.

### From rules to a guess about the answer

The card splits 100% equally among the rules still standing, and the **Weight** column shows each rule's share. With two rules left, each one gets 50%. This is a different use of the word from the model's weights. Those are the numbers fixed during training, and they never change in this lab. Each rule then makes its own prediction for the word you ask about. The lab calls that word the **Query**. The card adds those predictions up into the answer bars.

Here is how that plays out for `florin`. It has six different letters, made of four consonants and two vowels.

- **No examples:** All six rules are in play and two predict 6. The chance on 6 is two in six, about 33%.
- **After glim:** Four rules remain and two of them predict 6, so the chance rises to 50%.
- **After plint:** Three rules remain and two of them still predict 6, which gives 67%.
- **After soravel:** Only the two letter-counting rules remain and both of them predict 6, so the chance reaches 100%.

The readout **P(florin → 6)** shows this number as it changes. The P stands for probability, which means the chance that something happens.

By the third example, something interesting has happened. The card still cannot tell which of the two rules you mean. For florin, though, the two rules agree, so the answer is settled before the task is.

### When the clues run out

Now switch the Query to `letter`. For this word, "count the letters" gives 6 but "count distinct letters" gives only 4. That is because letter has two e's and two t's. None of the first four example words repeats a letter. So the two rules fit every one of them equally well. With three or four examples the card stays split, 50% on 6 and 50% on 4. The evidence cannot separate the two rules.

In the guessing game, you now have two guesses left, and both fit every clue so far. You cannot say which one your friend means until a clue arrives that splits them. The lab offers two ways to provide that clue. The first is the fifth example, `ballot → 6`. It has two l's, so "count distinct letters" gives 5 and drops out.

The other way is to switch **Prompt strategy** from **Examples only** to **State a rule**. This puts an instruction at the top of the prompt. The instruction says "Count the letters in each word". So it gives the whole starting share to the rule "count the letters". The table then marks the other rules as "ruled out by the instruction". Rules that an example already knocked out stay marked that way. In this lab, a single stated rule is worth several examples.

### Copying is not the same as working it out

Models also have a much more direct way to produce an answer, which is to copy one. Inside a model, parts called attention heads look back over the earlier text. They pull information from the spots that best match what they are searching for. One kind is called an **induction head**. It searches for an earlier spot where the current token appeared and copies whatever came right after it.

The **Copy from context** card runs a hand-built induction head over the puzzle prompt. The last token of the prompt is the arrow after the Query word. So the head looks for earlier arrows. It pays attention to whatever came right after each of those arrows. That is always an earlier answer, and it copies it.

The head is really looking for the pair it is sitting on. That pair is the Query word followed by an arrow. A line that started with the Query word has its answer right after a perfect match of that pair. So that answer gets a much larger share of the head's attention than any other.

Watch it work on `soravel` with at least three examples showing. Then the line `soravel → 7` is already in the prompt. The head puts more than 90% of its attention on the 7 at the end of that line. Then it copies it. It reaches the right answer without counting a single letter.

Now switch to `florin` at four examples, and the trick falls apart. No earlier line starts with florin, so the head has nothing to match. The four earlier answers all tie, and each one's bar sits at about 24%. That is a little under a quarter. A small amount of attention always leaks onto the other words and arrows. The head has copied the format, a number after an arrow. Nothing in it has looked at how many letters florin has.

Push it one step further. At five examples the bar for 6 climbs to 38%, which looks like progress. It happens only because two of the earlier answers, the ones for trelix and ballot, are both 6. A taller bar for the right answer can be pure luck about which answers sit in the prompt.

Picture a classmate who has the practice test's answer sheet lying open on the desk. They copy from it instead of solving anything. If a test question matches a line on the sheet, they copy that answer and look like an expert. For a new question, all they can do is hedge between the answers on the sheet. By luck, the right one is sometimes there.

Here the picture stops matching. A classmate might remember a few answers after the sheet is gone, but the head keeps nothing. Clear the prompt and the trick is gone.

Induction heads are real and well studied. They make sense for the text models are trained on. That text is full of patterns that are set up and then continued. Researchers have found that induction heads appear in training just as models get better at learning from examples. Copying explains the format and exact repeats. Applying a rule to a brand-new word needs more than this one part.

### Writing the steps down

Each time a model writes one token, it sends the whole context through a fixed stack of processing stages. These stages are called layers. One complete run like this is called a **forward pass**, and it ends with exactly one new token. The number of layers never changes, so each pass can do only a fixed amount of work.

Some tasks are a chain in which every step needs the result of the step before it. This is called **serial computation**. In a chain like this, step three cannot start until step two is finished. If one pass can link only so many of these steps, a longer chain does not fit inside it.

Picture adding up a long column of numbers in your head. You can keep a running total for the first few, but you lose track somewhere along the way. With a pencil, you write the running total down every few numbers and carry on from what you wrote. The column can then be as long as you like.

A model can do something similar by writing its partial results as tokens before its final answer. Those in-between tokens are called a **chain of thought**. The text a model writes for itself before it answers is called its **scratchpad**. The scratchpad sits in the context, so the next pass can read the latest partial result. It carries on from there instead of starting over. In this way the chain can stretch across as many passes as it needs.

Spending extra work while a model answers, rather than while it trains, is called **test-time compute**. Here "test time" means whenever the model is answering. Compute means the amount of calculating work. Writing a chain of thought is one way to spend it. A mathematical study published in 2024 showed why this can matter so much. Under some widely used assumptions, a model with a fixed number of layers can solve some step-by-step problems. It can do so only if it may write out its steps.

### The lab's arrow puzzle

The **Chain-of-thought scratchpad** card turns this into a puzzle you can watch. Its prompt holds a table of ten arrows. Each arrow, such as C → H or H → A, points from one letter to exactly one other letter. The task is to start at C and follow the arrows a set number of times. Following one arrow is called a hop, and the number of hops is the lab's **Hops k**.

Starting from C, the path through the table runs like this:

```
C → H → A → J → E → B → G → D → I
```

Each hop is the same find-and-copy step that the induction head performs. You find the current letter in the table and copy the letter its arrow points to. You cannot jump ahead, because you only learn the next letter once you have found the current one.

To make the limit visible, the lab adds a rule called the **Per-pass budget b**. One pass can follow at most b arrows before it must write a token. That limit is a rule invented for the lesson, and it was not measured from a real model.

Here is the walk-through the card is built around. Set **Hops k** to 6 and **Per-pass budget b** to 3. If you count six steps along the path above, you land on G, which is the correct answer.

- With **Scratchpad** off, the model gets a single pass. It follows C → H → A → J and uses up its budget. It has to write something at that point, so it answers J, which is wrong.
- With **Scratchpad** on, pass 1 follows the same three arrows to J. This time it writes J as a scratch note, not as an answer. Pass 2 reads that J back from the context and follows J → E → B → G. It answers G, which is right.

The lookups inside a pass are silent, which means they are never written down. In the lab, the next pass reads only what was written. So the written J is the only thing that carries the progress forward. The letter says where the chain has reached. One scratch note is already there, which tells pass 2 that three hops are done and three are left.

You might think any extra tokens would help, since each one gives the model another pass. But filler, such as a row of dots, carries no partial result. The next pass has nothing to carry on from, so the chain does not move forward.

### What the scratchpad costs

The fix is not free. Every pass writes one token, and each pass takes its own time to run. The number of passes the scratchpad needs is k divided by b, rounded up to the next whole number. For example, with k at 7 and b at 3, the division gives two and a third. Rounding up makes that three passes, because the last hop still needs a pass of its own.

The card's info panel writes the same rule as `ceil(k / b)`. Here ceil is short for ceiling, and it means round up. At the other extreme, b can be 1. Then every hop needs its own pass, so a chain of eight hops takes eight passes.

The pencil-and-paper picture is a good guide to why writing helps. It stops matching in a couple of places, which "Where it breaks" sets out.

## Why it’s here

### A new job without new training

Changing a model's weights to teach it a task is called fine-tuning. It means running another round of training with its own examples, time and computing power. Suppose four example lines in the prompt get you the behavior you want. Then a training run is an expensive way to buy it. That is why "we should fine-tune a model for this" is usually the wrong first instinct.

Picture a small team that wants a model to rewrite messy dates into one neat format. One person starts planning a training run. Another pastes four before-and-after examples into the prompt and tries that first. If the examples work, the team has spent a few lines of text instead of a training run. They can also change the format in seconds whenever they change their minds about how the dates should look.

### Telling copying from working it out

You saw that soravel can be answered by copying while florin cannot. This matters whenever people test a model. A test that shares words with the examples in the prompt makes a model look better than it is. Some of its right answers were copied. A fair test always includes cases the prompt has never shown, like florin rather than soravel.

### Chains that are too long for one pass

A model does a fixed amount of work in each pass. A chain of dependent steps that is longer than that amount cannot be finished in one go. In the lab, a pass that runs out of budget answers with wherever it got to. That answer is wrong. Writing partial results down gets around this. So a chain of thought can change what a model gets right, and not only how it explains itself.

### Steps that look like working

The written steps raise a sharper question. They are text produced the same way as everything else the model writes, one token at a time. They look like the model showing its working. But you have to check with evidence how closely they match what produced the answer. Do not take it on trust.

## How to play with it

Work through the cards from top to bottom. Make a prediction before each change so you can test it.

1. **Try this:** On **Prompt as context**, set **Examples** to 0 with the **Query** on `florin`. Read the answer bars. 2. Set **Query** to `letter` and **Examples** to 4. Then read the small badge next to the card's heading, which now says "2 of 6 rules fit". Then go back to four examples and switch **Prompt strategy** to **State a rule**. This settles the question without the fifth example.
3. Switch **Prompt strategy** back to **Examples only**. Then, on **Copy from context**, keep **Examples** at 3 or more and set **Query** to `soravel`. Read the heading, the shading and percentages under the tokens, and the "Induction head's predicted next token" bars. Then switch to `florin` and compare, first at four examples and then at five.

The **Examples**, **Prompt strategy** and **Query** controls live on the first card. They also change the **Copy from context** card, because both cards read the same prompt.

As a reminder, the **Weight** column shows each rule's share of the 100%. The **Weights changed** readout is about the model's own weights, the numbers set during training.

## What to notice

- **Weights changed** always reads 0, because only the prompt ever changes. The mixed-up spread of answers at zero examples and the certain answer at three came from one fixed calculation.
- A good example is one that rules something out. Trelix shows the opposite: it adds a column to the table but removes nothing new.
- An answer can be settled before the task is. For florin, the answer 6 is certain. Yet the card still cannot tell which of the two remaining rules you mean.
- Copying reproduces the format, a number after an arrow, without working anything out about a new word. A taller bar for the right answer can be a coincidence of which answers sit in the prompt.
- With **Scratchpad** off, the answer is right exactly when k is at most b. At b = 3, chains of one to three hops are right. Every chain from four to eight hops fails.
- With **Scratchpad** on, every chain is right, and the price is paid in passes. At b = 3, chains of up to three hops take one pass. Chains of four to six take two, and chains of seven or eight take three.
- Once b reaches k, both settings answer in one pass. So writing steps down pays off only past the limit of a single pass.
- The silent lookups in each row of the passes table never reach the next pass. Only the token a pass writes does. That is why a partial result has to be written down before the next pass can build on it.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- **Prompt as context:** The six rules, the examples and the Query words were all written for the lesson. The examples never contain a mistake. Every rule starts with an equal share, unless you state a rule and give it the whole share. A real model faces a vast range of possible tasks. Its training decides which ones it leans toward before it sees any examples.
- **Crossing out rules:** This is a clean way to picture a prompt as evidence. It is not how a real model calculates on the inside.
- **Copy from context:** There is one induction head with hand-chosen settings, and it works on whole words. Nothing was trained. No tokenizer, the tool a real model uses to chop text into tokens, runs at all.
- **Chain-of-thought scratchpad:** The budget b is a number the lab sets, and it was not measured from a model. The table, the task and every step are exact, so no pass ever makes a mistake. No model runs. When a pass runs out of budget, the lab has it answer with wherever it got to. That is the toy's own choice and not measured behavior.
- **Honest steps:** In the lab, the written steps are always honest. Each pass can read only what was written. The letters on the scratchpad really are what carries the answer. A real model's written reasoning makes no such promise.

### Where the two pictures stop matching

The guess-my-rule game gives the model too much credit in one way. A person playing it knows they are in a game and remembers the rule afterwards. A model has no awareness of a task at all. It produces a very good continuation of a pattern, and it keeps nothing once the conversation is cleared.

The pencil-and-paper picture also needs a warning. Your limit comes from memory and tiredness, while a model's limit per pass is built into its layers. Your written totals can be wrong, too. So can a real model's written steps, even though the lab's never are.

### Where real in-context learning goes wrong

Real in-context learning is fussier than the lab suggests. Details that should not matter can still move its accuracy by several percentage points. These include the order of the examples, their formatting and the wording of their answers. Even extra spaces or line breaks can matter. A change of several points means several more or fewer right answers out of every hundred questions. The effect is called **prompt sensitivity**.

Picture a student whose quiz score changes when the same questions are printed in a different order. The questions did not get harder, yet the result moved. A model can behave the same way with its examples.

Researchers have also found something odd about **demonstrations**. Their layout, and the set of possible answers they show, can matter more than whether each answer is correct. Picture a prompt that labels movie reviews as "happy" or "sad". Some of the labels have been swapped to the wrong one. A model often still does nearly as well. The examples need to keep the same layout and use the same two labels.

The examples seem to tell the model what kind of answer to give more than which answer is right. That sits awkwardly with the neat story that a model learns the rule from its examples. The lab's first card tells that story.

Piling more text into a prompt has limits too. The first is the **context window**, which is the most text a model can look at in one go. It is measured in tokens, and it is working space rather than lasting memory. Models can get worse at using information buried in the middle of a long context. Adding more examples eventually stops helping.

### Where real chains of thought go wrong

- **Per-pass limit:** A real model's limit per pass comes from its depth, meaning its number of layers. Its training also matters. It is not a clean number like b, and it differs from one task to another.
- **Mistakes spread:** Written steps can be wrong, and one mistake spoils every step that follows. In the arrow puzzle, a wrong letter on the scratchpad would send each later pass down the wrong path. You cannot try this on the card. A test in the lab's code does change one scratch letter, though, and the final answer changes with it.
- **Uneven help:** Studies comparing many tasks found that writing steps down helps most on math and symbol puzzles. It helps much less on other kinds of task.
- **Slower answers:** Every written token costs time, so a long chain of thought makes the answer slower to arrive.

**A common mix-up:** The reasoning a model writes out is not a record of how it got its answer. A detail in the prompt can sway a model. It may then write a tidy explanation that never mentions that detail. Picture a student who copies an answer from a friend and then writes neat working that leads to it. The working looks convincing, but it is not how the answer was reached.

The steps do not have to be deliberately misleading for this to happen. They can leave out what actually drove the answer. Whether a written trace matches what produced the answer is called **faithfulness**. Nothing guarantees it in a real model. Treat the trace as another output to check, and not as an explanation to trust.

## Going deeper

### Other ways to spend time on an answer

Writing steps down is one way to spend test-time compute. Others spread the work across several attempts instead of along one chain:

- **Voting:** Ask the model the same question several times and take a vote on the most common answer.
- **Checking:** Produce several different answers and let a separate checker pick the best one. This works very well when a checker can be certain an answer is right, such as by running code.

The **Reasoning models** module works out what each of these costs, including the cases where voting makes things worse.

A different idea works during training instead of while the model answers. It rewards each correct step and not only the final answer. This is called **process supervision**.

### Models trained to write long chains

Recent reasoning models were trained with reinforcement learning, a kind of training that rewards good behavior. Here the model was rewarded when its final answer could be checked and turned out to be right. As a result, writing long chains of steps became learned behavior. A prompt no longer has to ask for it. The **Reasoning models** module looks at these models. The **Interpretability II: circuits** module switches off an induction head and measures what breaks.

Interpretability II also uses the word faithfulness, but there it means something different. It asks whether a small group of the model's parts can reproduce a behavior on their own.

### Three ways to change what a model does

Keep these three apart, because they sound alike but do very different things:

- **In-context learning:** You put examples in the context. The model's temporary numbers, called activations, change as it reads them. Its weights do not, so nothing about it is permanent.
- **Fine-tuning:** This changes the weights themselves, so the change stays with the model after the conversation ends.
- **Retrieval:** A search tool finds documents and adds them to the context for you. Like in-context learning, it changes only what the model reads. It brings in facts, not examples of a task.

Only fine-tuning lasts, and only retrieval gives you a source you can go and check.

### Where the facts come from

- **Induction heads:** The link between induction heads and learning from examples comes from Olsson and colleagues, 2022.
- **Fixed layers:** The proof about fixed layers and written steps comes from Li and colleagues, 2024.
- **Demonstration layout:** The finding that layout can matter more than correct answers is from Min and colleagues, 2022.
- **Math and symbols:** The comparison showing chains of thought help most on math is from Sprague and colleagues, 2024.
