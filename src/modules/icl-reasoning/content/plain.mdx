## The big picture

To start, here is a small puzzle: look at these lines and decide which number belongs after the last
arrow.

```
glim → 4
plint → 5
soravel → 7
florin → ?
```

Most people answer 6, because each number seems to be the count of letters in the word before it.
Nobody told you that rule, though, so you pieced it together from three examples and then used it on a
word you had never seen before.

A language model, the kind of program behind a chatbot, can often do the same thing. Show it three or
four examples of a made-up task and it will often do the task. Yet the settings it learned in training
stay exactly the same as it reads them.

This module explains how that works, and why a right answer does
not always mean the model worked it out, since it may have copied the answer from your message. It also
shows what changes when a model is allowed to write out its steps before it answers.

### Where you have met it

If you have ever given a chatbot a couple of examples of the layout you wanted and watched it follow
them, you have used this idea. It is the reason that a well-written prompt, the message you send to a
model, can get it to do a new job without any extra training.

You may also have heard of reasoning models, a newer kind of model that writes out a long chain of steps
before it gives an answer. The last part of this module looks at why writing steps down can change
whether an answer is right. It also explains why those written steps deserve a careful look rather than
blind trust.

### Why it matters

Without this ability to learn from examples in the prompt, every new task would need its own round of
training. That would cost time, computing power and a large set of training examples for every single
job. Prompting with a few examples, which is the cheapest way to point a model at something new, would
not work at all.

It also matters that you can tell what kind of success you are looking at. A model that repeats an
answer it can already see in your message has not shown that it can handle a new case. In the same way,
a tidy list of written steps does not prove that those steps are how the answer was reached. Both are
tempting mistakes when you judge what a model can do, and this module shows you how to avoid them.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- How a model can pick up a task from the examples in your message by treating them as clues, while the
  settings it learned in training stay exactly the same.
- The difference between copying an answer that already sits in the message and working out a rule that
  also fits a word the message never mentioned.
- How writing progress down, in what is called a scratchpad, lets a model that does a fixed amount of
  work per run follow a longer chain of steps. Each step in the chain needs the one before it, and you
  will see how many extra runs the scratchpad costs.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab has three cards, and each one is a small toy that was built by hand for this lesson:

- **Prompt as context** shows the puzzle above with six possible rules beside it. You add examples one
  at a time and watch the rules that do not fit get crossed out, while a chart shows how the possible
  answers are shared out.
- **Copy from context** shows a copying trick that real models use, and lets you watch it succeed on a
  word it has already seen. Then you can watch the same trick fail on a word it has never seen.
- **Chain-of-thought scratchpad** sets a chain of lookups that is too long to finish in one go. You can
  switch on a scratchpad to see when writing steps down fixes the answer, and count the extra work it

No real model runs anywhere in the lab, so every number you see comes from rules written for the lesson.
That makes each result exact and something you can check by hand, while the section "Where it breaks"
explains what real models do differently.

## What it is

### Three words you will need

A language model reads and writes text in small pieces called **tokens**, which are whole words or parts
of words, and it produces its reply one token at a time. What it does with those tokens is controlled by
a huge collection of numbers inside it, called its **weights**. Those numbers were adjusted again and
again while the model was trained, and once training ends they stay fixed while people use the model.

The text a model can see at any moment, meaning your message plus everything earlier in the
conversation, is called its **context**. Keep this split in mind for the whole lesson: changing the
context changes what the model is reading, while changing the weights changes the model itself.

### Learning without changing: in-context learning

Suppose you send a model a few examples of a task it was never trained on, using words it has never
seen. It will often carry on the pattern correctly for the next word. Its weights are exactly the same
after it answers as they were before it read your examples, because nothing in the model was adjusted at
all. This is called **in-context learning**, since all of the "learning" lives in the context and none
of it reaches the weights.

This lesson's puzzle uses letter counting because you can check every answer by hand. Real models are
actually weak at counting letters, because they see tokens rather than single letters, so a real chatbot
may well miscount florin. On tasks they can see clearly, though, a few examples are often enough for them
to pick up a pattern they were never trained on.

Nothing is kept afterwards, either, because the examples in the context were the only thing that
changed. If you clear the conversation and ask about `florin` with no examples, the model is back where
it started.

Three terms describe the different ways of asking a model to do a task:

- Giving a handful of examples, like the puzzle lines, is called **few-shot prompting**, where each
  "shot" is one example.
- Each example pair in the prompt, such as `glim → 4`, is called a **demonstration**.
- Asking with an instruction and no examples at all, such as "Count the letters in each word" followed
  by `florin →`, is called **zero-shot**. Models that were trained to follow instructions handle
  zero-shot requests far better, although models without that training can still manage some tasks.

So how can a model do a task it was never trained on, without any change to its weights? During training it took
in an enormous amount of text and became very good at continuing text in a way that fits what came
before.

Your examples narrow down what kind of text this is, so the continuation that fits best is the
one that carries on the pattern. Carrying on the pattern and doing the task turn out to be the same
thing.

### A game of guess-my-rule

Picture a friend who starts a guessing game with you, without telling you the rule. "Glim gives 4, plint
gives 5, soravel gives 7," they say, "so what does florin give?" You do not study, and you do not become
a different person while you play. Instead you use each clue to throw out the guesses that do not fit,
and then you answer with whatever rules are left.

The lab plays this game out in the open, so you can watch each guess get thrown out. The game is a way
to picture the examples as clues. It is not a picture of what happens inside a real model, and "Where it
breaks" explains where the two stop matching.

### The examples are clues: task inference

The **Prompt as context** card lists six rules that someone might mean by `glim → 4`. They are "count the
letters", "count distinct letters", "consonants + 1", "vowels + 3", "count consonants" and "count
vowels". Here "distinct" means that each different letter is counted only once, and the vowels are a, e,
i, o and u. Each rule is a real calculation, so the card runs it on every example word and marks the
result with a ✓ or an ✗.

Watch what the first example, `glim → 4`, does to the list. Glim has four letters and none of them
repeats, so "count the letters" and "count distinct letters" both give 4. It has three consonants and
one vowel, so "consonants + 1" and "vowels + 3" also come to 4. Only "count consonants", which gives 3,
and "count vowels", which gives 1, miss the answer, so those two rules are out.

The next two examples keep cutting the list down, one rule each. Plint has only one vowel, so "vowels +
3" gives 4 instead of 5 and drops out. Soravel has four consonants, so "consonants + 1" gives 5 instead
of 7 and drops out as well. That leaves two rules standing: "count the letters" and "count distinct
letters".

Treating the examples as evidence about which task you mean, and keeping only the rules that fit, is
called **task inference**. The word inference means reaching a conclusion from evidence, which is exactly
what you do in the guessing game.

Not every example earns its place, though, and the fourth example, `trelix → 6`, crosses out nothing at
all. Trelix has no repeated letters, so both of the rules that survived soravel already give 6 for it. A
good example is one that rules something out.

### From rules to a guess about the answer

The card splits 100% equally among the rules still standing, and the **Weight** column shows each
rule's share. This is a different use of the word from the model's weights, the numbers fixed during
training, which never change in this lab. With two rules left, for example, each one gets 50%. Each rule then makes its own
prediction for the word you are asking about, which the lab calls the **Query**, and those predictions
are added up into the answer bars.

Here is how that plays out for `florin`, a word with six different letters, made of four consonants and
two vowels:

- With no examples, all six rules are still in play and two of them predict 6, so the chance on 6 is two
  out of six, about 33%.
- After glim, four rules remain and two of them predict 6, so the chance rises to 50%.
- After plint, three rules remain and two of them still predict 6, which gives 67%.
- After soravel, only the two letter-counting rules remain and both of them predict 6, so the chance
  reaches 100%.

The readout **P(florin → 6)** shows this number as it changes, where the P stands for probability,
meaning the chance that something happens.

By the third example, something interesting has happened: the card still cannot tell which of the two
rules you mean. For florin, though, the two rules agree, so the answer is settled before the task is.

### When the clues run out

Now switch the Query to `letter`, a word where "count the letters" gives 6 but "count distinct letters"
gives only 4, because letter has two e's and two t's. None of the first four example words repeats a
letter, so these two rules fit every one of them equally well. With three or four examples the card
stays split, 50% on 6 and 50% on 4, because the evidence cannot separate the two rules.

In the guessing game, this is the moment when you have two guesses left and both fit every clue so far.
You cannot say which one your friend means until a clue arrives that splits them, and the lab offers two
ways to provide that clue. The first is the fifth example, `ballot → 6`, which has two l's, so "count distinct letters"
gives 5 and drops out.

The other way is to switch **Prompt strategy** from **Examples only** to **State a rule**, which puts an
instruction at the top of the prompt. The instruction says "Count the letters in each word", so it gives
the whole starting share to the rule "count the letters". The table then marks the other rules as "ruled
out by the instruction", unless an example has already knocked them out. In this lab, then, a single
stated rule is worth several examples.

### Copying is not the same as working it out

Models also have a much more direct way to produce an answer, which is to copy one. Inside a model,
parts called attention heads look back over the earlier text and pull information from the spots that
best match what they are searching for. One kind, called an **induction head**, searches for an earlier
spot where the current token appeared and copies whatever came right after it.

The **Copy from context** card runs a hand-built induction head over the puzzle prompt. The last token of
the prompt is the arrow after the Query word, so the head looks for earlier arrows. It pays attention to
whatever came right after each of those arrows, which is always an earlier answer, and that is what it
copies.

The head is really looking for the same pair it is sitting on, which is the Query word followed by an
arrow. On a line that started with the same word as the Query, the answer comes right after a perfect
match of that pair. So that answer gets a much larger share of the head's attention than any other.

Watch it work on `soravel` with at least three examples showing, which means the line `soravel → 7` is
already in the prompt. The head puts more than 90% of its attention on the 7 at the end of that line and
copies it, reaching the right answer without counting a single letter.

Switch to `florin` at four examples and the trick falls apart, because no earlier line starts with
florin. With nothing to match, the four earlier answers all tie, and each one's bar sits at about 24%.
That is a little under a quarter, because a small amount of attention always leaks onto the other words
and arrows.
The head has copied the format, a number after an arrow, but nothing in it has looked at how many letters
florin has.

Push it one step further, and at five examples the bar for 6 climbs to 38%, which looks like progress.
It happens only because two of the earlier answers, the ones for trelix and ballot, are both 6. A taller
bar for the right answer can be pure luck about which answers sit in the prompt.

Picture a classmate who has the practice test's answer sheet lying open on the desk and copies from it
instead of solving anything. If a question on the real test matches a line on the sheet, they copy that
answer instantly and look like an expert. For a new question, all they can do is hedge between the
answers on the sheet, which will sometimes include the right one by luck.

Here the picture stops matching. A classmate might remember a few answers after the sheet is gone, but
the head keeps nothing. Clear the prompt and the trick is gone.

Induction heads are real and well studied, and they make sense for the text models are trained on, which
is full of patterns that are set up and then continued. Researchers have found that induction heads
appear during training at about the same time as models get much better at learning from examples in the
prompt. Copying explains the format and exact repeats, but applying a rule to a brand-new word needs more
than this one part.

### Writing the steps down

Each time a model writes one token, it sends the whole context through a fixed stack of processing
stages, called layers, from the first to the last. One complete run like this is called a **forward
pass**, and it ends with exactly one new token. Because the number of layers never changes, each pass
can do only a fixed amount of work.

Some tasks are a chain in which every step needs the result of the step before it, which is called
**serial computation**. In a chain like this, step three cannot start until step two is finished. If one
pass can link only so many of these steps, a longer chain does not fit inside it.

Picture adding up a long column of numbers in your head, where you can keep a running total for the
first few but lose track somewhere along the way. With a pencil, you write the running total down every
few numbers and carry on from what you wrote, so the column can be as long as you like.

A model can do something similar by writing its partial results as tokens before its final answer, and
those in-between tokens are called a **chain of thought**. The text a model writes for itself before it
answers is called its **scratchpad**. Because the scratchpad sits in the context, the next pass can read
the latest partial result and carry on from it instead of starting over. In this way the chain can
stretch across as many passes as it needs.

Spending extra work while a model answers, rather than while it trains, is called **test-time compute**.
Here "test time" means whenever the model is being used to answer, and compute means the amount of
calculating work.

Writing a chain of thought is one way to spend it,
and a mathematical study published in 2024 showed why it can matter so much. Under some widely used
assumptions, a model with a fixed number of layers can solve some step-by-step problems only when it is
allowed to write out its steps.

### The lab's arrow puzzle

The **Chain-of-thought scratchpad** card turns this into a puzzle you can watch, using a table of ten
arrows in its prompt. Each arrow, such as C → H or H → A, points from one letter to exactly one other
letter. The task is to start at C and follow the arrows a set number of times. Following one arrow is
called a hop, and the number of hops is the lab's **Hops k**.

Starting from C, the path through the table runs like this:

```
C → H → A → J → E → B → G → D → I
```

Each hop is the same find-and-copy step that the induction head performs: find the current letter in the
table and copy the letter its arrow points to. You cannot jump ahead, because you only learn the next
letter once you have found the current one.

To make the limit visible, the lab adds a rule called the **Per-pass budget b**: one pass can follow at
most b arrows before it must write a token. That limit is a rule invented for the lesson, not something
measured from a real model.

Here is the walk-through the card is built around, with **Hops k** set to 6 and **Per-pass budget b** set
to 3. If you count six steps along the path above, you land on G, which is the correct answer.

- With **Scratchpad** off, the model gets a single pass, which follows C → H → A → J and uses up its
  budget. It has to write something at that point, so it answers J, which is wrong.
- With **Scratchpad** on, pass 1 follows the same three arrows to J, but this time it writes J as a
  scratch note instead of an answer. Pass 2 reads that J back from the context, follows J → E → B → G,
  and answers G, which is right.

The lookups inside a pass are silent, which means they are never written down. In the lab, the next pass
reads only what was written, so the written J is the only thing that carries the progress forward. The
letter says where the chain has reached, and the fact that one scratch note is already there tells pass 2
that three hops are done and three are left.

You might think any extra tokens would help, since each one gives the model another pass. But filler,
such as a row of dots, carries no partial result, so the next pass has nothing to carry on from and the
chain does not move forward.

### What the scratchpad costs

The fix is not free, because every pass writes one token and each pass takes its own time to run. The
number of passes the scratchpad needs is k divided by b, rounded up to the next whole number. For
example, with k at 7 and b at 3, the division gives two and a third. Rounding up makes that three
passes, because the last hop still needs a pass of its own.

The card's info panel writes the same rule as `ceil(k / b)`, where ceil is short for ceiling and means
round up. At the other extreme, with b at 1, every hop needs its own pass, so a chain of eight hops takes
eight passes.

The pencil-and-paper picture is a good guide to why writing helps, but it stops matching in a couple of
places, which "Where it breaks" sets out.

## Why it’s here

### A new job without new training

Changing a model's weights to teach it a task is called fine-tuning, and it means running another round
of training with its own examples, time and computing power. If four example lines in the prompt get you
the behavior you want, a training run is an expensive way to buy it. That is why "we should fine-tune a
model for this" is usually the wrong first instinct.

Picture a small team that wants a model to rewrite messy dates into one neat format. One person starts
planning a training run, while another pastes four before-and-after examples into the prompt and tries
that first. If the examples work, the team has spent a few lines of text rather than a training run.
They can also change the format in seconds whenever they change their minds about how the dates should
look.

### Telling copying from working it out

You saw that soravel can be answered by copying while florin cannot, and this matters whenever people
test a model. A test that shares words with the examples in the prompt will make a model look better than
it is, because some of its right answers were copied. A fair test always includes cases the prompt has
never shown, like florin rather than soravel.

### Chains that are too long for one pass

A model does a fixed amount of work in each pass, so a chain of dependent steps that is longer than that
amount cannot be finished in one go. In the lab, a pass that runs out of budget answers with wherever it
got to, and that answer is wrong. Writing partial results down gets around this, which means a chain of
thought can change what a model gets right, not only how it explains itself.

### Steps that look like working

The written steps raise a sharper question, because they are text produced in the same way as everything
else the model writes, one token at a time. They look like the model showing its working, but how closely
they match what really produced the answer has to be checked with evidence, not taken on trust.

## What to notice

- **Weights changed** always reads 0, because only the prompt ever changes. The confused spread of
  answers at zero examples and the certain answer at three examples both came from the same fixed
  calculation.
- A good example is one that rules something out, and trelix shows the opposite: it adds a column to the
  table but removes nothing new.
- An answer can be settled before the task is. For florin, the answer 6 is certain while the card still
  cannot tell which of the two remaining rules you mean.
- Copying reproduces the format, a number after an arrow, without working anything out about a new word.
  A taller bar for the right answer can be a coincidence of which answers happen to be in the prompt.
- With **Scratchpad** off, the answer is right exactly when k is at most b. At b = 3, chains of one to
  three hops are right, while every chain from four to eight hops fails.
- With **Scratchpad** on, every chain is right, and the price is paid in passes. At b = 3, chains of up to
  three hops take one pass, chains of four to six take two, and chains of seven or eight take three.
- Once b reaches k, both settings answer in one pass, so writing steps down pays off only past the limit
  of a single pass.
- The silent lookups in each row of the passes table never reach the next pass, because only the token a
  pass writes does. That is why a partial result has to be written down before the next pass can build
  on it.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- On **Prompt as context**, the six rules, the examples and the Query words were all written for the
  lesson. The examples never contain a mistake, and every rule starts with an equal share unless you
  state a rule, which gives it all to one rule. A real model
  faces a vast range of possible tasks, and which ones it leans toward before seeing any examples comes
  from its training.
- Crossing out rules is a clean way to picture a prompt as evidence, but it is not how a real model
  calculates on the inside.
- On **Copy from context**, there is a single induction head whose settings were chosen by hand, and it
  works on whole words. Nothing was trained, and no tokenizer, the tool a real model uses to chop text
  into tokens, runs at all.
- On **Chain-of-thought scratchpad**, the budget b is a number the lab sets, not something measured from
  a model. The table, the task and every step are exact, so no pass ever makes a mistake, and no model
  runs. When a pass runs out of budget, the lab has it answer with wherever it got to, which is the toy's
  own choice rather than measured behavior.
- In the lab, the written steps are always honest, because the lab is built so that each pass can read
  only what was written. The letters on the scratchpad really are what carries the answer, while a real
  model's written reasoning makes no such promise.

### Where the two pictures stop matching

The guess-my-rule game gives the model too much credit in one way. A person playing it knows they are in
a game and remembers the rule afterwards, while a model has no awareness of a task at all. It produces a
very good continuation of a pattern, and it keeps nothing once the conversation is cleared.

The pencil-and-paper picture also needs a warning, because your limit comes from memory and tiredness,
while a model's limit per pass is built into its layers. Your written totals can be wrong, too, and so
can a real model's written steps, even though the lab's never are.

### Where real in-context learning goes wrong

Real in-context learning is fussier than the lab suggests, because details that should not matter can
still move its accuracy by several percentage points. These include the order of the examples, their
formatting, the wording of their answers, and even extra spaces or line breaks. A change of several
points means several more or fewer right answers out of every hundred questions, and the effect is called
**prompt sensitivity**.

Picture a student whose quiz score changes when the same questions are printed in a different order. The
questions did not get harder, yet the result moved, and a model can behave the same way with its
examples.

Researchers have also found that the layout of the **demonstrations**, and the set of possible answers
they show, can matter more than whether each example's answer is correct. Picture a prompt that labels
movie reviews as "happy" or "sad", where some of the labels have been swapped to the wrong one. A model
often still does nearly as well, as long as the examples keep the same layout and use the same two
labels.

The examples seem to tell the model what kind of answer to give more than which answer is right. That
sits awkwardly with the neat story that a model learns the rule from its examples, the way the lab's
first card does.

Piling more text into a prompt has limits too, starting with the **context window**, which is the most
text a model can look at in one go. It is measured in tokens, and it is working space rather than lasting
memory. Models can get worse at using information buried in the middle of a long context, and adding
more examples eventually stops helping.

### Where real chains of thought go wrong

- A real model's limit per pass comes from its depth, meaning its number of layers, and from its training.
  It is not a clean number like b, and it differs from one task to another.
- Written steps can be wrong, and one mistake spoils every step that follows. In the arrow puzzle, a wrong
  letter on the scratchpad would send each later pass down the wrong path. You cannot try this on the
  card, but a test in the lab's code changes one scratch letter and shows that the final answer changes
  with it.
- Studies comparing many tasks found that writing steps down helps most on math and symbol puzzles, and
  much less on other kinds of task.
- Every written token costs time, so a long chain of thought makes the answer slower to arrive.

**A common mix-up:** the reasoning a model writes out is not a record of how it got its answer. A model can
be swayed by some detail in the prompt and then write a tidy explanation that never mentions that detail.
Picture a student who copies an answer from a friend and then writes neat working that leads to it. The
working looks convincing, but it is not how the answer was reached.

The steps do not have to be deliberately misleading for this to happen. They can simply leave out what
actually drove the answer. Whether a written trace matches what produced the answer is called **faithfulness**, and
nothing guarantees it in a real model. Treat the trace as another output to check, not as an explanation
to trust.

## Going deeper

### Other ways to spend time on an answer

Writing steps down is one way to spend test-time compute, and there are others that spread the work
across several attempts instead of along one chain:

- Ask the model the same question several times and take a vote on the most common answer.
- Produce several different answers and let a separate checker pick the best one. This works very well
  when the checker can tell for certain whether an answer is right, such as running a piece of code to
  see whether it works.

The **Reasoning models** module works out what each of these costs, including the cases where voting
makes things worse.

A different idea works during training rather than while the model answers. It rewards each correct step
rather than only the final answer, which is called **process supervision**.

### Models trained to write long chains

Recent reasoning models were trained with reinforcement learning, a kind of training that rewards good
behavior. Here the model was rewarded when its final answer could be checked and turned out to be right.
As a result, writing long chains of steps became learned behavior rather than something a prompt has to
ask for. The **Reasoning models** module looks at these models, and the **Interpretability II:
circuits** module switches off an induction head and measures what breaks.

Interpretability II also uses the word faithfulness, but there it means something different: whether a
small group of the model's parts can reproduce a behavior on their own.

### Three ways to change what a model does

Keep these three apart, because they sound alike but do very different things:

- In-context learning: you put examples in the context. The model's temporary numbers, called
  activations, change as it reads them, but its weights do not, so nothing about it is permanent.
- Fine-tuning: this changes the weights themselves, so the change stays with the model after the
  conversation ends.
- Retrieval: a search tool finds documents and adds them to the context for you. Like in-context
  learning, it changes only what the model reads, but it brings in facts rather than examples of a task.

Only fine-tuning lasts, and only retrieval gives you a source you can go and check.
