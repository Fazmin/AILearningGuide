## Two words nobody agrees on

AGI is said to be years away, already here, or incoherent; ASI is described as the best and the worst
outcome of the technology. Much of that disagreement is not about systems. It comes from one word
standing for several definitions, and from measurements, definitions, arguments and forecasts being
quoted as if they carried the same weight.

No AGI or ASI exists, so no lab can exhibit one. What this lab can do is pin each term to a precise
definition, run the arithmetic behind the strongest arguments, and show the evidence forecasts rest on.

### What you will be able to explain

- How a two-axis grading of performance and generality places narrow AI, AGI and ASI, and why a
  narrow superhuman system is not ASI.
- Why one jagged capability profile can be 57%, 20% or 0% of the way to AGI depending on the
  aggregation rule, and why a human-capped scale cannot register ASI.
- How speed, parallel copies, serial dependence and physical-world latency bound the wall-clock
  advantage of a faster mind, and what the arithmetic omits.
- The intelligence-explosion argument as a feedback loop, and how returns to research and an
  unautomatable share (a Baumol-style bottleneck) decide between runaway, exponential and
  decelerating growth.
- How to classify a claim as a measurement, definition, argument or forecast, and how question
  wording and an assumed trend move a forecast.

## Build the vocabulary, one card at a time

Six cards, one per step; **Next** under a card opens the following one.

### Two axes, not one ladder

Morris et al. (ICML 2024) grade AI on two axes. Performance runs from level 0 (no AI) through
Emerging, Competent (at least the 50th percentile of skilled adults), Expert (90th), Exceptional (99th)
to Superhuman (outperforms 100% of humans). Generality is narrow or general.

**Pause and predict:** has any AI system already outperformed every human at something?

Yes, narrowly. Table 1 places AlphaFold, AlphaZero and Stockfish at level 5 narrow. In the general
column it places ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2 and Gemini at level 1 (Emerging AGI) and marks every higher
general cell "not yet achieved". Level 2 general, Competent AGI, is in the authors' words probably the
best catch-all for many existing definitions of AGI. Level 5 general is **artificial superintelligence**.

The point of the second axis is that superhuman performance is already common one task at a time.
ASI requires it across a wide range of tasks, which no single-axis ladder can express.

### One profile, three verdicts

Hendrycks et al. (2025) define AGI as matching the cognitive versatility and proficiency of a
well-educated adult, operationalized as ten domains worth 10 points each. Their Table 1 scores GPT-4
at 27 and GPT-5 at 57. GPT-5's profile runs from 10 in reading and writing and in mathematics to 0 in
long-term memory storage: a **jagged profile**.

**Pause and predict:** if AGI required adult level in every domain, where would GPT-5 stand?

At 0%. The paper's AGI score is a sum, so strength compensates for weakness. A minimum, letting the
weakest domain decide, gives 0% for both models; counting domains at full adult level gives 20% for
GPT-5 and 0% for GPT-4. The scores are identical in all three readings; only the aggregation changes.
Only the sum is the paper's definition. The other two are readings the lab adds.

The scale also caps at the adult. A system far beyond every person and one that just matches the
adult both score 100%, so an AGI benchmark built this way is structurally unable to register ASI.

### Faster, more, or better

A mind can exceed human performance by speed, by parallelism or by quality. The card computes the
first two for a project of `W` expert-years:

`T = W · [w + (1 − w) · (s / v + (1 − s) / (v · n))]`

where `v` is speed, `n` is the number of copies, `s` is the share of the thinking that is serial and
`w` is the share that waits on the physical world. It is Amdahl's law with a term that no compute can
shrink.

**Pause and predict:** with `v = 1,000`, `n = 10⁶` and `w = 10%`, how long does a 100 expert-year
project take?

About 10 years, against about 3 seconds with `w = 0`. Once thinking is cheap, `T → W · w`, which the
card reports as **Fastest possible here**. Serial work behaves similarly with respect to copies:
with `s = 50%`, extra copies cannot cut the time below `W · (1 − w) · s / v`.

Quality has no term in the formula. That is the omission that matters most, because many arguments
about ASI turn on qualitative superiority, and it cannot be reduced to speed or headcount.

### A loop that feeds itself

Good (1965) argued that a machine able to design better machines would set off an **intelligence
explosion**. The card's model makes the loop explicit. Let `A` be AI research ability relative to
today's human research community. Research effort, with a share `b` that only people can do, is

`R(A) = 1 / (b + (1 − b) / max(1, A))`

and progress follows `d ln A / dt = g · Rʳ / A`, with `g = 0.15` per year from `A = 0.1`. Below
`A = 1`, `R = 1` and `A` grows linearly, reaching 1 in year 6 in every run. With `b = 0` and `A > 1`,
`d ln A / dt = g · A^(r − 1)`: **returns to research** `r > 1` give finite-time blow-up, `r = 1` a
constant doubling time and `r < 1` deceleration.

With `b = 0`, the climb from 1× to 100×, the **takeoff**, takes 30.7 years at `r = 1`, 12.0 at
`r = 1.5` and 3.3 at `r = 3`. Below `r = 1`, 100× is not reached within 40 years.

**Pause and predict:** at `r = 1.5`, what does `b = 20%` do?

It caps research effort at `1/b = 5`, after which `d ln A / dt ≤ g · 5^r / A` and growth becomes
linear; 100× is not reached within 40 years. This is the **bottleneck** argument of Aghion, Jones and
Jones (2017): growth is limited by what is essential and hard to improve. A small share behaves
differently. At `b = 1%` the curve still reaches 100× in year 19.8, against 18.0 with no bottleneck,
but no longer leaves the chart. Any `b > 0` eventually removes the singularity in this model; how
much overshoot precedes it depends on how small `b` is.

### Reading a forecast

Grace et al. (2024) surveyed 2,778 published AI researchers in late 2023. The aggregate forecast gave
machines outperforming humans at every task a 10% chance by 2027 and 50% by 2047, 13 years earlier
than the 2022 survey's 2060. Full automation of all occupations received 10% by 2037 and 50% by 2116.

**Pause and predict:** how far apart are the 50% years for "every task" and "every occupation"?

69 years, from the same respondents, for nearly the same world. Framing moves the answer by more than
the inter-survey shift.

Kwa et al. (2025) measured the **task time horizon**: the length, in human working time, of software
tasks frontier models complete with 50% success. It was about 50 minutes in early 2025 and had doubled
about every seven months since 2019. Extrapolated five years at that rate it reaches about 317 hours,
1.9 working months, consistent with the paper's projection, which it conditions on the results
generalizing to real-world tasks. At a 12-month doubling the same start reaches 27 hours. The fitted
segment is evidence; the extension is an assumption about it.

### More words in this family

- **Narrow AI** (ANI): the left column of card 1.
- **Human-level machine intelligence** (HLMI): the survey's wording, unaided machines that can do
  every task better and more cheaply than human workers.
- **Transformative AI**: defined by impact, roughly a transition comparable to the industrial
  revolution, independent of whether AGI has been reached.
- **Recursive self-improvement**: an AI improving the design of its successors, the step that closes
  card 4's loop.
- **Technological singularity**: Vinge (1993), the point at which superhuman intelligence makes
  further change unpredictable; he forecast the technical means within thirty years.
- **Takeoff**: the interval from roughly human-level to far beyond, described as fast or slow.
- **Orthogonality thesis**: Bostrom (2012), intelligence and final goals vary independently.
- **Alignment**: getting a system to pursue what its designers intend; the Post-training lab measures
  a small case.

## Your turn: sort the claims

The sixth card lists eight claims drawn from the earlier cards. Classify each as a **measurement**
(an observation), a **definition** (a choice of usage), an **argument** (a consequence of
assumptions) or a **forecast** (a statement about the future). Each answer shows the reason.

The hard cases are composites. A survey result is a measurement of opinion whose content is a
forecast; a fitted trend is a measurement whose extrapolation is a forecast. The card asks for the
kind of the claim as stated.

## What this lab cannot tell you

### What the lab simplifies

Card 3 has no coordination cost for copies and treats the world share as fixed; the first flatters
parallelism, the second may understate what better ideas could redesign. Card 4's functional form,
starting point and rate `g` are chosen for legibility, so its years carry no meaning and 1× and 100×
are not units of intelligence. Cards 1 and 2 reproduce their authors' judgments at publication and do
not grade later systems.

### Where the arguments are open

The parameters that decide card 4's shape have not been measured. Estimates of the returns to AI
research differ, as do views on how much of research is bound by experiments, compute or people;
Erdil and Besiroglu (2023) review the arguments for explosive growth and conclude it is plausible
without warranting high confidence. Whether quality of cognition can rise far above human levels, or
whether some problems are hard for any mind, is likewise unsettled, and it is the question card 3
cannot represent.

**A common mix-up:** reading a narrow superhuman result or a benchmark percentage as a rung on one
ladder toward ASI. The first is a position on the performance axis only; the second, on a
human-capped scale, cannot exceed the cap by construction.

**How sure is this?** Table placements, domain scores, survey years and the time-horizon trend are
copied from their sources. Every number on cards 3 and 4 follows from inputs chosen for the lab.

## Where to go from here

- **What AI actually is** introduced AGI as a goal with no agreed test.
- **Scaling laws** covers the fitted trends most forecasts extrapolate, and where fits fail.
- **Post-training** measures a small alignment failure: a proxy reward outrunning the real objective.
- **Interpretability I** and **Interpretability II** ask how a system's learned behaviour could be
  checked, which bears on oversight of more capable systems.
- **Ethics and impact** covers present harms and benefits.
