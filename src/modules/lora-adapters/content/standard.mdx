## The big picture

**LoRA**, short for low-rank adaptation, adapts a pretrained model without rewriting it: it freezes a
weight matrix `W` and trains a separate update beside it, `ΔW = (alpha / rank) · B · A`. `B` and `A`
are thin matrices whose shared inner dimension is the rank, and alpha / rank sets the update's
scale. The question LoRA answers is how much of a fine-tune you can keep
while training and shipping only that small update.

*Fine-tuning & transfer* kept training a pretrained table's own weights on a new corpus and
measured the forgetting that caused. This lab freezes the same Harbor base, compares a low-rank
update with full fine-tuning at the same budget, and then follows the update into serving and onto
real model sizes.

### Where it shows up

- Hugging Face's PEFT library, named for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, implements LoRA among
  related methods, and TRL's trainers, such as `SFTTrainer`, integrate with PEFT.
- In a transformer, adapters often target the query and value projections, sometimes all four
  attention projections (query, key, value and output) and the feed-forward layers.
- **QLoRA** trains a 16-bit adapter on a base stored in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), which let a
  65B-parameter model fine-tune on one 48 GB GPU.
- vLLM serves LoRA adapters registered by name when started with `--enable-lora`, and each request
  selects one.

### Why it matters

Full fine-tuning carries gradients and optimizer state for every parameter, and Adam, for example,
keeps two extra values per trainable parameter. LoRA confines both to the adapter. The LoRA paper
reports that, compared with fine-tuning GPT-3 175B with Adam, LoRA can cut the trainable parameters
by 10,000 times.

Because the base is never written, one server can hold a single copy of it and choose among many
small adapters per request, instead of a full copy of the weights per task. The price is capacity:
whether an adapter matches full fine-tuning depends on the task.

### What you will be able to explain

- What the scaled product `B · A` adds to a frozen weight matrix.
- How **Rank** trades fit against parameter count, and why a sweep at a fixed **Alpha** changes two
  things at once.
- What merging an adapter into `W` gains at inference, and what it forecloses.
- Why a freshly initialized adapter changes nothing until it is trained.
- How to compute an adapter's trainable parameters, share of the base model and file size from an

## What it is

**LoRA** freezes a weight matrix and trains an addition to it. The **Effective weights** row
shows the whole mechanism with the current numbers substituted: `W + (alpha / rank) · B · A`.

`W` is the base table — the same 900-weight character bigram model, pretrained 60 epochs on Harbor
weather notes, scoring 1.921 there and 2.556 on Recipe steps. It is read on every forward pass and
written on none. `B` is 30 × r and `A` is r × 30, so their product is another 30 × 30 matrix.

For a
d × k matrix the pair holds `r · (d + k)` numbers instead of `d · k`; here that is 60r against 900.
**Rank** sets r, and only `B` and `A` receive updates.

The base is the identical 60-epoch, seed 1 Harbor table that *Fine-tuning & transfer* starts from,
rebuilt inside this lab. *Model merging* trains its own Harbor base for 48 epochs instead. Each lab
fixes its own epoch count, and none tests the difference.

### What the diagram shows

The matrix-product diagram on **The low-rank update** draws the trained numbers: A across the top, B
down the left, and ΔW where they meet. Every row of ΔW is a weighted mix of the same r rows of A, so
ΔW can have at most r independent directions. The chart beside the full fine-tune's map confirms it: the
LoRA curve reaches 100% at k = r exactly.

### Why an untrained adapter does nothing

`B` starts at all zeros and `A` starts random, so `B · A` is exactly the zero matrix. That is **zero
initialization**: adaptation begins from the pretrained behaviour. The gradient reaching `B` is
proportional to `A`, which is non-zero, so `B` moves on the first batch; once `B` is non-zero, `A`
starts receiving gradient too.

### What Alpha is for

**Alpha** sets the constant `alpha / rank` that multiplies the product. At the defaults — **Rank** 4
and **Alpha** 8 — the scale is 2.00, so the adapter's contribution to each logit is twice the raw
product.

## Why it’s here

The previous lab left you a whole new 900-weight table per task. LoRA changes the shipping unit:
the base stays byte-identical and the per-task artifact is 60r numbers. That separation is what
makes **hot-swapping** and **merging** possible.

The reason anyone accepts the constraint is the measured result. At the same 24-epoch budget, a
rank-4 adapter training 240 of 900 weights reaches 2.077 on Recipe steps, while full fine-tuning of
all 900 reaches 2.090.

## How to play with it

Move **Rank** from 1 to 12. B widens, A deepens, and the bar list reads 60, 120, 240, 480 and 720
trainable weights against 900.

Set **Adapter epochs** to 0. B and ΔW go blank, **Target loss** reads 2.556, and every pair of
bars under **Next character after** matches. Raise it back to 24 and watch the solid curve leave
the dotted frozen-base line.

At 24 epochs sweep **Rank** and read the recipe loss in the bar list: 2.462, 2.316, 2.077, 1.990 and
2.059 for ranks 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12. Then pick `s` under **Next character after**: the base gives `a`
5%, the adapter 29%.

Switch **Serving** between `Base only` and `Base + adapter`: **Target loss** moves between 2.556 and
2.077, **Source loss** between 1.921 and 2.235. Then press **Merge the adapter into W** and read the
logit gap. Then press **Set alpha = rank** and repeat the rank sweep at a scale of 1.00.

Last, open **Adapter size calculator**. It starts on Llama 3 8B with every matrix targeted at rank
16: **Trainable parameters** reads 41,943,040, **Share of the base model** 0.522%, and **Adapter
size** 83.9 MB at 16-bit. Switch the MLP off and the count falls to 13,631,488, or 0.170%. Set
**Adapter precision** to 32-bit and the size doubles to 167.8 MB. Then read the callout: at rank 4
the toy trains 26.7% of its table, and the 8B preset trains 0.131%.

## What to notice

- The headline: 240 trainable weights matched 900 at the same budget, 2.077 against 2.090. It is
  one measurement on one corpus.
- The full fine-tune did not need low rank to get there. Its update is spread out: the first 4
  directions hold 63% of it, the first 8 hold 86%. LoRA found a different, rank-4 update that fits
  recipes about as well.
- More rank is not more fit. Rank 12 lands at 2.059, worse than rank 8 at 1.990, but that sweep
  confounds two things: at **Alpha** 8, rank 1 has scale 8.00 and rank 12 has 0.67.
- The adapter forgets *more* than full fine-tuning here. On Harbor the base is 1.921, full
  fine-tuning gives 2.088, and the rank-4 adapter 2.235. A scale of 2.00 amplifies its update.
- So separate two claims. LoRA protects the base **weights**, which is what makes swapping and
  merging work. It does not protect the base model's **behaviour**.
- Detaching restores the base exactly: **Source loss** returns to 1.921.
- **Merging** matches the unmerged path to about 4e-7 per logit at rank 4, which is float32 rounding,
  and the recipe loss agrees to four decimals.
- The toy overstates LoRA's footprint by about two orders of magnitude. A pair costs
  `r · (inputs + outputs)` while its matrix costs `inputs · outputs`, so at rank 4 the toy trains
  26.7% of its table and the 8B preset trains 0.131% with every matrix targeted, about 200 times
  less. This is the **trainable fraction**.
- Which matrices you target matters as much as rank. At rank 16 the MLP holds 67.5% of the 8B
  adapter, and adding it to q and v multiplies the count by 5.15.
- Size is exactly linear in rank, layers, and bits, and the share falls as the model grows: at rank
  16 on every matrix it is 0.912% for the 1B model, 0.522% for 8B, and 0.294% for 70B, whose adapter
  is 207,093,760 parameters.
- The same arithmetic reproduces a published figure. For Llama 3.2 1B with q only at rank 8 it gives
  524,288 trainable parameters, the count the PEFT quick tour prints for that configuration.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The adapter, its gradients, both losses, the spectra and the merge check are
computed live. The structural gap is that this model has one matrix, so **target modules** has one
member. A real LoRA attaches to chosen projections inside each attention block — often the query and
value projections, sometimes all four and the feed-forward layers — and that choice is much of the
practical skill.

Both ΔW maps subtract each row's mean before drawing, because a softmax ignores a constant added to a
whole row. The full fine-tune never adds one; the adapter does, so its raw ΔW is larger than it
looks. **Rank** stops at 12; at 15, 60 × 15 would equal the full 900.

Nothing is held out, and the optimizer is plain SGD at a fixed 0.6, with no dropout on the adapter.

The **Adapter size calculator** is closed-form arithmetic. It trains and serves nothing, so it cannot
say whether a given rank is enough for a task. Its Custom model follows Llama 3's ratios and is not a
published architecture, and its counts leave out anything beyond the LoRA pairs.

**In real systems.** Parity with full fine-tuning is a property of the task. Adapters fall behind on
large shifts — new languages, new modalities, genuinely new knowledge. Serving many unmerged
adapters needs batched kernels to avoid per-request latency, and merging several adapters into one
base makes them interfere, which is the next lab's subject.

The file on disk also depends on the dtype it is stored in, which can differ from the base model's.
A small trainable fraction says nothing about quality.

## Going deeper

The empirical claim behind the method is that the *update* a fine-tune needs has low intrinsic rank
even when the weights do not (Hu et al., 2021). The constraint also acts as a regularizer, which is
part of why small ranks sometimes generalize better than large ones.

`alpha / rank` exists so rank and update strength can be tuned separately. The product `B · A` sums r
rank-one terms, so its size tends to grow with r; dividing by r holds it roughly steady, and a
learning rate tuned at one rank still works at another. Later work (rsLoRA) argues that dividing by
the square root of r is steadier at large ranks.

The larger saving is training state, not the forward pass. Adam keeps two extra values per
*trainable* parameter, so 240 trainable weights carry optimizer state for 240 rather than 900, and
gradients for the frozen matrices are never stored.

**QLoRA** pushes further: the frozen base is stored in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), dequantized to 16
bits on the fly for each multiply, while the adapter trains in 16-bit precision. That cuts the base's
memory about fourfold and let a 65B-parameter model fine-tune on one 48 GB GPU.

The *Quantization* lab shows the uniform affine version of this storage trade, with a bit width and
a scope. NF4 places its 16 levels non-uniformly, a data type the QLoRA paper describes as
information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights. Double quantization quantizes
the quantization constants themselves to save more memory.

Earlier **adapter** designs inserted small trainable bottleneck layers between frozen ones, which
stay in every forward pass. LoRA's update is linear in the weights, so it can be folded into `W`
once — which is also why an adapter with a nonlinearity inside cannot be merged.

### Other ways to adapt

LoRA is one member of the family that Hugging Face's PEFT library collects under parameter-efficient
fine-tuning. The others change what is trained, and where:

- **Prompt tuning** trains soft prompt vectors that are added to the input embeddings of a frozen
  model.
- **Prefix tuning** also trains vectors, but inserts them in every layer rather than only at the
  input.
- **IA3** learns vectors that rescale the keys, the values, and the intermediate feed-forward
  activations. PEFT's guide says an IA3 model trains about 0.01% of T0's parameters where LoRA trains
  more than 0.1%.
- **DoRA** splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction, adapts the direction with LoRA, and
  learns the magnitude as a separate parameter. PEFT turns it on with `use_dora` and recommends
  merging for inference, because it adds overhead.

PEFT also offers `use_rslora`, which scales the update by alpha over the square root of rank.

### Serving many adapters

Because an adapter is small, one server can hold a single copy of the base model and many adapters,
and choose one per request. PEFT's quick tour shows the in-process version: `add_adapter` registers
a named adapter and `set_adapter` activates it.

vLLM serves LoRA adapters when started with `--enable-lora`. Adapters are registered by name, a
request selects one through its `model` field, and `max_loras` bounds how many run at once.

The S-LoRA paper describes a system that keeps every adapter in main memory and fetches those used
by running requests into GPU memory. It reports up to four times the throughput of vLLM and PEFT
baselines while serving several orders of magnitude more adapters. A merged adapter gives all of
this up: each merge is a separate full copy of the weights.

### Toy versus real

- **Fractions.** The toy trains 26.7% of its weights at rank 4. The Hugging Face PEFT quick tour
  prints 524,288 trainable parameters of 1,236,338,688, which is 0.0424%, for a rank-8 adapter on the
  query projections of Llama 3.2 1B. The calculator gives 0.522% for rank 16 on every matrix of Llama
  3 8B. The LoRA paper's abstract says that, compared with fine-tuning GPT-3 175B with Adam, LoRA can
  cut the trainable parameters by 10,000 times.
- **Settings.** The toy uses ranks 1 to 12, alpha 8, and plain SGD at 0.6. The QLoRA paper used
  r = 64, alpha = 16, adapters on all linear layers, and learning rates of 2 × 10⁻⁴ for 7B and 13B
  models and 1 × 10⁻⁴ for 33B and 65B. The PEFT quick tour's example uses r = 8, lora_alpha = 32,
  and 1 × 10⁻³. These rates are not comparable with the toy's.
- **Files.** `save_pretrained` writes the adapter as `adapter_config.json` plus
  `adapter_model.safetensors`. Safetensors is a format for storing tensors safely, as opposed to
  pickle, while staying fast through zero-copy loading. The PEFT quick tour's opt-350m adapter is
  6.3 MB against about 700 MB for the model, and PEFT casts 16-bit adapter weights to float32 by
  default for stable training.
- **Tools.** Hugging Face PEFT provides `LoraConfig`, `get_peft_model`, and `merge_and_unload` to fold
  an adapter into the base. TRL's trainers, such as `SFTTrainer`, integrate with it, and the QLoRA
  recipe stores the base in NF4. mergekit can extract a PEFT-compatible low-rank approximation from a
  fine-tuned model with `mergekit-extract-lora`.
