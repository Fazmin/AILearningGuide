## The big picture

This module is about a single neuron: a unit that multiplies each input by a learned **weight**,
adds a learned **bias**, and passes the resulting **weighted sum** `z` through an
**activation**, a fixed scalar function. *Tokens & embeddings* turned text into vectors; a neuron
reads a vector of inputs like those and returns one number.

The question it answers is what a few learned numbers can decide about an input, and what they
cannot. The answer is geometric. The inputs where `z = 0` form the **decision boundary**, a
straight line in this lab's two-input plane and a hyperplane in general. The weights and bias
place that boundary; the activation sets how the output changes across it.

### Where it shows up

- **Layers in this course.** Dense layers, attention projections, and MLP blocks are all built
  from this one weighted sum.
- **Transformers.** Attention scores, MLP hidden units, and output logits are all weighted sums
  with a nonlinearity nearby.
- **Activation choices.** Hidden layers in production mostly use smooth relatives of ReLU, and
  sigmoid remains at binary outputs and gates.

### Why it matters

A neuron decides from its inputs using only numbers that training can change: its
**parameters**, the weights and the bias. Three ingredients are there because leaving any one
out causes a specific failure:

- Without the bias, the decision boundary is forced through the origin.
- Without a nonlinear activation, extra layers add no expressive power.
- Without a usable slope in the activation, gradient descent has nothing to follow, which is why
  step units cannot be trained by it.

Even so, one neuron draws one straight boundary. A pattern is **linearly separable** when a
single straight boundary splits its classes; one that is not is out of reach. XOR is the
standard example, and the reason *Stacking neurons* comes next.

### What you will be able to explain

- Compute a weighted sum `z = x₁w₁ + x₂w₂ + b` by hand, and check it against the lab's
  **Weighted sum** readout.
- Describe how sigmoid, ReLU and step shape the output from the same `z`, and how each one's
  slope decides whether gradient descent gets a signal.
- Relate the weights and bias to the position of the decision boundary: the direction of the
  weight vector `w = (w₁, w₂)` turns it, the ratio `|b| / ‖w‖` sets how far it sits from the
  origin, and scaling all three together leaves it in place.

## What it is

A neuron is two operations in sequence: an affine map, then a scalar **activation**. For the
two inputs in this lab the affine part is `z = x₁w₁ + x₂w₂ + b` and the output is `a = f(z)`.
Only the weights and the bias are learned; the inputs arrive from data or a previous layer.

At the defaults, `z = 0.70 × 1.2 + 0.35 × (−0.8) + (−0.1) = 0.460`, and `σ(0.460) = 0.613`.

### The geometry of z = w · x + b

Written for any width, `z = w · x + b`. The **weight vector** `w` is the gradient of `z` with
respect to the input, so it is perpendicular to every level line of `z`, including the
**decision boundary** `z = 0`. Its direction sets the boundary's orientation. Its length sets
how fast `z` grows: the lines `z = ±1, ±2` sit `1 / ‖w‖` apart.

The bias sets the offset. The signed distance from an input to the boundary is `z / ‖w‖`,
which the lab prints as **Distance to boundary**: 0.319 at the defaults, where `‖w‖` is
1.442. Without a bias the boundary would be forced through the origin.

### What the activation adds

- **Sigmoid** maps any `z` into the open interval (0, 1). Its slope `σ(1 − σ)` peaks at 0.25
  at `z = 0` and falls below 0.05 beyond `|z| ≈ 2.89`.
- **ReLU** returns `max(0, z)`. It is unbounded above, and its slope is exactly 1 on the
  active side and 0 on the other.
- **Step** returns 1 when `z > 0` and 0 otherwise. Its slope is 0 everywhere except the jump,
  where no derivative exists, so it gives gradient descent nothing to follow.

## Why it’s here

This is the smallest unit that learns, and the rest of the course is built from copies of it.
A layer is many neurons reading the same inputs with different weights; a deep network is
layers of those.

Two affine maps composed are still one affine map, so without a nonlinearity any stack
collapses to a single linear function. The next lab tests that claim directly.

## How to play with it

Move **Input x₁** and **Input x₂** and watch the probe cross the plane while **Weighted
sum** and **Activation** update. Hold the inputs and sweep **Weight w₁** from `-2` to `2`:
the boundary rotates and the `w` arrow stays perpendicular. **Bias b** translates it.

With the default parameters, set the inputs to `0.75` and `1.00` so `z = 0`, then switch
**Activation**: Sigmoid gives 0.500, ReLU 0.000, Step 0. Finally set **Corner task** to
`AND`, `OR`, and `XOR` and try to score 4 / 4 on each.

## What to notice

- Scaling `w₁`, `w₂`, and `b` by the same factor leaves the boundary and the distance in
  place while `z` scales. From (1.0, −0.5, −0.2) to (2.0, −1.0, −0.4), the default probe's `z`
  goes from 0.325 to 0.650 and its distance stays 0.291.
- Scaling only the weights moves the boundary toward the origin, because `b` did not scale.
- The three activations disagree most near `z = 0`. At the lab's maximum, `z = 6`, sigmoid
  reads 0.998 with slope 0.0025, step reads 1, and ReLU reads 6.
- AND and OR reach 4 / 4, for example `w = (1, 1)` with `b = −1.5` or `b = −0.5`. XOR never
  passes 3 / 4.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

Nothing trains. The parameters are hand-set, the inputs are clamped to the unit square, and
the four corners are a truth table, not a dataset with a loss. The `w` arrow's drawn length
is scaled for display; its direction is exact.

### In real systems

One neuron draws one hyperplane, so any pattern that is not **linearly separable** is out of
reach. XOR is the standard example: an exhaustive search over this lab's slider grid finds
no setting above 3 / 4. Step units cannot be trained by gradient descent. Sigmoid units in
deep stacks slow learning through **saturation**, and ReLU units can die: once `z` is
negative for every example, the gradient is zero and the unit may never recover.

A weight is also not an importance score. Rescale an input feature and its weight
compensates, with no change in behaviour.

## Going deeper

Production hidden layers mostly use GELU or SiLU, smooth relatives of ReLU that allow small
negative outputs and keep a usable slope near zero. Sigmoid survives at binary outputs and
inside gates; its multi-class relative, softmax, appears in every attention head.

Initialization scale keeps `z` in the responsive part of the activation at the start of
training, and normalization layers keep it there as depth grows. With a step activation,
this unit is Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958). Minsky and Papert's 1969 analysis of what one
layer cannot compute, XOR among it, is answered by Stacking neurons, the next lab.
