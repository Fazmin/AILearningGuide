## The big picture

Picture deciding whether to go to the beach on Saturday. You look at a few things, like how sunny
the forecast is and how much homework you still have, and some of them count for more than others.
Sunshine pulls you toward going while homework pulls you back, and you weigh it all up into one
answer: go, or stay home.

This lab is about the smallest working part of an AI model, a single artificial neuron, which does
a stripped-down version of that using only numbers. It takes a few numbers in, scales each one by
how much it counts, adds them up, and turns the total into one answer. No thinking is involved, only
multiplying and adding, but that small recipe is the brick the later models in this course are
built from.

### Where it shows up

Every model in the rest of this course, including the kind of text-writing model behind a chatbot,
is built from huge numbers of copies of this one piece. A layer is a row of neurons that all read
the same inputs but count them differently, and a deep network is layers feeding their answers into
more layers. In the last lab, Tokens & embeddings, text became lists of numbers, and a neuron is the
kind of piece that reads a list like that and returns one number. Even the attention scores you will
meet later come from the same multiply-and-add.

### Why it matters

When a model learns, a process called training adjusts the few numbers inside each neuron, so one
neuron shows you what learning actually changes. The neuron's last step, the activation, is what
makes stacking neurons worth doing, because without it a tall stack of neurons would be no better
than one. Its biggest limit is that it can only sort inputs into yes and no by drawing one straight
line between them. That is why real models need many neurons working together.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lab you will be able to:

- work out a neuron's total by hand, by multiplying each input by how much it counts, adding the
  results, and adding one fixed nudge;
- describe how the three answer-shaping curves in the lab turn that same total into three
  different kinds of answer, and why the flat parts of a curve slow learning down;
- explain how the neuron's numbers turn and slide the line where its answer flips from no to yes,
  and why one neuron can never solve a small puzzle called XOR.

### What the lab shows

The lab has three cards, and the same sliders drive all of them. **Decision boundary and controls**
shows a square map of every possible pair of inputs, coloured by the neuron's answer, with the line
where that answer flips. **Neuron equation** writes out the arithmetic with your current numbers,
while **Activation curve** shows where your total lands on the chosen curve and how steep the curve
is there. A **Corner task** setting also puts a small four-corner puzzle on the square, where you
try to get the neuron to say yes at the right corners.

Every number on screen is worked out exactly from the neuron's formula, so nothing is guessed or
measured from a real model. Nothing is trained either: you set by hand the numbers a real network
would learn, and the four-corner puzzle is a tiny made-up test rather than real data.

## What it is

A neuron does two small jobs in a row: it weighs and adds up its inputs, then passes the total
through a curve that shapes the answer.

### Job one: weigh and add

Go back to the beach decision and turn each thing you look at into a number from 0 to 1. Call the
sunshine forecast x₁, where 0 means pouring rain and 1 means a clear sky. Call the homework you
still have x₂, where 0 means none left and 1 means all of it. These are the neuron's inputs, and in
the lab they are the **Input x₁** and **Input x₂** sliders.

Each input gets a multiplier that says how much each unit of that input counts and in which
direction, called a **weight**. Sunshine pulls you toward going, so its weight w₁ is positive,
starting at 1.2 in the lab. Homework pulls you back, so its weight w₂ is negative, starting at −0.8,
which means "the more of this, the less likely yes."

The neuron then adds one fixed amount that does not depend on any input, called the **bias**. You
can think of it as your mood before you even check the forecast, and the lab starts it at −0.1, a
slight lean toward staying home. When every input is 0, the bias is the only thing left in the
total, so it alone decides which way the neuron leans.

Multiply each input by its weight, add the results, then add the bias, and you get the **weighted
sum**. The lab writes it as `z = x₁ × w₁ + x₂ × w₂ + b`, where z is the total and b is the bias.
Here it is with the lab's starting numbers, a fairly sunny forecast of 0.70 with about a third of
your homework, 0.35, still to do:

- The sunshine part is 0.70 × 1.2 = 0.84, a push toward going.
- The homework part is 0.35 × (−0.8) = −0.28, a pull toward staying home.
- Adding both parts and the bias gives 0.84 − 0.28 − 0.10 = 0.46.

So z is 0.46, a small positive total, which means the neuron leans toward yes. The **Neuron
equation** card shows exactly this arithmetic, with each line into the middle circle carrying its
weight and the product it adds. Its **Weighted sum** row prints the whole sum with your current
numbers, so you can check every step.

Multiplying two lists of numbers pair by pair and adding up the results has its own name, the **dot
product**. Here one list holds the two inputs and the other holds the two weights, so the weighted
sum is their dot product plus the bias. You will meet the dot product again in later labs, because
attention uses the same move to score how well two lists of numbers line up.

The weights and the bias are the neuron's adjustable settings, called **parameters**. In a real
network nobody sets them by hand. Training shows the network many examples with known right answers
and nudges the parameters a little at a time until it makes fewer mistakes. The inputs are not
adjusted, because they arrive fresh with each example, the way each Saturday brings a new forecast.
In this lab you set the parameters yourself with the **Weight w₁**, **Weight w₂** and **Bias b**
sliders.

### Job two: the activation

The weighted sum can be any number, large or small, positive or negative, so the neuron's second job
is to turn it into a useful answer. The curve that does this is called the **activation**, and the
**Activation** control in the lab offers three of them.

Picture a graph with the total z along the bottom and the answer up the side. Find your z on the
bottom, go straight up until you hit the curve, and read across: that height is the neuron's answer.
The **Activation curve** card draws exactly this picture for whichever curve you pick.

**Sigmoid** squashes any total into a number between 0 and 1 along a smooth S-shaped curve. A total
of 0 gives exactly 0.5, big positive totals creep toward 1, and big negative totals creep toward 0,
though it never quite reaches either end. The lab prints its recipe as `1 / (1 + e^−z)`, where e is
a fixed number a little over 2.7. You never need to work that out by hand, but one case is quick.
At z = 0 the `e^−z` part becomes `e^0`, which is 1, because any number to the power 0 is 1. That
leaves 1 ÷ (1 + 1), which is 0.5. The Neuron equation card shortens sigmoid to σ, which is the Greek
letter sigma.

**ReLU** passes a positive total through unchanged and turns any negative total into 0. Its recipe
is `max(0, z)`, which means "whichever is bigger, 0 or z", so a total of 0.46 stays 0.46 while a
total of −2 becomes 0.

**Step**, known in full as the **step function**, gives a flat yes or no with nothing in between. It
answers 1 when z is above 0 and 0 otherwise, which includes the case where z is exactly 0.

With the starting total of 0.46, the three activations give three different answers:

- Sigmoid gives 0.613, a mild lean toward yes that sits about 61% of the way from 0 to 1.
- ReLU gives 0.460, which is the total itself, because the total is positive.
- Step gives 1, a flat yes, because the total is above 0.

The activation never changes z, only what the neuron does with it. Each of these curves bends
somewhere, which a plain multiply-and-add can never do, and Why it’s here explains why that bend
matters so much.

### The line where the neuron is undecided

Now picture every possible Saturday at once, laid out on the big square of the **Decision boundary
and controls** card. Sunshine x₁ runs from 0 on the left to 1 on the right, while homework x₂ runs
from 0 at the bottom to 1 at the top. Each point is one pair of inputs, and the dark dot, called the
probe, marks the pair your **Input x₁** and **Input x₂** sliders are set to.

Every point on the map has its own total z. Points where z is positive are shaded green, and points
where it is negative are shaded red, or grey when ReLU is picked. How deep the shading looks depends
on the activation. Between them runs a solid dark line made of every point where z is exactly 0,
which is called the **decision boundary**. These are the Saturdays where the pushes and pulls cancel
out, so the neuron is perfectly undecided.

With the starting numbers, the line runs from near the bottom-left corner up to the top edge. One
point on it is a forecast of 0.75 with all your homework still to do, and the sum shows why it sits
there: 0.90 − 0.80 − 0.10 = 0.

To see why the boundary is always straight, imagine z as a height above each point of the map. With
the starting weights, every step of 0.1 to the right raises z by the same 0.12, wherever you start.
Every step of 0.1 upward lowers it by the same 0.08, and that happens because z only ever multiplies
and adds. A surface that rises by the same amount for every equal step, with no bumps or dips, is a
perfectly flat, tilted sheet, like a ramp. The decision boundary is where the ramp crosses floor
level. A flat ramp always meets a flat floor along a straight line, so one neuron's boundary is
straight whatever numbers you choose.

The weights set which way the ramp tilts and how steeply. Changing them turns the line, and it can
shift the line too, because a new tilt changes where the ramp crosses the floor. The bias raises or
lowers the whole ramp without tilting it, so changing the bias slides the line to a new spot without
turning it.

### The arrow and the distance

The lab also draws the two weights together as one arrow, labelled w. The first weight says how far
the arrow goes across and the second says how far it goes up. With the starting weights of 1.2 and
−0.8, the arrow points to the right and somewhat downward. A list of numbers drawn as an arrow like
this is called a vector, and this one is the **weight vector**.

The arrow always points straight uphill on the ramp, toward bigger z and the green "yes" side. You
can check it with the starting weights, where moving right raises z by 1.2 for each full step and
moving down raises it by 0.8. So the fastest climb heads right and somewhat down, exactly where the
arrow points. Any part of a step that runs along the line is wasted, because z does not change along
it. So the steepest climb cuts straight across the line at a right angle.

The thin dashed lines join points at the same height, like the height lines on a hiking map. They
mark where z is 1 or 2 on the green side, and −1 or −2 on the red side.

The true length of w measures how steep the ramp is, and the lab prints it as **‖w‖**, which you
can read as "the length of w". The drawn arrow gets longer as ‖w‖ grows, but it is resized to fit
the picture. You find ‖w‖ the way you find the long side of a right triangle: square each weight,
add the squares, and take the square root. With the starting weights the squares are 1.44 and 0.64,
which add to 2.08, and the square root of 2.08 is about 1.442. That is how much z rises when you
walk one full unit in the arrow's direction, which is why it measures steepness.

**Distance to boundary** tells you how far the probe sits from the line along the shortest path,
which the lab draws as a short dotted line from the probe. The lab works it out as `z / ‖w‖`, which
is the probe's height divided by how steep the ramp is.

To see why that gives a distance, picture a ramp that rises 2 metres for every metre you move
across the floor toward its high side. If you are standing 1 metre up, the floor line is half a
metre away across the floor, which is 1 ÷ 2.
In the lab, at the start, that is 0.460 ÷ 1.442, or about 0.319, which is a bit under a third of the
square's width. The sign only tells you which side you are on: positive on the side the arrow points
to, and negative on the other side.

## Why it’s here

Every model needs a way to turn many incoming numbers into one useful signal, and that way of
combining them has to come with knobs that learning can adjust. A neuron does both with very few
moving parts. Each weight decides how much one input counts and whether it pushes toward yes or
toward no, while the bias sets the starting lean before any input arrives.

### Why the bias is there

You can see the bias earn its place on the map, because without it a neuron whose inputs are all 0
would have a total of exactly 0. That would force the decision boundary to pass through the
bottom-left corner every single time. Step would always say no there, and Sigmoid would always say
exactly 0.5. With a bias the line can sit anywhere, so the neuron can choose its own answer even
when every input is 0.

### Why the bend matters

A model gets its power from stacking neurons in layers, but stacking only helps if each layer adds
something the others cannot. Without an activation every neuron is a multiply-and-add, and a
multiply-and-add fed into another multiply-and-add collapses into a single multiply-and-add.

Here is a tiny example with one input, where the first neuron doubles its input and adds 1, and the
second neuron triples that answer and subtracts 4. Feed in 5 and the first neuron gives 11, and then
the second neuron turns that 11 into 29. Now picture a single neuron that multiplies by 6 and
subtracts 1, which also turns 5 into 29. In fact it matches the pair for every other input you try,
so the two-neuron stack was one neuron in disguise all along.

A hundred layers would collapse the same way, which would make all that depth useless. The reason is
that graphing any multiply-and-add, like 6 × x − 1, always gives a straight line, and two
straight-line steps in a row still give a straight line.

An activation stops the collapse because its curve bends: ReLU has a sharp corner at 0, sigmoid
flattens out at both ends, and step jumps. To see a bend at work, let a first neuron give x − 2, and
then let ReLU turn anything negative into 0. Inputs 0, 1 and 2 all come out as 0, then 3 gives 1 and
4 gives 2. That graph is flat and then climbs, a bent line that no single multiply-and-add can draw.

With a bend between layers the stack can no longer be squeezed into one neuron, so each extra layer
can add new shapes. The next lab, Stacking neurons, tests this directly.

### Why one neuron is not enough

The flat ramp also explains the neuron's biggest limit, because a boundary that is always one
straight line can only sort inputs that a straight line can split. Real tasks are rarely that tidy,
so models use many neurons that each draw their own line, and later neurons combine those lines
into more complicated shapes. The last step of this lab, with **Corner task** set to XOR, shows you
exactly where one neuron runs out.

## How to play with it

Work through these five steps in order, since each one builds on the last.

### 1. Move the inputs

Move **Input x₁** and **Input x₂** and watch the probe travel across the square. The **Weighted
sum** and **Activation** rows on the Neuron equation card update as you go, and so does the z badge
on the Decision boundary and controls card. Notice that moving the inputs never moves the line,
because the inputs are data, not parameters.

### 2. Turn and slide the line

**Try this:** Hold the inputs still, push **Weight w₁** up to 2, and then drag it slowly down toward
0. The line turns around a point a little below the bottom-left corner and lies flatter as you go.
Meanwhile the arrow w swings with it, always at a right angle to the line, though all you changed
was how much the sunshine counts.

Bring it down to 0.1 and the solid line leaves the square altogether, because now no point on the
map has a positive total. Even the best Saturday, full sun and no homework, now gives at most
0.1 − 0.1 = 0. The arrow keeps turning, though, so you can still see which way the ramp tilts.

Now set **Weight w₁** back to 1.2 and move **Bias b**, and this time the line slides without
turning, staying parallel to where it was. The worst corner, no sun and all the homework, gives −0.8
plus the bias, and the best corner, full sun and no homework, gives 1.2 plus the bias. So push the
bias up to 0.8 and the whole map turns green, while pulling it down to −1.2 turns it all red. Either
way, the line has slid right off the square and left only one colour behind.

### 3. One total, three answers

Return the two weights to 1.2 and −0.8 and the bias to −0.1, which are the lab's starting values.
Then set **Input x₁** to 0.75 and **Input x₂** to 1.00. That puts the probe exactly on the decision
boundary, so the z badge on the Decision boundary and controls card reads 0.000. Flip **Activation**
between Sigmoid, ReLU and Step, and read the output each time:

- Sigmoid gives 0.500, exactly halfway between no and yes, which is what it gives anywhere on the
  line.
- ReLU gives 0.000, because a total that is not positive becomes 0.
- Step gives 0, because it only says yes when z is above 0, and 0 is not above 0.

Keep an eye on the **Activation curve** card as you switch, where the solid violet curve is your
pick and the faint dashed ones are the other two. The dot on the solid curve marks where your z
lands, which right now is the centre line of the plot, z = 0. It sits halfway up for Sigmoid, and
down at 0 for ReLU and Step.

### 4. Double everything

Put the inputs back at their starting values, 0.70 and 0.35, and set **Activation** to Sigmoid. Set
the three parameters to the first values below and note what z and **Distance to boundary** say,
then double all three:

- **Weight w₁**: first 1.0, then double it to 2.0
- **Weight w₂**: first −0.5, then double it to −1.0
- **Bias b**: first −0.2, then double it to −0.4

Before doubling, z reads 0.325, and after doubling it reads 0.650, which is twice as much. Yet the
solid line does not budge, and **Distance to boundary** reads 0.291 both times. The dashed lines for
z = 1 and z = −1 do move, though. Before doubling they sit outside the square, and after doubling
they slide into view, closer to the solid line. A ramp twice as steep reaches a height of 1 in half
the distance.

In ramp terms, doubling all three makes the ramp twice as steep while it still crosses the floor in
the same place. The height doubles and the steepness doubles, so height divided by steepness, which
is the distance, stays the same.

### 5. The corner puzzle

Set **Corner task** to AND, and four marks appear at the corners of the square. A filled circle
wants the neuron to answer 1 and an open square wants 0, while the ✓ or ✗ beside each mark scores
the current setting. A corner counts as a yes when its z is above 0, which is the same rule Step
uses, whichever activation you picked.

AND wants a yes only at the top-right corner, where both inputs are 1, like a game that starts only
when both players press ready. With the lab's starting sliders the **Corners correct** readout shows
3 / 4. See if you can find a setting that scores 4 / 4 before checking the answer in What to notice.

Then switch to OR, which wants a yes whenever at least one input is 1. Last, switch to XOR and try
to get all four corners right, giving it a real try before you read Where it breaks.

## What to notice

- **The three activations differ near the line and far out on the green side.** Right on the line
  they give 0.5, 0 and 0. Just past it, Step jumps to 1, while sigmoid is still near 0.5 and ReLU
  is still near 0. Far
  out on the red side all three settle near 0 and agree. Far out on the green side, sigmoid creeps
  up to 1 and step is already at 1, while ReLU keeps climbing with z, so there the gap grows.
- **Switching Activation never moves the line.** It changes the shading and the output, but the
  line, the arrow and the corner marks stay put. They come from z and the weights alone, before the
  activation is applied. The line is where z = 0, the arrow is the two weights, and a corner counts
  as yes when its z is above 0.
- **Each activation shades the map its own way.** Sigmoid is pale near the line and deepens further
  out, while Step splits the square into two flat regions, one on each side of the line. ReLU paints
  the whole negative side grey, because every output there is exactly 0.
- **A negative weight is not a mistake**, because it means "the more of this, the less likely yes."
  The Neuron equation card draws it as a dashed red line instead of a solid green one, and the
  thickness of each line shows the size of its weight.
- **Doubling all three parameters makes sigmoid more sure of itself**, because z doubles while the
  line stays put. With Sigmoid selected, the output in step 4 rose from 0.581 to 0.657, moving
  further from the undecided halfway point.
- **Doubling only the weights does move the line.** If you leave the bias at −0.2 while the weights
  double, the line slides toward the bottom-left corner, because the bias no longer keeps pace.

### The flat zone

Push z as high as it can go by setting both inputs to 1 and both weights and the bias to 2. Now z is
6, and with Sigmoid selected the output reads 0.998, which is almost exactly 1.

The **Slope f′(z)** readout tells you how steep the curve is at your dot. Its label f′(z) is maths
shorthand for "the slope of the curve at z". The short pink line on the Activation curve card shows
the same slope as a picture. Both answer the question "if z moved a tiny bit, how much would the
output move?" Sigmoid is steepest at z = 0, where its slope is 0.25. That means nudging z by 0.1
there moves the output by a quarter as much, about 0.025.

At z = 6 the slope reads 0.0025, a hundred times smaller, and the readout turns red because it is
below 0.05. The shaded bands on the sigmoid plot, labelled `slope < 0.05`, mark where z is beyond
about 2.9 in either direction. This flat stretch, where big changes in z barely change the output,
is called **saturation**, and it comes back in the training labs.

### Solving AND and OR

AND reaches 4 / 4 with both weights at 1 and the bias at −1.5, and adding up each corner shows why:

- At the bottom-left corner both inputs are 0, so the total is −1.5, a no.
- At the two side corners one input is 1, so the total is 1 − 1.5 = −0.5, also a no.
- At the top-right corner both inputs are 1, so the total is 2 − 1.5 = 0.5, a yes.

OR reaches 4 / 4 with the same weights and the bias at −0.5 instead, which makes every corner except
the bottom-left one positive. XOR, by contrast, never gets past 3 / 4 whatever you try, and Where it
breaks explains why.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- Nothing is trained: you set by hand the numbers a real network would learn. There is no data, no
  running score of mistakes, and no rule for improving the weights.
- The inputs only run from 0 to 1, and the weights and bias only run from −2 to 2, so z always stays
  between −6 and 6.
- The four corners are a truth table, a short list of the right answer for every combination of
  inputs, rather than a real collection of examples.
- The arrow's direction is exact, but its drawn length is scaled so that it fits the picture.
- The neuron has only two inputs, which is what lets its whole map fit on a flat square.

### The beach picture stops matching

You know what sunshine and homework mean, but a neuron does not know what its inputs stand for. It
receives plain numbers with no labels, and in a real network its weights come from training rather
than from anyone's reasons. The beach decision is a way to picture the arithmetic, not a description
of what a neuron is doing.

### A weight is not an importance score

Picture two friends who each build the same beach neuron, except that one measures homework in hours
and the other measures it in minutes. Every homework number in the minutes version is 60 times
bigger, so its weight has to be 60 times smaller to give the same answers. Both neurons behave
identically, yet one weight looks tiny, which shows that a small weight does not mean an input
matters less. So a weight tells you how much one unit of its input counts, and that depends on what
unit the input is measured in.

### One straight line is all it gets

XOR wants a yes when exactly one input is 1 and a no when both inputs match. Picture a hallway light
with a switch at each end, wired so the light is on when exactly one switch is up. Both switches
down means off, one up means on, and both up means off again.

On the lab's square, that puts the two yes corners at the top-left and bottom-right, diagonally
across from each other, with the two no corners on the other diagonal. Any straight line you draw
leaves at least one corner on the wrong side, which is why **Corners correct** never passes 3 / 4.

You can check why with a pencil. Add up the totals at the two yes corners: the top-left gives w₂ + b
and the bottom-right gives w₁ + b, so together they make w₁ + w₂ + 2b. Now add up the two no
corners: the bottom-left gives b and the top-right gives w₁ + w₂ + b, which together also make
w₁ + w₂ + 2b. To score 4 / 4, both yes totals must be above 0, so their sum is above 0. Both no
totals must be 0 or below, so their sum is 0 or below. One number cannot be both, so some corner is
always wrong, whatever the sliders say.

On the map, the two diagonals cross at the centre of the square, and the centre's total is exactly
half of that shared sum. So the centre would have to sit on both sides of the line at once. A
computer search through every setting of the three sliders agrees. Wider sliders would not help
either, because bigger numbers can only turn or slide the line, never bend it.

A pattern that one straight line can split, like AND or OR, is said to have **linear separability**.
XOR is the classic pattern that lacks it, because no single line can split its corners. Solving it
takes more than one neuron: two that each draw a line, and one more that combines their answers,
which is where the next lab begins.

### Flat zones stall learning

Training improves a network by nudging each weight in whichever direction makes its mistakes
smaller, and it finds that direction by following slopes. The usual method is called **gradient
descent**, where the gradient is the collection of all those slopes. A slope of about 0 gives
training almost nothing to follow, so the weights behind that neuron barely move.

Picture a class test with questions so basic that everyone scores 99 or 100. Studying harder barely
changes anyone's score, so the test cannot tell the teacher who improved. A saturated sigmoid works
the same way, because its output sits so close to 0 or 1 that changing the weights hardly shows.

Here the test picture falls short, because everyone in that class is already right. A saturated
neuron inside a network can also be stuck near the wrong end, say near 1 when the right answer is 0.
It is then badly wrong, but because its slope is about 0 it gets almost no push to change. That is
when saturation hurts most.

ReLU has its own version of this problem, because its slope is 0 whenever z is negative. A ReLU
neuron whose total is negative for every example therefore gets no signal at all, and it may never
recover. Step is worse still: its slope is 0 everywhere except at the jump, where there is no slope
at all, so gradient descent cannot train it.

### Real neurons live in bigger spaces

A real neuron usually has many more than two inputs. With three inputs, the square becomes a box and
the boundary becomes a flat wall cutting through it. With more inputs the same idea continues in
more directions than anyone can draw, so only two or three inputs can be pictured the way this lab
does.

Inside a trained network, one neuron's line is also only one of many lines that later layers
combine. Reading a single neuron's boundary on its own tells you much less about the whole model
than it seems to.

**A common mix-up:** The name comes from nerve cells in the brain, but an artificial neuron is not a
tiny brain cell making a decision. It is one multiply-and-add followed by one bending curve, and it
remembers nothing it saw before.

## Going deeper

Real models mostly use smoother activations called GELU and SiLU in their hidden layers, which are
the layers between the input and the final output. Both are close relatives of ReLU that keep a
gentle slope near zero and allow slightly negative outputs, which makes training steadier than with
ReLU's sharp corner.

Sigmoid still shows up where a model gives a final yes-or-no answer. It also works inside parts
called gates, where a value between 0 and 1 sets how much of a signal gets through. Its relative for
choosing among many answers, called softmax, appears in every attention head, a part of the
attention step you will meet later.

Deep stacks of sigmoids have a further problem, because training multiplies the slopes of one layer
after another and even sigmoid's steepest slope is only 0.25. Multiply ten of those together and
you get less than one in a million, which is one reason the learning signal can fade to almost
nothing.

To avoid flat zones, starting weights are sized so that z lands in the responsive middle part of the
activation rather than at its flat ends. Inside deep models, extra steps called normalization keep z
in that range as more layers are added.

With Step as its activation, this neuron is the perceptron that the researcher Frank Rosenblatt
described in 1958. In 1969 the researchers Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert showed what one layer
like this cannot compute, with XOR among the examples. The
next lab, Stacking neurons, puts two of these lines together and solves XOR.
