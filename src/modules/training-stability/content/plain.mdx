## The big picture

Training a large AI model means running one loop millions of times. In each round the model makes
guesses, a score says how wrong they were, and the model's numbers are nudged so that the next
guesses come out a little better.

A run like that can take weeks on thousands of computer chips, and it can break in the middle. The
score for how wrong the model is, called the **loss**, can suddenly jump up in a **loss spike**,
and the run may or may not recover.

The numbers can also grow until the computer can no longer store them and the run stops for good,
which is called **divergence**. And sometimes nothing stops at all, while the model quietly gets
worse and every number on the screen still looks ordinary.

This module is about catching those problems early and fixing each one with the right tool. The
teams behind well-known models have written about this, and PaLM, a large model from Google, hit
about 20 loss spikes while it trained.

Each time, the PaLM team went back to an earlier saved copy of the model and left out some
training data, a fix you will try here.

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain:

- how to read a training dashboard, which is a screen of four live signals, and how to tell when
  one of them has left its normal range;
- why the small model in this lab blows up when its steps are too big, and what capping each step
  or starting with small steps changes about that;
- why 16-bit numbers hold only a narrow range of sizes, why training multiplies its numbers by a
  big factor to fit them in, and how that factor adjusts itself;
- how to spot a loss spike and recover from it by going back to a saved copy of the model and
  skipping the data that caused the spike;
- how the model's raw scores can grow without any limit, and what a small extra penalty, called
  z-loss, can and cannot do about it.

The lab gives you four cards to work with, and each one shows a different part of the story:

- **Fault and fixes** lets you break the same tiny model in one of four ways, and then try the
  fixes that belong to that problem.
- **Run monitor** is the dashboard itself, with four charts of signals measured at every single
  step of the real training run.
- **Run report** prints how the run ended in numbers, along with a sentence explaining what
  happened and what to try next.
- **Number formats** is a picture of how big and how small three kinds of computer numbers can get,
  worked out from their definitions rather than measured.

Every run is real training on your own computer, so every number on the dashboard was measured as
it happened. Only two things are staged: the 16-bit numbers enter at just one point of each step,
and the bad data in one fault is junk text the lab adds.

## What it is

### The model in this lab

The model in this lab is the same one you trained in **Train one from scratch**. It reads six
sentences about a harbor and learns to guess each character from the one before it, which gives it
378 pairs of neighbouring characters to learn from.

Everything the model knows sits in two small tables of numbers, called **weights**, and it scores
the next character by multiplying the two tables together. One table has 30 rows of 8 numbers and
the other has 8 rows of 30 numbers, so together they hold 480 weights.

Training looks at a group of 16 pairs at a time, called a **batch**, and it nudges the weights once
after each batch, which is called a **step**. The 378 pairs make 24 batches, and the run goes
through all of them 20 times, so a full run is 480 steps long.

At each step, training works out which way to push every weight to lower the loss, and how hard,
and that list of pushes is called the **gradient**. The weights then move by the gradient times one
number, the **learning rate**, which sets how big each step is.

### Why two tables can blow up

Picture a microphone held too close to a loudspeaker, so that a sound going into the microphone
comes out of the speaker a little louder. Then it goes back into the microphone and comes out louder
again, until the whole room hears a squeal.

That is called a **feedback loop**, because each trip around makes the next trip bigger. The two
tables in this model form one, since the push on each table is multiplied by the other table.

So a step that makes one table bigger also makes the next push on the other table bigger. With small
steps the loop stays quiet, the way a microphone held far from the speaker never squeals.

With a learning rate that is too big, each oversized step feeds an even bigger one, until some
number grows too large for the computer to store.

Here the picture stops matching, because a microphone squeals almost at once, while this model's
loop grows step by step over about 150 steps. The single-table model in **Hyperparameters &
schedules** cannot do this at all, because its pushes have a fixed upper size.

### Four signals on the dashboard

The **Run monitor** card draws four signals for every step of the run, and together they make the
dashboard:

- **Loss** shows how wrong the model is, with a thin line for each batch's loss and a thick line for
  the loss on all 378 pairs, every 4 steps.
- A dotted line on that chart sits at 3.401, which marks a model that knows nothing and spreads its
  guesses evenly across all 30 characters.
- **Gradient norm** is the overall size of the gradient, which you find by squaring every push,
  adding the squares together and taking the square root of the total.
- **Max logit** is the model's largest raw score for any next character, and these raw scores,
  called **logits**, are what the model turns into chances for each character.
- A huge logit means the model is almost completely sure of one character, so a wrong answer that it
  was sure about costs an enormous loss.
- **Update ÷ weight** compares how far one step moved the weights with how big they are, and this
  **update-to-weight ratio** is the only signal that measures the step itself.

Here is a worked example of the update ratio. If all the weights together have a length of 2, and one
step moves them by a length of 0.05, the ratio is 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025. That is close to the ratio of
this lab's healthy runs.

Three of the charts use a **log scale**, where each label up the side is a power of ten, such as 10,
100 and 1,000. On a log scale, a number that keeps getting multiplied by the same amount draws a
straight line.

Each chart also shades a band called the **normal range**, which runs from the 5th to the 95th
percentile of a clean run. That means 90 percent of the clean run's values fall inside it, so a
signal that leaves its band is the warning sign to look for.

### Four ways to break it

The **Fault** control picks how the run breaks, and each choice changes one thing about the healthy
run:

- **None** is the healthy run, which takes its steps at a learning rate of 0.5 for the whole run.
- **Learning rate too high** is the same run with a learning rate of 8, which is sixteen times
  bigger than the healthy one.
- **fp16 overflow** is the healthy run, except that each gradient is squeezed into a 16-bit number
  after being multiplied by a big number called a **loss scale**.
- **Garbage batch** swaps five real batches, at steps 400 to 404, for junk text that reads
  `qqqq zzzz xxxx jjjj` instead of real sentences.
- **Logit growth** uses a learning rate of 1 together with a different stepping rule, which the next
  paragraph explains.

The first three faults use the plain stepping rule from earlier labs, which moves each weight by its
push times the learning rate. The last two use **Adam**, from **Optimizers in practice**, which
sizes each weight's step using an average of how big that weight's recent pushes have been.

This Adam has no weight decay, the small pull toward zero that AdamW adds, so it behaves exactly
like AdamW with that pull switched off.

### Clipping and warmup

**Gradient clipping** puts a cap on the size of the gradient. When the gradient norm is bigger than
the cap, every push shrinks by the same factor until the norm equals the cap. That way the
direction of the step stays the same and only its length changes.

Picture a gradient with just two pushes, 3 and 4, whose squares, 9 and 16, add up to 25. The square
root of 25 is 5, so the norm is 5, and with a cap of 1 both pushes are divided by 5, giving 0.6 and
0.8.

**Warmup + cosine** changes the learning rate as the run goes on, starting at a tenth of its top
value and reaching the top after 15 percent of the steps. Then it slides down along a
smooth curve until it reaches 2 percent of the top at the very end.

That gives small steps while the weights are fresh, big steps while there is a lot left to learn,
and small steps again while the weights settle.

### Loss scaling

Computers store numbers using a fixed number of bits, the 0s and 1s in memory. A normal number here
uses 32 bits, but training often uses 16-bit numbers to go faster, and mixing the two sizes is called
**mixed precision**.

The catch is that 16 bits cannot hold every size of number, and the two common 16-bit formats,
which the glossary calls **fp16 and bf16**, make different trade-offs.

The fp16 format can only hold numbers up to 65,504, and very tiny numbers round down to zero. The
bf16 format reaches as far as a 32-bit number in both directions, but it rounds every number more
roughly.

**Loss scaling** helps fp16 by multiplying every gradient by a big number, the loss scale, before
squeezing it into 16 bits, and then dividing the same number back out. That lifts tiny pushes above
the point where they would round to zero, so they are not lost from the update.

For example, a push of 0.00000004 sits just above fp16's round-to-zero point. Multiplied by 2²⁰,
which is about a million, it becomes about 0.042, a size that fp16 stores easily.

The danger is that a scale that is too big pushes a large value past 65,504. That is called an
**overflow**, and it turns the number into infinity, which then spreads into every weight it
touches.

With that same scale of 2²⁰, a push of just 0.1 becomes about 105,000, which is far past 65,504, so
it overflows.

A static scale stays the same for the whole run, while **dynamic loss scaling** watches for
overflows and skips any step where one happens. Each time it skips a step it halves the scale, and
after 200 clean steps in a row it doubles the scale again.

### Roll back and skip

Training saves a copy of the weights every so often, and each copy is called a **checkpoint**. This
lab assumes that one is saved every 50 steps, so there is always a recent copy to go back to.

**Checkpoint rollback** means going back to a copy saved before a problem and training forward again
from there. **Batch skipping** means leaving out the batches that were in use around the problem
when training starts again.

### z-loss

The model turns its logits into chances by raising a special number, about 2.718, to the power of
each logit and dividing by the total. The log of that total is called the **log-partition**, written
log Z, and it grows whenever all the logits grow together.

Adding the same amount to every logit leaves the chances exactly the same, but it moves log Z. So
nothing in the ordinary loss stops all the logits from drifting upward together.

A **z-loss** adds a small penalty to every example's loss, equal to a small number times log Z
squared. That penalty pulls log Z back toward zero, which keeps the logits from drifting as a group.

For example, with log Z at 20 and a coefficient of 0.01, the penalty is 0.01 × 20 × 20 = 4, which
is bigger than the loss of a healthy model. With log Z at 2, the same penalty is only 0.04.

## Why it’s here

Every earlier training lab assumed that the run would finish, but large runs often do not, so the
people running them spend much of their time keeping them on track. They log signals, save
checkpoints, and decide within hours whether a bump in the loss is noise, a spike that will recover,
or the start of a divergence.

A crash is expensive, because if a run breaks at step 154 of 480, every step since the last
checkpoint is wasted. A run that drifts quietly can waste the rest of its time before anyone notices,
and for the largest models that can mean weeks of work on thousands of chips.

That is why teams build these checks in from the start instead of adding them after a failure has
already cost them time.

Each fix in this lab answers one problem, and none of them can stand in for another. A clip does
nothing for an fp16 overflow, and a loss scale does nothing for steps that are too big.

In the same way, z-loss cannot rescue a run whose steps are too large, which is why you read the
dashboard first and then choose the fix that matches.

## How to play with it

### Read a healthy dashboard

Start with **Fault** on None, where the run ends with a loss of 2.039, well below the 3.401 of a
model that knows nothing.

Read the shaded band on each chart, which comes from this run itself, so it shows what normal looks
like for this model:

- **Loss** runs from 1.79 to 3.38 for single batches, while the thick line falls steadily toward 2.
- **Gradient norm** runs from 0.114 to 0.700, and its highest value in the whole run is 0.955.
- **Max logit** runs from 0.118 to 6.23, and its highest value in the whole run is 6.707.
- **Update ÷ weight** runs from 0.021 to 0.040, with a middle value of 0.026 across the run.

A dashed line on **Update ÷ weight** marks 0.001, written 10⁻³, a rule of thumb for big networks from
a Stanford course. This healthy run sits about 26 times above it.

That rule is a starting point for large models, not a test that this tiny model has to pass, so the
band matters more than the line.

### Push the learning rate

Set **Fault** to Learning rate too high and look at step 1, where the loss, the gradient norm and the
max logit match the healthy run exactly. They match because all three are measured
before the first step moves any weight.

Only **Update ÷ weight** is different on step 1, reading 0.310 against 0.021 for the healthy run,
because the step itself is sixteen times longer.

The gradient norm does not rise above its band until step 6, and it passes 10 at step 36. After that
it climbs in a straight line on its log scale, getting multiplied by about the same amount every
step.

By step 153 the gradient norm is 1.1 × 10¹⁹, which means 11 followed by 18 zeros. The max logit is
3.1 × 10³⁸, which is close to the largest number that a 32-bit float can hold.

On step 154 a number finally overflows, so the run halts, and a red line marks the step where every
chart stops.

**Try this:** set **Gradient clip** to 0.50 and watch the run survive, with the cap shrinking 459 of
the 480 steps. The run ends at 3.172, which is only a little better than a model that knows nothing.

Now set **Schedule** to Warmup + cosine while keeping the clip, and the run ends at 1.904. That beats
the healthy run's 2.039, even though its top learning rate is sixteen times higher.

### Overflow in fp16

Set **Fault** to fp16 overflow, with **Loss scale** at 2²⁰ and **Scaling** on Static, where 2²⁰
means 2 multiplied by itself 20 times, or 1,048,576.

At that scale, any push of 0.062 or more overflows once it is multiplied. On step 33 one does, and a
red triangle marks it at the top of **Gradient norm**.

The infinite push goes straight into the weights, so the run halts on that same step, with the red
halt line falling exactly where the triangle is.

Now switch **Scaling** to Dynamic, so that each step that overflows is skipped and the scale is
halved. The run skips steps 33, 62, 184 and 430, and its scale ends at 2¹⁷.

It finishes at 2.040, against 2.039 for the run with no 16-bit numbers at all, so skipping four steps
out of 480 cost almost nothing.

### Survive a bad batch

Set **Fault** to Garbage batch, and keep in mind that the same Adam run without the junk text ends
at 1.991.

With the junk, the first bad batch scores a loss of 6.636 on step 400 and pushes the gradient norm
to 2.806, far above its band of 0.483 to 0.851.

The **Spike** readout shows the loss on all the pairs rising by 1.395, from 2.051 to 3.447 at step
404. That is above the dotted line, so for a moment the model is worse than knowing nothing.

Set **Gradient clip** to 1.00 and read **Spike** again, and the spike shrinks to 0.734 and peaks at
step 408, but the junk still went into the weights.

Set the clip back to off and press **Roll back and skip**, and the run goes back to the checkpoint
saved after step 350. It replays steps 351 to 399 exactly and never uses steps 400 to 404.

It ends at 1.993, against 2.022 without the rollback, which is almost as good as never seeing the
junk at all.

### Tame logit growth

Set **Fault** to Logit growth, where Adam moves each weight by about the learning rate on its first
step. Here that rate is 1, so on step 1 **Update ÷ weight** reads 0.989.

By step 4 the loss on all the pairs is already above the dotted line, and the run ends at 11.515,
which is far worse than knowing nothing.

**Max logit** has a middle value of 66 and peaks at 395, yet nothing halts, because numbers that size
are still ordinary numbers that the computer can store.

Raise **z-loss** to 0.01, and the middle value of log Z falls from 19.7 to 2.2 while the peak max
logit falls from 395 to 179. Even so, the loss still ends at 3.277, which is close to the 3.401 of
knowing nothing.

Now lower **Learning rate** to 0.1, and the run ends at 1.985, a healthy result, because the steps
themselves are finally small enough.

## What to notice

### On the dashboard

- **The step shows trouble first:** at a learning rate of 8, the update ratio is out of range on
  step 1, and the others only on step 6.
- **A straight climb on a log scale is an explosion,** because it means each step multiplies the
  value by about the same amount.
- **A run can finish and still be ruined:** Warmup + cosine with no clip never halts, but it ends at
  19.152, and its max logit peaks at 1.2 × 10²⁸.
- **The ratio's rule of thumb can mislead:** over the last 50 steps of that ruined run, the update
  ratio has a middle value of 0.0026, near the 10⁻³ line.
- **The batch loss jumps first:** in Garbage batch it jumps on step 400, the first junk step, but
  the loss on all the pairs peaks on step 404.

### On the fixes

- **Neither fix for the high rate works alone:** Warmup + cosine without a clip ends at 19.152, and
  a clip of 0.50 without a schedule ends at 3.172.
- **A looser cap can be worse:** a clip of 1.00 on its own ends at 4.050, worse than knowing
  nothing, while clip and schedule together reach 1.904.
- **Dynamic scaling is cheap,** because its four skipped steps move the final loss only from 2.039
  to 2.040.
- **The stepping rule decides how much damage junk does:** with the plain rule at 0.5, the same five
  junk batches raise the loss by only 0.044, against 1.395 under Adam.
- Under the plain rule, the junk's gradient norm, 0.608 on the first bad step, stays inside the
  healthy band, so the dashboard barely notices it at all.
- **A clip helps less under Adam:** a clip of 1.00 roughly halves the Adam spike, because Adam
  divides each weight's step by the size of that weight's recent pushes.
- The weights for j and x never get a push in the clean text, since those letters never start a pair
  there, so the junk moves them a full step.

### On the rollback

- **The replay is exact:** the dotted amber line of the old run lies under the new one until step
  400, because the same weights meet the same batches.
- **Skipping costs little:** leaving out five batches ends at 1.993, close to the 1.991 of the run
  that never saw junk and better than 2.022 with it.
- **The checkpoint sets the price:** going back to step 350 means redoing the 49 steps from 351 to
  399, and saving less often means redoing more.

### On z-loss

- **It treats a symptom:** at every learning rate on the control, a z-loss of 0.01 lowers log Z, but
  at a rate of 1 the loss still ends at 3.277.
- **It barely matters in a healthy run:** at a rate of 0.1 the run ends at 1.985 with it and 1.991
  without it, though the peak max logit halves.

### On the number formats

- **The fp16 window moves but does not grow:** each doubling of **Loss scale** moves both ends of the
  green bar down by the same factor.
- From 2¹⁷ to 2¹⁸ the top of that bar falls from about 0.5 to 0.25, and the static run goes from
  finishing to halting at step 184.
- **The bf16 format reaches as far as fp32,** which is why bf16 runs usually need no loss scale,
  though it keeps only 7 bits of detail against 23 for fp32.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

The blow-up is real, because at a learning rate of 8 a weight grows past the largest number a 32-bit
float can hold, halting the run at step 154.

Everything else about this model is small, with 480 weights, no attention, one short text and one
seed, so each number here comes from a single run.

The 16-bit numbers are copied in at only one point, where the gradient is squeezed into fp16. The
guesses and the weights stay in 32 bits, so the lab cannot show any other number overflowing.

The lab cannot show the other fp16 problem either. Across the whole healthy run, none of the 153,008
pushes that were not already zero rounds down to zero, even with no scaling.

The smallest of those pushes, 3.6 × 10⁻⁸, sits just above fp16's round-to-zero point of 3.0 × 10⁻⁸.
With no scaling, 949 of them do land in fp16's less exact range near zero.

So this lab shows only the overflow side of loss scaling, and never the side that loss scaling was
invented for in the first place.

**A common mix-up:** a run that never halts is not necessarily healthy, because Logit growth never
produces a number the computer cannot store. Even so, that run ends at 11.515, which is far worse than
knowing nothing at all.

A halt is only the loudest kind of failure, and the dashboard's quieter signals are there to catch the
rest of them.

The junk batch has a known cause, because the lab puts it in on purpose. The lab also never stores a
checkpoint, since **Roll back and skip** trains again from step 1 with the five junk batches skipped.

That gives exactly what restarting from step 350 would, because the first 399 steps replay exactly the
same way both times.

The **Max logit** chart shows only the model's final scores. With no attention in this model, the
growth that real models see inside attention can only be described here.

The Adam runs have no weight decay, and with a decay of 0.1 at a rate of 1, the peak max logit stays at
only 17.8. The loss still ends at 3.136, close to knowing nothing, so decay alone reins in these logits
without fixing the run.

A research team saw the same calming effect of weight decay on the output logits of larger models.

Each pair's loss is capped at about 27.6, because the lab never lets a chance fall below one in a
trillion. In the Logit growth run, 63 of the 378 pairs sit at that cap, so 11.515 is the lowest the
true loss could be.

The update ratio's rule of thumb does not fit this toy either, since its healthy runs sit between 0.021
and 0.040, which is twenty to forty times the 10⁻³ line.

### Where the real mechanism fails

Real spikes rarely announce their cause, and when PaLM's team replayed the batches from around a spike,
starting from a different and earlier checkpoint, no spike appeared.

They concluded that spikes come from particular batches meeting particular weights, and not from bad
data alone.

Adam itself can be part of the cause. One research team argues that Adam can reach a state where its
steps are large and point in a direction unrelated to lowering the loss.

They say that state leads to divergence, and that it shows up most often in deep models trained on
large batches.

Not every problem is a sudden spike, and another team studied slow divergence instead, finding that
growing logits appear in small models trained with high learning rates.

That finding lets small models stand in for big ones when people study these problems, which saves a
great deal of computer time.

Training with 16-bit numbers has its own failures, and OPT, a model from Meta, linked its divergences
to its dynamic loss scale crashing toward zero.

Teams also keep a 32-bit copy of the weights, because 16-bit numbers can round a small update to a big
weight away entirely, and bf16 rounds more roughly still.

## Going deeper

### Attention logits and QK-norm

Bigger language models use **attention**, where each piece of the input scores how much to look at
every other piece. Those scores are logits too, and they can grow in the same way.

A Google team training an image model with 22 billion weights saw its loss blow up after a few
thousand steps. The attention scores had grown so large that attention locked onto single patches of
the image.

They fixed it by normalizing two of the numbers inside attention before multiplying them together,
which is called **QK-norm**. The OLMo 2 team does the same, and **Modern attention at scale** shows
where these scores come from.

### z-loss in practice

PaLM adds a z-loss with a coefficient of 0.0001 to keep log Z close to 0, and its team found that it
made training more stable. The small-model study described above uses that very same coefficient.

ST-MoE, a mixture-of-experts model that sends each word to a few specialist parts, uses a z-loss of
0.001 on the scores that pick those parts.

OLMo 2 uses z-loss too, but its paper gives two different coefficients, 0.0001 in its text and 0.00001
in its table, so it is worth checking before copying either one.

### Prevention: architecture, initialization and data

Most stability work happens before a run even starts, and one research team traced spikes to sudden
growth in the gradient norm and found model shapes that prevent it.

They checked those shapes by scaling or normalizing the numbers that feed into the model, and the
spikes went away.

The OLMo 2 team changed how its weights start out and stopped pulling some of its weights toward zero.
It also removed documents that repeat the same phrase 32 or more times, because it found that such
data caused spikes.

That is cleaning data as a stability fix, which is the subject of **Building a dataset**.

### Toy versus real

- **Clipping:** PaLM caps its gradient norm at 1.0, and OPT lowered its cap from 1.0 to 0.3 to keep
  its run stable, while this lab needs a cap of 0.50.
- **Loss scale:** PyTorch, a popular training tool, starts its scale at 2¹⁶, or 65,536, and its
  default settings list a shrink factor of 0.5 and a growth factor of 2.
- PyTorch waits 2,000 clean steps before growing the scale, while this lab waits only 200, because
  its whole run is just 480 steps long.
- **Rollback:** PaLM went back about 100 steps and skipped about 200 to 500 batches for each spike.
  This lab skips only 5, because it knows exactly which batches are bad.
- PaLM could replay any stretch of its run exactly from a checkpoint, which is what this lab's replay
  of steps 351 to 399 shows on a tiny scale.
- **Practice:** Hugging Face's Smol Training Playbook calls going back to before the spike and skipping
  the bad batches the most common fix for spikes.
- That playbook also lists a tighter clip, z-loss and QK-norm among the other fixes that training
  teams reach for.

colleagues, OPT by Zhang and colleagues, and OLMo 2 by the OLMo team.
