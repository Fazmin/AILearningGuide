## The big picture

A pretraining run is a long, expensive loop that can fail in the middle. The loss can jump and
not come back (a **loss spike**), climb until the numbers stop being numbers (**divergence**),
or drift quietly upward while every value stays finite. This module is about catching those
failures on a dashboard and applying the fix that matches each one.

It builds on **Train one from scratch**, whose rank-8 factored model halted at a learning rate of
8, and on **Optimizers in practice**, which introduced Adam and warmup. Here the same model is
broken four ways on purpose and repaired four ways.

### Where it shows up

- **Spikes in production.** PaLM saw roughly 20 loss spikes while training its largest model,
  with gradient clipping on. Each time it restarted from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the
  spike and skipped roughly 200 to 500 batches (Chowdhery et al., JMLR 24(240), Section 5.1).
- **Restarts as routine.** OPT's training log recovers from divergences by lowering the learning
  rate and restarting from an earlier checkpoint, and ties divergence to its dynamic loss scale
  collapsing toward zero (Zhang et al., arXiv 2205.01068, Section 2.5).
- **Stability built in.** OLMo 2 adds QK-norm and a z-loss term, drops weight decay on
  embeddings, and filters documents with long repeated n-grams because data caused both
  gradient-norm and loss spikes (Team OLMo, arXiv 2501.00656, Sections 2.1, 3.1, 3.3 and 3.4).
- **Number formats.** Training in 16-bit floats needs **loss scaling** with fp16, and PyTorch's
  GradScaler does it dynamically; bf16 has fp32's range and usually needs none.

### Why it matters

A failed step does not cost one step. A run that diverges at step 154 of 480 has wasted every
step since its last checkpoint, and a run that drifts into a bad state without halting can waste
the rest of its budget before anyone notices. At frontier scale those are weeks of accelerator
time.

The fixes are cheap when they match the failure and useless when they do not. A gradient clip
does nothing for an fp16 overflow, a loss scale does nothing for a too-large learning rate, and
z-loss does not rescue a run whose steps are simply too big. Reading the dashboard is how you
choose.

### What the lab shows

Every run trains for real, 480 optimizer steps of the rank-8 factored model on the six training
sentences of Harbor weather notes, from the same seed. **Run monitor** draws four signals the
trainer measured at every step, and **Run report** prints the run's outcome. Each panel shades
a normal range, the 5th to 95th percentile of a clean reference run.

Three parts are not plain measurements:

- **fp16 overflow** is emulated: each gradient entry is multiplied by the loss scale, rounded to
  half precision and divided back. The forward pass, the weights and the update stay in 32 bits.
- **Garbage batch** injects five batches of junk text at steps 400 to 404, a cause the lab knows
  in advance and a real run does not.
- **Number formats** is closed-form arithmetic on the definitions of fp32, bf16 and fp16.

## What it is

### The model, and why it can blow up

The model predicts each character from the one before it. Its 30 × 30 table of scores is stored
as the product of two thin matrices, 30 × 8 and 8 × 30, so it has 480 weights. It trains on 378
character pairs, in 24 batches of 16 per epoch, for 20 epochs: 480 steps.

The two matrices are why it can diverge. Each matrix's gradient is multiplied by the other
matrix, so a step that makes one matrix larger makes the next gradient for the other larger too.
With a small learning rate that feedback is harmless. With a large one, every oversized step
feeds a larger one.

The single-table model of **Hyperparameters & schedules** cannot do this: its gradient for one
row is a probability vector minus a one-hot vector, so its length is at most √2.

### Four signals

- **Loss.** The thin line is each batch's own cross-entropy before the update; the thick line is
  the whole-corpus loss every 4 steps. The dotted line is ln 30 = 3.401, a model that knows
  nothing.
- **Gradient norm.** The length of the whole batch gradient before clipping, on a log scale.
  Exponential growth shows as a straight line.
- **Max logit.** The largest raw score any example in the batch gave any character. Very large
  logits make the softmax nearly certain, and a wrong certain answer costs a huge loss.
- **Update ÷ weight.** The **update-to-weight ratio**: the length of the step's change to the
  weights divided by the length of the weights. It is the only signal that measures the step
  itself.

### Four faults

- **Learning rate too high.** Plain SGD at 8, sixteen times the healthy 0.5.
- **fp16 overflow.** The healthy run with its gradients cast to half precision under a **loss
  scale** of 2²⁰.
- **Garbage batch.** Adam at 0.1, with the text `qqqq zzzz xxxx jjjj` in place of the real
  batches at steps 400 to 404.
- **Logit growth.** Adam at 1, ten times the garbage run's rate.

Adam here has no weight decay, so it is AdamW with its decay set to zero. **Fault** set to None
shows the healthy run: plain SGD at 0.5, the recipe of Train one from scratch.

### The fixes

- **Gradient clipping.** If the batch gradient's norm exceeds the threshold, the whole gradient
  is multiplied by threshold ÷ norm. The direction is kept and the step's length is capped.
- **Warmup + cosine.** The rate starts at a tenth of its peak, reaches it after 15 percent of the
  steps, then follows half a cosine down to 2 percent: small steps while the weights are new,
  and again while they settle.
- **Loss scaling.** Each gradient entry is multiplied by the scale before the fp16 cast and
  divided by it afterwards, which moves small values above fp16's rounding-to-zero point and
  large ones toward its overflow point of 65,504. A static scale applies an overflow; a dynamic
  one skips that step, halves the scale, and doubles it after 200 clean steps.
- **Roll back and skip.** Reload the checkpoint saved after step 350 and train forward again,
  reading the batches at steps 400 to 404 but never applying them.
- **z-loss.** Add z · (log Z)² to each example's loss, where log Z is the log of the softmax's
  normalizer. Shifting every logit by the same amount leaves the softmax alone but moves log Z,
  so the ordinary loss puts no pull on the logits' overall level, and this term adds one. At
  log Z = 20 and z = 0.01 the term is 4 nats, more than a healthy run's whole loss; at log Z = 2
  it is 0.04. The term acts through the gradient: every loss the lab prints is cross-entropy
  alone, so runs with and without z-loss are scored on the same scale.

## Why it’s here

Every earlier training lab assumed the run finished. Large runs often do not, and the people
running them spend much of their effort keeping them on track: logging signals, saving
checkpoints, and deciding within hours whether a bump in the loss is noise, a spike that will
recover, or the start of a divergence.

Each fix in this lab answers one mechanism. **Gradient clipping** bounds the length of a step.
Warmup and decay change how the learning rate moves over the run. Loss scaling moves gradients
into fp16's range. **Checkpoint rollback** with **batch skipping** undoes damage already done.
**z-loss** pulls the **log-partition** toward zero. None of them substitutes for another, which
is why the dashboard comes first.

## How to play with it

### Read a healthy dashboard

With **Fault** on None, the run finishes at 2.039 nats. Read each panel's shaded band, which is
this run's own 5th to 95th percentile:

- **Loss**: 1.79 to 3.38 per batch, while the whole-corpus line falls toward 2.
- **Gradient norm**: 0.114 to 0.700, peaking at 0.955.
- **Max logit**: 0.118 to 6.23.
- **Update ÷ weight**: 0.021 to 0.040, with a median of 0.026.

The last band sits about 26 times above the dashed 10⁻³ line, the rule of thumb from the CS231n
course notes. That line is a starting point for large networks, not a test this toy has to pass.

### Push the learning rate

Set **Fault** to Learning rate too high. On step 1 the loss, the gradient norm and the max logit
match the healthy run exactly, because they are measured before any update. **Update ÷ weight**
already reads 0.310 against the healthy 0.021: the step is sixteen times longer.

The gradient norm does not rise above its band until step 6, passes 10 at step 36, and then
climbs in a straight line on its log axis. At step 153 it is 1.1 × 10¹⁹ and the max logit is 3.1 × 10³⁸,
close to the largest 32-bit float. On step 154 a value overflows and the run halts.

Now set **Gradient clip** to 0.50. The clip rescales 459 of 480 steps and the run finishes, at
3.172 nats, barely below chance. Set **Schedule** to Warmup + cosine, keeping the clip, and it
ends at 1.904: better than the healthy run, at sixteen times its rate.

### Overflow in fp16

Set **Fault** to fp16 overflow, with **Loss scale** at 2²⁰ and **Scaling** on Static. At that
scale a gradient entry overflows once it reaches 0.062, and on step 33 one does. A red triangle
marks the overflow on **Gradient norm**, the infinite gradient goes straight into the weights,
and the run halts on the same step.

Switch **Scaling** to Dynamic. Now an overflowing step is skipped and the scale is halved, and
after 200 clean steps it doubles again. The run skips steps 33, 62, 184 and 430, ends at a scale
of 2¹⁷, and finishes at 2.040 nats against 2.039 with no fp16 at all.

### Survive a bad batch

Set **Fault** to Garbage batch. The clean version of this Adam run finishes at 1.991. Here the
first garbage batch scores 6.636 nats on step 400 and pushes the gradient norm to 2.806, far above
its clean band of 0.483 to 0.851. The **Spike** readout shows the whole-corpus loss rising by
1.395 nats, from 2.051 to 3.447 at step 404, above the dotted line.

Set **Gradient clip** to 1.00. The spike shrinks to 0.734 nats, peaking at step 408, but the
garbage still went in. Set the clip back to off and press **Roll back and skip**. The run
restarts from the checkpoint saved after step 350, replays steps 351 to 399 exactly, never
applies steps 400 to 404, and finishes at 1.993, against 2.022 without the rollback.

### Tame logit growth

Set **Fault** to Logit growth. Adam moves each weight by about the learning rate on its first
step, here 1, so on step 1 **Update ÷ weight** reads 0.989. The whole-corpus loss is above the dotted line by
step 4 and ends at 11.515 nats. **Max logit** has a median of 66 and peaks at 395, and nothing
halts, because those are still ordinary numbers.

Raise **z-loss** to 0.01. The median log-partition falls from 19.7 to 2.2 and the peak max logit
from 395 to 179, but the loss ends at 3.277, still near chance. Lower **Learning rate** to 0.1
and it ends at 1.985.

## What to notice

### On the dashboard

- **The step is the earliest signal.** At a learning rate of 8, the update ratio is out of range
  on step 1, while the gradient norm and the max logit only rise above their bands on step 6.
- **Log axes turn explosions into lines.** A straight climb on Gradient norm or Max logit means
  each step multiplies the value by a roughly constant factor.
- **A finite run can still be ruined.** Warmup + cosine with no clip never halts, but the loss
  ends at 19.152 and the max logit peaks at 1.2 × 10²⁸. Over its last 50 steps the update ratio
  has a median of 0.0026, close to the 10⁻³ rule of thumb.
- **The batch loss jumps first.** At Garbage batch the batch loss spikes on step 400, the
  first garbage step, and the whole-corpus loss peaks on step 404, after five damaging updates.

### On the fixes

- **Neither fix for the high rate works alone.** Warmup + cosine without a clip ends at 19.152.
  A clip of 0.50 on a constant rate ends at 3.172, and a clip of 1.00 at 4.050, worse than
  chance. Clip and schedule together reach 1.904.
- **Dynamic scaling costs almost nothing.** Four skipped steps out of 480 move the final loss
  from 2.039 to 2.040.
- **The optimizer decides how much damage garbage does.** Under plain SGD at 0.5, the same five
  batches raise the whole-corpus loss by only 0.044, and their gradient norm, 0.608 on the first,
  never leaves the healthy band. Under Adam they raise it by 1.395.
- **A clip under Adam helps, but less.** Clipping at 1.00 roughly halves the Adam spike. Adam
  divides each weight's step by that weight's recent gradient size, and the rows for j and x,
  letters that never start a pair in the clean text, have no gradient history at all.

### On the rollback

- **The replay is exact.** The dotted amber line, the run without the rollback, lies under the
  new run until step 400, because the same weights meet the same batches in the same order.
- **Skipping costs little.** Leaving out five batches ends at 1.993, against 1.991 for the run
  that never saw garbage, and 2.022 for the run that trained on it.
- **The checkpoint sets the price.** Restarting from step 350 means redoing the 49 steps from 351
  to 399; a run that saves checkpoints less often redoes more.

### On z-loss

- **It treats a symptom.** At every rate on the control, z-loss at 0.01 lowers the median
  log-partition, but at a rate of 1 it leaves the loss near chance, at 3.277.
- **It barely matters in a healthy run.** At a rate of 0.1 the run ends at 1.985 with it and
  1.991 without, while the peak max logit halves, from 12.2 to 6.1.

### On the number formats

- **fp16's window moves, it does not grow.** Each doubling of the loss scale moves both ends of
  the green bar down by the same factor. From 2¹⁷ to 2¹⁸ its top falls from about 0.5 to 0.25,
  and the static run goes from finishing to halting at step 184.
- **bf16 has fp32's reach.** Its bar spans the same powers of ten as fp32's, which is why bf16
  runs usually need no loss scale; it pays with 7 fraction bits against fp32's 23.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The divergence is real: at a learning rate of 8 a weight overflows the 32-bit
float range and the run halts at step 154. Everything else about this model is small. It has 480
weights, no attention, one corpus of 378 pairs, and one seed, so each number here is one sample.

fp16 is emulated only where the gradient is cast. The forward pass, the activations and the
weights stay in 32 bits, so activation overflow cannot happen here. Underflow does not happen
either: across the whole healthy run, none of the 153,008 nonzero gradient entries rounds to zero,
even at a scale of 1. The smallest entry in the 32-bit run, 3.6 × 10⁻⁸, sits just above fp16's
rounding-to-zero point of 3.0 × 10⁻⁸; at a scale of 1, 949 entries land in fp16's coarser
subnormal range. The lab shows only the overflow side of loss scaling.

The garbage batch has a known cause, injected on purpose. The lab stores no checkpoints either:
**Roll back and skip** retrains from step 1 with steps 400 to 404 skipped, which equals
restarting from the step-350 checkpoint because the first 399 steps replay bit for bit.

The **Max logit** panel shows output logits only; with no attention layers, the attention-logit
growth that QK-norm addresses can only be described here, not shown.

The Adam runs use no weight decay. With AdamW's decoupled decay of 0.1 at a rate of 1, the max
logit peaks at only 17.8, though the loss still ends at 3.136, near chance. Decay alone reins in
these logits, as Wortsman and colleagues also observed for larger models.

The losses here cap each character pair at about 27.6 nats, because each probability is floored
at one in a trillion. In the Logit growth run, 63 of the 378 pairs sit at that floor, so 11.515 is
a lower bound.

The update ratio's rule of thumb does not hold for this toy: its healthy runs sit at 0.021 to
0.040, twenty to forty times the 10⁻³ line.

**In real systems.** Spikes rarely announce their cause. PaLM replayed the batches from around a
spike starting from a different, earlier checkpoint and saw no spike, so it concluded that spikes
come from particular batches meeting a particular state of the weights, not from bad data alone
(Chowdhery et al., Section 5.1).

Adam itself can be part of the cause. Molybog and colleagues argue that Adam can reach a state
where its update has a large norm and is essentially unrelated to the direction that lowers the
loss, which leads to divergence, more often in deep models with large batches (arXiv 2304.09871).

Not every instability is a spike. Wortsman and colleagues study slow divergence instead, and show
that attention-logit growth and output-logit divergence appear in small models at high learning
rates, so small models can stand in for large ones when studying them (arXiv 2309.14322).

Mixed precision fails in its own ways. OPT tied its divergences to its dynamic loss scale
crashing toward zero (Zhang et al., Section 2.5). Micikevicius and colleagues keep an fp32 master
copy of the weights because 16-bit numbers can round a small update to a large weight away
(arXiv 1710.03740). bf16 removes the overflow but, with only 7 fraction bits, rounds more coarsely
than fp16 (Kalamkar et al., arXiv 1905.12322).

## Going deeper

### Attention logits and QK-norm

In a Transformer, attention logits are dot products of queries and keys, and they can grow just
as output logits do. ViT-22B saw its loss diverge after a few thousand steps because attention
logits became so large that attention was almost one-hot, and fixed it by normalizing queries and
keys before the dot product (Dehghani et al., arXiv 2302.05442, Section 2).

Wortsman and colleagues reproduce the same growth in small models and show that qk-layernorm
removes it, and OLMo 2 normalizes keys and queries with RMSNorm for the same reason. **Modern
attention at scale** shows the attention computation these logits come from.

### z-loss in practice

PaLM adds 10⁻⁴ times the squared log-partition to the loss "to encourage the softmax normalizer
log(Z) to be close to 0", which it found increases stability (Chowdhery et al., Section 5).
Wortsman and colleagues use the same coefficient. ST-MoE applies a router z-loss with a
coefficient of 0.001 to the logits that choose experts in a mixture-of-experts model (Zoph et
al., arXiv 2202.08906).

OLMo 2 also uses z-loss, but its text and its table give different weights, 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻⁵, so
check the exact value before copying it.

### Prevention: architecture, initialization and data

Most stability work happens before the run starts. Takase and colleagues trace spikes to sudden
growth of the gradient norm and derive conditions that prevent it: small sub-layers and a large
shortcut, checked with scaled or normalized embeddings (arXiv 2312.16903).

OLMo 2 changes its initialization, removes weight decay from embeddings, shrinks AdamW's epsilon
from 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁸, and removes documents with 32 or more repeated n-grams, which it found caused
spikes. That is data cleaning as a stability fix, the subject of **Building a dataset**.

### Toy versus real

- **Clipping.** PaLM clips the global gradient norm at 1.0 (Chowdhery et al., Section 5), and OPT
  lowered its clip from 1.0 to 0.3 to help stability. At a rate of 8 the lab's factored model
  needs 0.50 to end below chance.
- **Loss scale.** PyTorch's GradScaler defaults to a starting scale of 2¹⁶ = 65,536, a backoff
  factor of 0.5 and a growth factor of 2 every 2,000 steps; the lab doubles after 200, because its
  run is only 480 steps long. PyTorch's examples note that the optimizer step is skipped when the
  gradients hold infs or NaNs.
- **Rollback.** PaLM skipped 200 to 500 batches per spike; the lab skips 5, because it knows
  which 5 are bad. PaLM's run was bitwise reproducible from any checkpoint, the property the lab's
  exact replay of steps 351 to 399 shows.
- **Practice.** The Smol Training Playbook calls rewinding to before the spike and skipping the
  problematic batches the most common fix for spikes, and lists tighter clipping, z-loss and
  QK-norm among the others (Ben Allal et al., Hugging Face, 2025).
