## The big picture

Training a large AI model means running one loop millions of times. In each round, the model makes guesses, and a score says how wrong they were. Then the model's numbers get nudged, so the next guesses come out a little better.

A run like that can take weeks on thousands of computer chips, and it can break in the middle. The score for how wrong the model is gets called the **loss**. When the loss suddenly jumps up, that jump is a **loss spike**, and the run may not recover.

The numbers can also grow until the computer can no longer store them. Then the run stops for good, and that is called **divergence**. Sometimes nothing stops at all, and the model just gets quietly worse while every number looks ordinary.

This module is about catching those problems early and fixing each one with the right tool. The teams behind famous models have written about this. PaLM, a large model from Google, hit about 20 loss spikes while it trained. Each time, its team went back to an earlier saved copy and left out some training data. You will try that same fix in this lab.

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain these five things:

- How to read a training dashboard. It is a screen of four live signals, and you will learn when one leaves its normal range.
- Why the small model in this lab blows up when its steps are too big. You will also see what a cap on each step changes.
- Why 16-bit numbers hold only a narrow range of sizes. Training multiplies them by a big factor to fit, and that factor can adjust itself.
- How to spot a loss spike and recover from it. You go back to a saved copy of the model and skip the bad data.
- How the model's raw scores can grow without any limit. You will see what a small extra penalty, called z-loss, can and cannot do.

The lab gives you four cards, and each one shows a different part of the story:

- **Fault and fixes:** This card lets you break the same tiny model in one of four ways. Then you try the fixes for that problem.
- **Run monitor:** This card is the dashboard itself. It has four charts of signals measured at every step of a real run.
- **Run report:** This card prints how the run ended. It adds a sentence about what happened and what to try next.
- **Number formats:** This card pictures how big and how small three kinds of computer numbers can get. It comes from their definitions, not from measuring.

Every run is real training on your own computer, so every number on the dashboard was measured. Only two things are staged: 16-bit numbers enter at just one point of each step. The bad data in one fault is junk text that the lab adds.

## What it is

### The model in this lab

This is the same model you trained in **Train one from scratch**. It reads six sentences about a harbor and learns to guess each character from the one before it. That gives it 378 pairs of neighbouring characters to learn from.

Everything the model knows sits in two small tables of numbers, called **weights**. It scores the next character by multiplying the two tables together. One table has 30 rows of 8 numbers. The other has 8 rows of 30 numbers, so together they hold 480 weights.

Training looks at 16 pairs at a time, and that group is called a **batch**. After each batch it nudges the weights once, and that nudge is called a **step**. The 378 pairs make 24 batches, and the run goes through all of them 20 times. So a full run is 480 steps long.

At each step, training works out which way to push every weight to lower the loss. That list of pushes is called the **gradient**. The weights then move by the gradient times one number. That number is the **learning rate**, and it sets how big each step is.

### Why two tables can blow up

Picture a microphone held too close to a loudspeaker. A sound goes into the microphone and comes out of the speaker a little louder. Then it goes back in and comes out louder again, until the whole room hears a squeal.

That is called a **feedback loop**, because each trip around makes the next trip bigger. The two tables in this model form one. The push on each table gets multiplied by the other table.

So a step that makes one table bigger also makes the next push on the other table bigger. With small steps, the loop stays quiet, like a microphone held far from the speaker that never squeals.

With a learning rate that is too big, each oversized step feeds an even bigger one. Some number finally grows too large for the computer to store.

Here the picture stops matching, because a microphone squeals almost at once. This model's loop grows step by step, over about 150 steps. The single-table model in **Hyperparameters & schedules** cannot do this, because its pushes have a fixed upper size.

### Four signals on the dashboard

The **Run monitor** card draws four signals for every step, and together they make the dashboard:

- **Loss:** This chart shows how wrong the model is. A thin line shows each batch's loss, and a thick line shows the loss on all 378 pairs.
- **The dotted line:** It sits at 3.401 on the loss chart. That marks a model that knows nothing and spreads its guesses evenly over 30 characters.
- **Gradient norm:** This is the overall size of the gradient. You square every push, add the squares and take the square root.
- **Max logit:** This is the model's largest raw score for any next character. These raw scores are called **logits**, and the model turns them into chances.
- **A huge logit:** It means the model is almost completely sure of one character. If that sure answer is wrong, it costs a huge loss.
- **Update ÷ weight:** This compares how far one step moved the weights with how big they are. This **update-to-weight ratio** is the only one that measures the step itself.

Here is a worked example of the update ratio. Say all the weights together have a length of 2. One step moves them by a length of 0.05. Then the ratio is 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025, close to this lab's healthy runs.

Three of the charts use a **log scale**. Each label up the side is a power of ten, such as 10, 100 and 1,000. On a log scale, a number that keeps getting multiplied by the same amount draws a straight line.

Each chart also shades a band called the **normal range**. It runs from the 5th to the 95th percentile of a clean run. That means 90 percent of the clean run's values fall inside it. A signal that leaves its band is the warning sign.

### Four ways to break it

The **Fault** control picks how the run breaks. Each choice changes one thing about the healthy run:

- **None:** This is the healthy run, which steps at a learning rate of 0.5.
- **Learning rate too high:** This is the same run with a learning rate of 8, sixteen times bigger.
- **fp16 overflow:** This is the healthy run, but each gradient gets squeezed into a 16-bit number. First it is multiplied by a big number called a **loss scale**.
- **Garbage batch:** This run swaps five real batches, at steps 400 to 404, for junk text. The junk reads `qqqq zzzz xxxx jjjj`.
- **Logit growth:** This run uses a learning rate of 1 and a different stepping rule, explained next.

The first three faults use the plain stepping rule from earlier labs. It moves each weight by its push times the learning rate. The last two use **Adam**, from **Optimizers in practice**. Adam sizes each weight's step using an average of that weight's recent push sizes.

This Adam has no weight decay, the small pull toward zero that AdamW adds. So it acts exactly like AdamW with that pull switched off.

### Clipping and warmup

**Gradient clipping** puts a cap on the size of the gradient. When the gradient norm is bigger than the cap, every push shrinks by the same factor. The shrinking stops when the norm equals the cap. The direction stays the same, and only the length changes.

Picture a gradient with just two pushes, 3 and 4. Their squares, 9 and 16, add up to 25. The square root of 25 is 5, so the norm is 5. With a cap of 1, both pushes get divided by 5, which gives 0.6 and 0.8.

**Warmup + cosine** changes the learning rate as the run goes on. It starts at a tenth of its top value. It reaches the top after 15 percent of the steps. Then it slides down a smooth curve to 2 percent of the top.

That gives small steps while the weights are fresh. It gives big steps while there is a lot left to learn. Then it gives small steps again while the weights settle.

### Loss scaling

Computers store numbers using a fixed number of bits, the 0s and 1s in memory. A normal number here uses 32 bits, but training often uses 16-bit numbers to go faster. Mixing the two sizes is called **mixed precision**.

The catch is that 16 bits cannot hold every size of number. There are two common 16-bit formats, called **fp16 and bf16** in the glossary, with different trade-offs.

The fp16 format can only hold numbers up to 65,504. Very tiny numbers round down to zero in it. The bf16 format reaches as far as a 32-bit number in both directions, but it rounds more roughly.

**Loss scaling** helps fp16 by multiplying every gradient by a big number, the loss scale, before the squeeze. Then it divides the same number back out. That lifts tiny pushes above the point where they would round to zero.

For example, a push of 0.00000004 sits just above fp16's round-to-zero point. Multiplied by 2²⁰, about a million, it becomes about 0.042. The fp16 format stores that easily.

The danger is a scale that is too big. Then a large value gets pushed past 65,504. That is called an **overflow**, and it turns the number into infinity. Infinity then spreads into every weight it touches. With that same scale of 2²⁰, a push of just 0.1 becomes about 105,000. That is far past 65,504, so it overflows.

A static scale stays the same for the whole run, while **dynamic loss scaling** watches for overflows. When one happens, it skips that step and halves the scale. After 200 clean steps in a row, it doubles the scale again.

### Roll back and skip

Training saves a copy of the weights every so often, and each copy is called a **checkpoint**. This lab assumes one gets saved every 50 steps. So there is always a recent copy to go back to.

**Checkpoint rollback** means going back to a copy saved before a problem and training forward from there. **Batch skipping** means leaving out the batches that were in use around the problem.

### z-loss

The model turns its logits into chances with a formula. It raises a special number, about 2.718, to the power of each logit and divides by the total. The log of that total is called the **log-partition**, written log Z.

Adding the same amount to every logit leaves the chances the same, but it moves log Z. So nothing in the ordinary loss stops all the logits from drifting up together.

A **z-loss** adds a small penalty to every example's loss. The penalty is a small number times log Z squared. It pulls log Z back toward zero, so the logits stop drifting as a group.

Here is an example. Say log Z is 20 and the coefficient is 0.01. Then the penalty is 0.01 × 20 × 20 = 4, which is bigger than a healthy model's loss. With log Z at 2, the same penalty is only 0.04.

## Why it’s here

Every earlier training lab assumed the run would finish, but large runs often do not. So the people running them spend much of their time keeping runs on track. They log signals and save checkpoints. They also decide fast if a bump in the loss is noise, a spike or the start of divergence.

A crash is expensive. If a run breaks at step 154 of 480, every step since the last checkpoint is wasted. A run that drifts quietly can waste the rest of its time before anyone notices. For the largest models, that can mean weeks of work on thousands of chips.

That is why teams build these checks in from the start. They do not wait until a failure has already cost them time.

Each fix in this lab answers one problem, and no fix can stand in for another. A clip does nothing for an fp16 overflow. A loss scale does nothing for steps that are too big. In the same way, z-loss cannot rescue a run whose steps are too large. So you read the dashboard first, then pick the matching fix.

## How to play with it

### Read a healthy dashboard

Start with **Fault** on None, where the run ends with a loss of 2.039. That is well below the 3.401 of a model that knows nothing.

Read the shaded band on each chart, which comes from this run itself. So each band shows what normal looks like:

- **Loss:** It runs from 1.79 to 3.38 for single batches. Meanwhile the thick line falls steadily toward 2.
- **Gradient norm:** It runs from 0.114 to 0.700. Its highest value in the whole run is 0.955.
- **Max logit:** It runs from 0.118 to 6.23. Its highest value in the whole run is 6.707.
- **Update ÷ weight:** It runs from 0.021 to 0.040, with a middle value of 0.026.

A dashed line on **Update ÷ weight** marks 0.001, written 10⁻³. It is a rule of thumb for big networks from a Stanford course. This healthy run sits about 26 times above it.

That rule is a starting point for large models. It is not a test this tiny model must pass, so the band matters more than the line.

### Push the learning rate

Set **Fault** to Learning rate too high and look at step 1. The loss, the gradient norm and the max logit all match the healthy run exactly. They match because all three are measured before the first step moves any weight.

Only **Update ÷ weight** differs on step 1, reading 0.310 against 0.021 for the healthy run. That is because the step itself is sixteen times longer.

The gradient norm does not rise above its band until step 6, and it passes 10 at step 36. After that it climbs in a straight line on its log scale. It gets multiplied by about the same amount every step.

By step 153, the gradient norm is 1.1 × 10¹⁹, which means 11 followed by 18 zeros. The max logit is 3.1 × 10³⁸, close to the largest number a 32-bit float can hold.

On step 154, a number finally overflows and the run halts. A red line marks the step where every chart stops.

**Try this:** Set **Gradient clip** to 0.50 and watch the run survive. The cap shrinks 459 of the 480 steps. The run ends at 3.172, only a little better than a model that knows nothing.

Now set **Schedule** to Warmup + cosine and keep the clip, and the run ends at 1.904. That beats the healthy run's 2.039, even though its top learning rate is sixteen times higher.

### Overflow in fp16

Set **Fault** to fp16 overflow, with **Loss scale** at 2²⁰ and **Scaling** on Static. Here 2²⁰ means 2 multiplied by itself 20 times, which makes 1,048,576.

At that scale, any push of 0.062 or more overflows once multiplied, and on step 33 one does. A red triangle marks it at the top of **Gradient norm**.

The infinite push goes straight into the weights. So the run halts on that same step, and the red halt line falls right where the triangle is.

Now switch **Scaling** to Dynamic, so each step that overflows gets skipped and the scale gets halved. The run skips steps 33, 62, 184 and 430, and its scale ends at 2¹⁷.

It finishes at 2.040, against 2.039 for the run with no 16-bit numbers at all. Skipping four steps out of 480 cost almost nothing.

### Survive a bad batch

Set **Fault** to Garbage batch, and remember that the same Adam run without junk ends at 1.991.

With the junk, the first bad batch scores 6.636 on step 400 and pushes the gradient norm to 2.806. That is far above its band of 0.483 to 0.851.

The **Spike** readout shows the loss on all the pairs rising by 1.395. It goes from 2.051 to 3.447 at step 404. That is above the dotted line, so for a moment the model does worse than knowing nothing.

Set **Gradient clip** to 1.00 and read **Spike** again. The spike shrinks to 0.734 and peaks at step 408. But the junk still went into the weights.

Set the clip back to off and press **Roll back and skip**. The run goes back to the checkpoint saved after step 350. It replays steps 351 to 399 exactly and never uses steps 400 to 404.

It ends at 1.993, against 2.022 without the rollback. That is almost as good as never seeing the junk at all.

### Tame logit growth

Set **Fault** to Logit growth, where Adam moves each weight by about the learning rate on its first step. Here that rate is 1, so on step 1 **Update ÷ weight** reads 0.989.

By step 4, the loss on all the pairs is already above the dotted line. The run ends at 11.515, far worse than knowing nothing.

**Max logit** has a middle value of 66 and peaks at 395. Yet nothing halts, because numbers that size are still ordinary numbers.

Raise **z-loss** to 0.01, and the middle value of log Z falls from 19.7 to 2.2. The peak max logit falls from 395 to 179. Even so, the loss still ends at 3.277, close to the 3.401 of knowing nothing.

Now lower **Learning rate** to 0.1, and the run ends at 1.985, a healthy result. The steps themselves are finally small enough.

## What to notice

### On the dashboard

- **The step warns first:** At a rate of 8, the update ratio is out of range on step 1. The others only leave their bands on step 6.
- **A straight climb on a log scale is an explosion:** Each step multiplies the value by a similar amount.
- **A run can finish and still be ruined:** Warmup + cosine with no clip never halts. But it ends at 19.152, and its max logit peaks at 1.2 × 10²⁸.
- **The rule of thumb can mislead:** In that run's last 50 steps, the ratio's middle value is 0.0026. That is near the 10⁻³ line.
- **The batch loss jumps first:** In Garbage batch, it jumps on step 400, the first junk step. The loss on all the pairs peaks on step 404.

### On the fixes

- **Neither fix for the high rate works alone:** Warmup + cosine without a clip ends at 19.152. A clip of 0.50 without a schedule ends at 3.172.
- **A looser cap can be worse:** A clip of 1.00 alone ends at 4.050, worse than knowing nothing. Clip and schedule together reach 1.904.
- **Dynamic scaling is cheap:** Its four skipped steps move the final loss only from 2.039 to 2.040.
- **The stepping rule sets the damage:** The plain rule at 0.5 lets the junk add only 0.044. Under Adam, the loss rises by 1.395.
- **The plain rule hides the junk:** Its gradient norm, 0.608 at the first bad step, stays in the band. So the dashboard barely notices.
- **A clip helps less under Adam:** A clip of 1.00 roughly halves the Adam spike. Adam divides each weight's step by the size of its recent pushes.
- **Some weights never move:** The weights for j and x never get a push in the clean text. Those letters never start a pair there, so the junk moves them a full step.

### On the rollback

- **The replay is exact:** The old run's dotted amber line lies under the new one until step 400. The same weights meet the same batches.
- **Skipping costs little:** Leaving out five batches ends at 1.993. That is close to the 1.991 of the run that never saw junk, and better than 2.022.
- **The checkpoint sets the price:** Going back to step 350 means redoing the 49 steps from 351 to 399. Saving less often means redoing more.

### On z-loss

- **It treats a symptom:** At every learning rate on the control, a z-loss of 0.01 lowers log Z. But at a rate of 1, the loss still ends at 3.277.
- **Healthy runs barely notice it:** At a rate of 0.1, the loss ends at 1.985 instead of 1.991. The peak max logit still halves.

### On the number formats

- **The fp16 window moves but does not grow:** Each doubling of **Loss scale** slides the whole green bar down.
- **One doubling can break the run:** From 2¹⁷ to 2¹⁸, the bar's top falls from about 0.5 to 0.25. The static run then halts at step 184.
- **The bf16 format reaches as far as fp32:** That is why bf16 runs usually need no loss scale. But bf16 keeps only 7 bits of detail, against 23 for fp32.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

The blow-up is real. At a learning rate of 8, a weight grows past the largest number a 32-bit float can hold. That halts the run at step 154.

Everything else about this model is small: 480 weights, no attention, one short text and one seed. So each number here comes from a single run.

The 16-bit numbers enter at only one point, where the gradient gets squeezed into fp16. The guesses and the weights stay in 32 bits. So the lab cannot show any other number overflowing.

The lab cannot show the other fp16 problem either. Across the whole healthy run, there are 153,008 pushes that are not already zero. None of them rounds down to zero, even with no scaling.

The smallest of those pushes is 3.6 × 10⁻⁸. That sits just above fp16's round-to-zero point of 3.0 × 10⁻⁸. With no scaling, 949 of them do land in fp16's less exact range near zero.

So this lab shows only the overflow side of loss scaling. It never shows the side that loss scaling was invented for.

**A common mix-up:** A run that never halts is not always healthy. Logit growth never makes a number the computer cannot store. Even so, that run ends at 11.515, far worse than knowing nothing.

A halt is only the loudest kind of failure. The dashboard's quieter signals are there to catch the rest.

The junk batch has a known cause, because the lab puts it in on purpose. The lab also never stores a checkpoint. **Roll back and skip** trains again from step 1 with the five junk batches skipped.

That gives exactly what restarting from step 350 would. The first 399 steps replay the same way both times.

The **Max logit** chart shows only the model's final scores. This model has no attention, so the growth real models see inside attention can only be described here.

The Adam runs have no weight decay. With a decay of 0.1 at a rate of 1, the peak max logit stays at only 17.8. The loss still ends at 3.136, close to knowing nothing. So decay alone calms these logits without fixing the run.

A research team saw the same calming effect of weight decay on the output logits of larger models.

Each pair's loss is capped at about 27.6. The lab never lets a chance fall below one in a trillion. In the Logit growth run, 63 of the 378 pairs sit at that cap. So 11.515 is the lowest the true loss could be.

The update ratio's rule of thumb does not fit this toy either. Its healthy runs sit between 0.021 and 0.040. That is twenty to forty times the 10⁻³ line.

### Where the real mechanism fails

Real spikes rarely announce their cause. PaLM's team replayed the batches from around a spike, starting from an earlier checkpoint, and no spike appeared. They concluded that spikes come from certain batches meeting certain weights. Bad data alone did not explain them.

Adam itself can be part of the cause. One research team argues that Adam can reach a bad state. In that state its steps are large and point in a direction unrelated to lowering the loss. They say it leads to divergence, most often in deep models trained on large batches.

Not every problem is a sudden spike, and another team studied slow divergence instead. They found that growing logits appear in small models trained with high learning rates. So small models can stand in for big ones when people study these problems.

Training with 16-bit numbers has its own failures. OPT, a model from Meta, linked its divergences to its dynamic loss scale crashing toward zero.

Teams also keep a 32-bit copy of the weights. A 16-bit number can round a small update to a big weight away entirely. The bf16 format rounds more roughly still.

## Going deeper

### Attention logits and QK-norm

Bigger language models use **attention**, where each piece of the input scores every other piece. Those scores are logits too, and they can grow in the same way.

A Google team trained an image model with 22 billion weights. Its loss blew up after a few thousand steps. The attention scores had grown so large that attention locked onto single patches of the image.

They fixed it by normalizing two numbers inside attention before multiplying them, a fix called **QK-norm**. The OLMo 2 team does the same. **Modern attention at scale** shows where these scores come from.

### z-loss in practice

PaLM adds a z-loss with a coefficient of 0.0001 to keep log Z close to 0. Its team found that it made training more stable. The small-model study above uses that very same coefficient.

ST-MoE is a mixture-of-experts model that sends each word to a few specialist parts. It uses a z-loss of 0.001 on the scores that pick those parts.

OLMo 2 uses z-loss too, but its paper gives two different coefficients. Its text says 0.0001, and its table says 0.00001. So check before you copy either one.

### Prevention: architecture, initialization and data

Most stability work happens before a run even starts. One research team traced spikes to sudden growth in the gradient norm. They found model shapes that prevent it.

They checked those shapes by scaling or normalizing the model's input numbers, and the spikes went away.

The OLMo 2 team changed how its weights start out. It stopped pulling some of its weights toward zero. It also removed documents that repeat the same phrase 32 or more times. The team found that such data caused spikes.

That is cleaning data as a stability fix. It is the subject of **Building a dataset**.

### Toy versus real

- **Clipping:** PaLM caps its gradient norm at 1.0. OPT lowered its cap from 1.0 to 0.3 to keep its run stable. This lab needs a cap of 0.50.
- **Loss scale:** PyTorch, a popular training tool, starts its scale at 2¹⁶, or 65,536. Its default settings list a shrink factor of 0.5 and a growth factor of 2.
- **Growth timing:** PyTorch waits 2,000 clean steps before growing the scale. This lab waits only 200, because its whole run is just 480 steps.
- **Rollback:** PaLM went back about 100 steps and skipped about 200 to 500 batches for each spike. This lab skips only 5.
- **Why only 5:** This lab knows exactly which batches are bad, so it can skip just those.
- **Exact replay:** PaLM could replay any stretch of its run exactly from a checkpoint. This lab's replay of steps 351 to 399 shows the same idea.
- **Practice:** Hugging Face's Smol Training Playbook calls rolling back and skipping bad batches the most common fix.
- **Other fixes:** That playbook also lists a tighter clip, z-loss and QK-norm.

The real-world facts come from papers listed under References. They include PaLM by Chowdhery and colleagues, OPT by Zhang and colleagues, and OLMo 2 by the OLMo team.
