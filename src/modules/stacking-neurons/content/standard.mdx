## The big picture

*A single neuron* showed one unit splitting its input with one straight boundary. This module stacks
units: side by side in a **hidden layer**, all reading the same inputs with their own weights, and
layer on layer, each reading the one before. **Width** is the number of units in a layer; **depth**
is the number of hidden layers.

The question is what a stack can compute that one neuron cannot. Each hidden layer rewrites its input
into a **representation**, a vector the next layer finds easier to use, and a nonlinear activation
keeps the stack from collapsing into one **affine map**, a linear map plus a constant. The module
also asks whether the shape of a fixed parameter budget matters, and how to tell learning a pattern
from memorizing labels.

### Where it shows up

- **Every later architecture** answers "what should the layers be?" A transformer block is a hidden
  layer with structure: one sublayer mixes information across positions, one transforms each
  position independently.
- **Convolutional, recurrent, and mixture-of-experts layers** are hidden layers that share or route
  their weights.
- **Feature hierarchies** form across depth: edges then object parts in vision models, surface form
  then syntax in language models.
- **Interpretability** has to work with **distributed representations**: a hidden vector spreads its
  encoding across many units, so single units rarely mean one clean concept.

### Why it matters

One neuron gives one straight cut, so XOR, where each class holds one diagonal's pair of opposite
corners, is out of reach. A hidden layer lets the next unit combine several cuts into a bent
boundary, but only through a nonlinearity: without one, linear layers compose into one affine map
however many you stack.

**Capacity**, how complex a function a network can express, cuts both ways. Given enough epochs, a
large network can fit wrong training labels along with the pattern, and training accuracy alone
cannot tell memorized labels from a learned pattern.

### What you will be able to explain

- How width limits what one hidden layer can express and find, and a measured task where depth
  beats width at the same parameter count.
- What a hidden representation is, and how a trained hidden layer moves XOR's points so one straight
  line separates them.
- Why depth adds nothing without a nonlinearity between layers.
- How comparing training and held-out accuracy separates memorized noisy labels from a learned
  pattern.

### What the lab shows

A two-input network trains in the app for 300 epochs of full-batch gradient descent with momentum.
Every accuracy and loss on every card is measured from real training on small, fixed synthetic
datasets; the lab's seed, which **New initialization** changes, sets only the starting weights.
Accuracy is on training points everywhere except **Held-out data**.

- **Network training controls**: **Dataset**, **Hidden activation** (`tanh`, `ReLU`,
  `none (linear)`), **Hidden layers**, **Neurons per layer**, **Training epochs** (replays the saved
  run), **Train 50 epochs**, and **New initialization**.
- **Decision surface** and **Network builder**: the output across the input square, and one tile per
  unit, with **Probe x₁** and **Probe x₂** tracing the forward pass at one point.
- **Depth against width**: two fixed 85-parameter shapes, one wide and one deep.
- **Held-out data**: a narrow and a large network trained on the Ring and scored on 120 points they
  never train on, with **Training labels** set to `Clean` or `20% flipped` and **Curves** set to
  `Accuracy` or `Loss`; these networks always use tanh units.

The key experiment: on `Checkerboard` with `tanh` and 300 epochs, two layers of 7 fit most of the
board while one layer of 21 stays near chance. Switch to `ReLU` and the wide network ties or wins, so
the result belongs to this task, activation, and training budget, not to depth in general.

## What it is

A **hidden layer** is a set of neurons reading the same inputs in parallel, each with its own
weights and bias. A layer of `m` units over `n` inputs is `h = f(Wx + b)`, with `W` of shape
`[m, n]`. Its output vector is the layer's **representation**: a rewriting of the input that
the next layer finds easier to use.

### What this lab runs

A real network: 2 inputs, 1 to 3 hidden layers of 1 to 6 units, and one sigmoid output. It
trains by full-batch gradient descent with momentum on mean binary cross-entropy, and every
epoch's weights are kept so the **Training epochs** slider replays the run. Backpropagation is
exact and checked against finite differences. Three datasets are available: XOR, Ring, and a 4 × 4
Checkerboard of 64 points.

Two more cards train fixed networks beside it. **Depth against width** compares two shapes with the
same number of parameters. **Held-out data** scores networks on points they never trained on.

Those training terms are defined in the glossary and taught in the next labs: Loss & gradient
descent covers the loss, the step, and momentum, and Backpropagation covers where each
weight's gradient comes from. Until then, read the training step as nudging every weight to
lower one loss number.

### Why the activation is load-bearing

Compose two affine maps and you get `W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = W'x + b'`, still one affine map.
Without a nonlinearity, depth buys nothing. In the lab, `none (linear)` leaves XOR at 50% and
the loss at 0.693 = ln 2, the loss of answering 0.5 everywhere, even at 3 layers of 6.

### How two units solve XOR

At the defaults (one layer of 2 tanh units, seed 2) the trained units are
`h1 = tanh(−4.11x₁ + 4.20x₂ + 1.97)` and `h2 = tanh(4.46x₁ − 3.82x₂ + 2.79)`. Both label-0
clusters map near `(h1, h2) = (1, 1)`; the label-1 clusters map to `(1.00, −0.97)` and
`(−0.99, 1.00)`. In that space one line separates them: the output `σ(−6.39h1 − 6.36h2 + 5.74)`
fires only when `h1 + h2` is below about 0.9.

### Depth against width at the same size

Width alone solves XOR and Ring, so neither can separate depth from width. The Checkerboard can.
**Depth against width** trains two fixed networks with 85 parameters each: one hidden layer of 21
units, and two hidden layers of 7.

With tanh units, at seed 2 and 300 epochs, the one-layer network ends at 56.3% and the two-layer
network at 87.5%. Across seeds 1 to 6 the first ends between 46.9% and 57.8%, the second between
81.3% and 100%. On XOR and Ring both shapes reach 100% at every seed.

The claim is narrower than it looks. Switch **Hidden activation** to ReLU and the order turns around:
the one-layer network ends between 85.9% and 100%, level with or ahead of the two-layer network (68.8%
to 100%) on every seed. Nor does the lab show that one wide layer cannot represent the board. It shows
that this optimizer, in 300 epochs, did not find it with tanh units.

### Held-out data and label noise

Every number above is scored on the points the network trained on. **Held-out data** are points it
never trains on. The Held-out data card keeps the Ring's 60 training points and adds 120 fresh ring
points. **Training labels** can be Clean or 20% flipped: 12 of the 60 labels are swapped, the same 12
every time. That is label noise.

A narrow network (1 layer of 3 units, 13 parameters) and a large one (2 layers of 6, 67 parameters)
train on it. With clean labels both reach 100% on the training points and at least 99% on the
held-out points at seeds 1 to 6.

With flipped labels, at seed 2 and epoch 300, the large network scores 96.7% on its training points
and 79.2% on the held-out points. Its training loss falls to 0.07 while its held-out loss bottoms out
near 0.28 at epoch 71 and climbs to 0.99. The narrow network scores 90.0% and 91.7%.

Across seeds 1 to 6 the large network's gap between the two is at least 15 points; the narrow network's
is at most 7. That gap is **overfitting**: fitting wrong labels, which do not repeat on new points.

## Why it’s here

This is where "learning features" becomes concrete. A single neuron matches one template. A
stack discovers intermediate quantities nobody specified, because the only pressure on them is
that the final output be correct.

It is also where the course first scores a network on points it did not train on, the habit that
Training dynamics builds on.

## What to notice

- First-layer tiles are always split by one straight edge; second- and third-layer tiles bend.
  Composition is visible.
- Width changes what is expressible. On Ring, one layer of 2 units reaches 86.7% at seed 2;
  3 units reach 100% from epoch 74.
- Width also changes what is findable. With 2 units, seeds 1 and 3 stick at 62.5% and 68.8%
  (loss about 0.35); with 3 or more, seeds 1 to 6 all solve XOR.
- At the same size the shape can matter. On the Checkerboard with tanh, 2 × 7 beats 1 × 21 at every
  seed from 1 to 6; with ReLU, 1 × 21 does at least as well.
- With clean labels the gap between training and held-out accuracy is about zero. With 20% flipped,
  the large network's training accuracy rises while its held-out accuracy falls behind.
- `Parameters` counts `n_in × n_out + n_out` per layer: 9 at the default, 109 at 3 layers of 6.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The network has two inputs, at most 64 training points, a fixed learning rate of 0.5 with
momentum 0.9, and 300 epochs. **Decision surface** and the training controls report training
accuracy only. Held-out points are scored only on **Held-out data**, on the Ring, with tanh units and
one fixed set of 12 flipped labels; flipping a different 12 changes the size of the gap. The
decision surface is drawable only because there are two inputs.

**Depth against width** has one dataset where the shapes come apart, only with tanh, and only within
300 epochs. A longer run might close the gap, and with ReLU the wide network does. Depth stacked on a
one-unit layer does nothing, because every layer after it receives a single number.

### In real systems

More capacity does not reliably mean better results: past a point the network fits noise in
its training set, which Held-out data shows and Training dynamics shows with polynomial fits. A
held-out set also stops being held out once you use it again and again to choose models.

Depth adds an optimization problem as well as expressive power. Gradients multiplied through many
layers decay or blow up: in the Backpropagation lab's Gradient flow card, twelve layers of sigmoid
units with sensible starting weights leave the first layer's gradient at 1.2e-7 of the last layer's.
Plain deep stacks were hard to train until normalization and residual connections became standard.

A hidden vector is a **distributed representation**. Unit 3 rarely means one clean concept;
units often respond to several unrelated features, the superposition problem the
interpretability labs take up.

## Going deeper

Universal approximation results say one hidden layer of sufficient width, with a
non-polynomial activation, can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain. They
are existence proofs, silent on width, trainability, and data. For many functions a deep
network needs far fewer units than a shallow one, because it reuses intermediate results; the
Checkerboard comparison is one small case, with the caveats above.

That reuse makes a **feature hierarchy** possible: edges then object parts in vision models,
surface form then syntax then task structure in language models. Specialized layers share or
route weights: convolutions reuse a filter across positions, recurrent layers reuse a layer
across time, and mixture-of-experts layers send each token to a few of many sublayers. Loss &
gradient descent, next, opens up the training step this lab ran for you.
