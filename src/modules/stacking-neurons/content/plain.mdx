## The big picture

In the last lab you worked with a single artificial neuron, which multiplies each input by a dial
setting and adds up the results. However you set its dials, it can only ever draw one straight line
between "yes" and "no". This module connects many of those neurons, some side by side and some one
after another, so that together they form a small **neural network**.

The big question is how a team of these simple parts can draw shapes that no single part can, such
as a bent boundary or a closed ring. The module also asks when adding more parts helps, and when it
quietly makes things worse.

### Where it shows up

Stacking layers of neurons is the basic plan behind the models in the rest of this course. Models
that work with pictures and models that work with language both pass their input through many
layers, each building on what the one before it produced. Even the transformer block, which later
labs build on, is a stack of layers with some extra structure added.

### Why it matters

Without stacking, a network would be stuck drawing one straight line, which sounds like a small
limit until you meet a puzzle like XOR. XOR has four clumps of dots, one near each corner of a
square, yet a single neuron can sort at most 3 of the 4 correctly.

Two more lessons sit inside this one, and the first is that stacking only helps if each layer bends
its numbers before passing them on. Without that bend, a hundred layers collapse into one plain
multiply-and-add, which can never do better than a single straight line. The second is that bigger is not automatically better, since a
large network can memorize mistakes in its practice data, which you only catch by testing it on fresh
examples.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this module you should be able to explain:

- how adding neurons to a layer lets a network draw more complicated shapes;
- a real case in this lab where two narrow layers beat one wide layer that has exactly the same
  number of dials;
- what a hidden layer hands to the next one, which is a new description of each input;
- why the bend between layers has to be there for extra layers to add anything at all;
- how comparing a network's score on its practice dots with its score on fresh dots tells
  memorizing mistakes apart from learning the real pattern.

### What the lab shows

The lab trains a real, tiny network right in front of you, and it trains a new one every time you
change the network's shape. Every color, score and curve you see is measured from that training, not
drawn by hand. The data, on the other hand, is made up: three fixed sets of dots on a square, called
XOR, Ring and Checkerboard, with 48 to 64 dots in each.

Each of the lab's five cards shows a different side of the experiment:

- **Decision surface** colors every spot on the square by the network's answer, so you can see the
  shape it has drawn.
- **Network training controls** let you pick the puzzle and the network's shape, then train it and
  replay the training round by round.
- **Network builder** shows each neuron's own map of the square, and the numbers flowing through the
  network at one point you choose.
- **Depth against width** trains two networks with the same number of dials side by side, one wide
  and one deep.
- **Held-out data** scores two networks on fresh dots they never practiced on, and lets you flip the
  labels on some practice dots to see which network memorizes them.

## What it is

### One neuron, one straight line

Start with a quick reminder of how a single neuron works on this lab's square of dots. Each dot is
described by two numbers: x₁ says how far left or right it sits, and x₂ says how far up or down. Both
numbers run from −1 to 1, so the middle of the square is the point (0, 0).

A neuron multiplies x₁ and x₂ by its own dial settings, called weights, adds them, and then adds one
more fixed nudge called a bias. It then passes that total through an activation, a small rule that
reshapes the number before handing it on.

Every spot where the total comes out exactly zero lies on one straight line across the square. On one
side of that line the total is positive, leaning "yes", and on the other side it is negative, leaning
"no". However you set the dials, one neuron gives you one straight cut.

### Three puzzles made of dots

Every dot in the lab has a label, 1 or 0, which you can read as "yes" or "no". On the **Decision
surface**, filled circles are label 1 and open squares are label 0. The network's job is to color the
square so that each dot sits on the side that matches its label.

The background is shaded by the network's answer: green where it leans toward "yes", red where it
leans toward "no", and pale where it is unsure. A dot the network gets wrong has a red ring drawn
around it.

The **Dataset** control offers three sets of dots, each laid out so that no single straight line can
split it:

- `XOR` has 48 dots in four clumps of 12, with one clump near each corner of the square. A dot is
  "yes" when exactly one of its two numbers is positive, so the two yes clumps sit diagonally across
  from each other.
- `Ring` has 24 "yes" dots packed into a small disc in the middle, and 36 "no" dots in a band around
  them.
- `Checkerboard` splits the square into a 4 × 4 grid of small squares that alternate between yes and
  no, with 4 dots in each, so 64 in all.

The name XOR is short for "exclusive or", which is the kind of "or" on a menu that says a meal comes
with soup or salad. You get one or the other, but not both and not neither. In the XOR puzzle, "x₁ is
positive" and "x₂ is positive" play the parts of soup and salad, so the yes clumps are the top-left
and bottom-right corners.

### A hidden layer: neurons side by side

Now put several neurons side by side and give them the same two inputs, while each one keeps its own
dial settings: that group is a **hidden layer**. Each neuron in it draws its own straight line, which
shows up on the **Decision surface** as a dashed line. The lines are labeled h1, h2 and so on, where
the h stands for hidden.

The layer is called hidden because you never read its outputs as the answer. Its numbers go to the
next layer instead, and at the very end one more neuron, the output neuron, turns them into the
network's answer.

That answer is written ŷ, said "y-hat", and it is always a number between 0 and 1: near 1 means "yes"
and near 0 means "no". A dot counts as correct when ŷ lands on its label's side of 0.5, so a "yes" dot
needs an answer of at least 0.5.

### An assembly line of neurons

Picture a network as an assembly line in a furniture workshop, where logs come in at one end and a
chair comes out at the other. The first station turns logs into planks, the next turns planks into
frames, and the last turns frames into a chair. The plank station never makes a chair, and nobody
there is told about chairs, because its job is to hand the next station better raw material.

In a network, each station is a layer and each worker is a neuron, which gives two separate ways to
grow:

- Making a layer **wider** adds more workers at the same station, and in the lab you do that with
  **Neurons per layer**, from 1 to 6.
- Making the network **deeper** adds more stations, each building on what the last one made, and in
  the lab you do that with **Hidden layers**, from 1 to 3.

These two numbers, how many neurons sit in each layer and how many layers there are, are the
network's **width and depth**.

The picture gets one thing right: each station only hands its work forward, and the whole line is
judged by the finished chair. It gets one important thing backwards, though. A real plank station is
told to make planks, but a hidden neuron is told nothing at all about what to make.

Its dials are tuned only by how good the final answer turns out to be, so whatever in-between
material it ends up producing is whatever happened to help. The picture gets a few more things wrong,
which "Where it breaks" comes back to.

### Capacity: what a network could draw

More workers at one station means more straight cuts for the next station to combine. How complicated
a shape a network could draw is called its **capacity**, and it is set mainly by width and depth.

The Ring puzzle shows this clearly, because two neurons give the output neuron only two straight lines
to work with. Two crossing lines make an X, and the space between two arms of an X is a wedge that
stays open at its far end. In the lab the two lines may meet outside the square, so you see a funnel
rather than an X, but it is still open on one side.

Two lines can fence off a wedge but never a closed area. So with the Ring chosen and the other
settings left at their starting values, the network gets 86.7% of the dots right and stops there. A
third neuron adds a third line, and three lines can form a triangle that walls in the middle disc on
every side, so the score climbs to 100%.

### Counting the dials

Every number that training can adjust is called a parameter, and the **Parameters** readout counts
them for you. That total is the network's **parameter count**, and you can work it out by hand. Each
neuron has one weight for every number it reads, plus one bias of its own.

Here is the count for the lab's starting network, which has one hidden layer of two neurons:

- Each hidden neuron reads x₁ and x₂, so it has 2 weights, one for each input, plus a bias, which
  makes 3 dials.
- Since there are two hidden neurons, together they hold 6 of the network's dials.
- The output neuron reads the two hidden numbers and has its own bias, which adds 3 more.
- That makes 9 in total, which is exactly the number the **Parameters** readout shows at the start.

With 3 hidden layers of 6 neurons, the count climbs all the way to 109. That is because every neuron in
a later layer reads all six neurons of the layer before it, so each one carries 7 dials. Layer by
layer, the count goes like this:

- The first hidden layer has 6 neurons with 3 dials each, which makes 18.
- The second and third hidden layers each have 6 neurons with 7 dials each, which makes 42 apiece.
- The output neuron reads 6 numbers and adds a bias, which makes 7.
- Adding 18, 42, 42 and 7 gives 109.

Between two layers of 6, every neuron reads every neuron before it, so 6 × 6 = 36 weights sit between
them. Make both layers 12 wide and that becomes 144, four times as many, which is why width drives
most of the total.

### Representation: a new description of each dot

Now for the central idea of the module: the list of numbers a layer hands forward, one from each
neuron, is that layer's **representation** of the input. A representation is not an answer, but a new
description of the dot, written in a form the next layer finds more useful.

At the lab's starting settings, the hidden layer has two neurons using `tanh`, an activation that
keeps every output between −1 and 1. That gives each dot two new numbers, h1 and h2, and here is where
the four XOR corners end up after training:

- The "no" dot at the top right, (0.6, 0.6), becomes about (1, 1).
- The "no" dot at the bottom left, (−0.6, −0.6), also becomes about (1, 1).
- The "yes" dot at the top left, (−0.6, 0.6), becomes about (1, −1).
- The "yes" dot at the bottom right, (0.6, −0.6), becomes about (−1, 1).

Look at what happened to the two "no" corners, which sat at opposite ends of a diagonal on the
original square. In the new description they land almost on top of each other, while each "yes" dot
gets one number close to −1.

Now add up each dot's two new numbers. Both "no" corners give about 1 + 1 = 2, while both "yes"
corners give about 1 + (−1) = 0. So the output neuron only needs one cut somewhere between 0 and 2.
After training, it says "yes" when h1 + h2 comes out below about 0.9, a value set by its own dials,
which the next section reads.

If you drew h1 and h2 as a new square, every spot where h1 + h2 equals 0.9 would sit on one straight
line. Among the four corners, a sum that small only happens when one of the two numbers is near −1.
That is true for both yes dots and for neither no dot.

This is the whole trick behind solving XOR, and it shows that the hidden layer never answers the
question itself. Instead, it moves the dots into a new layout where the output neuron's one straight
line is enough.

### Reading the output neuron's sum

**Network builder** prints this last step as a formula, in the row called "Output unit at the probe".
The probe is a point you place with **Probe x₁** and **Probe x₂**, and it starts at the bottom-right
corner, (0.6, −0.6).

The lab starts at seed 2 and epoch 150. The seed picks which random starting dials the network gets,
and the epoch counts how many rounds of training it has had so far. "What training does here" explains
both more fully. At those starting settings, the row reads
`ŷ = σ((−6.39)·(−1.00) + (−6.36)·1.00 + 5.74)`. If you have already changed things, press **Reset
interactive** to get back to that starting point.

In each product, the first number is one of the output neuron's weights and the second is h1 or h2 at
the probe, while 5.74 is the output neuron's bias. The σ, a Greek letter called sigma, stands for the
sigmoid from the last lab, which squashes any total into a number between 0 and 1. Big positive totals
come out near 1 and big negative ones near 0. Here is the sum at the starting probe, step by step:

- The first product, −6.39 times −1.00, is +6.39, because a negative times a negative is positive.
- The second product, −6.36 times 1.00, is −6.36, since multiplying by 1 changes nothing.
- Those two nearly cancel, so the total, which the lab calls z, lands close to the bias. The lab shows
  z = 5.73 because it adds up the exact values, not the rounded ones printed in the row.
- The sigmoid turns 5.73 into 0.997, which is very close to 1, so the answer is "yes".

Now move **Probe x₂** up to 0.6, so the probe sits on the top-right "no" corner. Both hidden numbers are
now close to 1, so the products come out at about −6.2 and −6.3, and together they outweigh the bias. The total z
drops to about −6.8, and the sigmoid turns that into 0.001, a clear "no".

Working out an answer this way, layer by layer from the inputs to ŷ, is called the **forward pass**.
The number under each tile in **Network builder** is one step of it, read at the probe.

### The bend between layers

Every hidden neuron passes its total through an activation before handing it on, and the **Hidden
activation** control offers three choices:

- `tanh` squashes any total into a number between −1 and 1, passing small totals through almost
  unchanged and flattening big ones. A total of 3, for example, comes out as about 0.995.
- `ReLU` keeps a positive total as it is and turns any negative total into 0.
- `none (linear)` passes the total through untouched, so the neuron only multiplies and adds.

Drawn as a graph, neither `tanh` nor `ReLU` is a straight line. For `ReLU`, the inputs −2, −1, 0, 1
and 2 give 0, 0, 0, 1 and 2. Plot those points and you get a flat line along the bottom that turns a
corner at 0 and then climbs. That corner is the bend, and `tanh` has a smooth version of it, flattening
out at both ends.

An activation that bends like that is called a **nonlinearity**, and stacking only pays off because
of it.

To see why, picture a friend who doubles a cookie recipe for a party, and a second friend who then
triples that doubled batch. Together they have multiplied the original by 6, which one person could
have done in a single step. Layers without a bend behave the same way, because each one only
multiplies and adds.

Now try it with actual numbers: say one layer doubles its input and adds 1, and the next layer triples
the result and subtracts 4. Feed in 5, and the first layer turns it into 11, which the second layer
turns into 29. But multiplying 5 by 6 and subtracting 1 also gives 29, and that shortcut works for
every input you try.

So however many multiply-and-add layers you stack, they collapse into a single multiply-and-add, which
can only ever draw a single straight line. A bend breaks the shortcut, and you can check this with
`ReLU` between two layers.

Feed in −2, 0 and 2, and `ReLU` turns them into 0, 0 and 2. Any single multiply-and-add, graphed,
is a straight line, and no straight line passes through all three of these results. It would have to
stay flat at 0 for −2 and for 0, and then rise to 2 at 2.

That corner is something no multiply-and-add can copy, so the next layer receives something genuinely
new to work with. That is what lets each new layer add something the last one could not.

A stack with no bend at all actually does worse on XOR than a single `tanh` neuron. One `tanh`
neuron can still sort 3 of the 4 clumps, because it flattens out and can say a firm "no" on one side
of its line. A plain multiply-and-add never flattens out, so its total keeps changing at the same
steady rate across the whole square.

Since the two "yes" corners sit opposite each other, any tilt that pushes one of them toward "yes"
pushes the other one just as far toward "no". Those pushes cancel out, so the best training can do is
answer 0.5 everywhere, which scores 50%.

### What training does here

The network starts with random dial settings, called its **initialization**. Each random start has a
number, called its **seed**, so the same seed always gives the same start. The note under the loss
chart shows which seed you are on. Each press of **New initialization** moves to the next seed and
so draws a fresh random start. From there, training repeats one loop: measure how badly
the network misses on the practice dots, then nudge every dial a little to shrink that miss.

- The miss score is the **Training loss**, which this lab measures with **binary cross-entropy**, a
  score for yes-or-no answers that punishes confident wrong answers heavily.
- A network that shrugs and answers 0.5 for every dot gets a loss of 0.693. That is why the loss chart
  has a dotted line marked "0.693 = always 50%".
- Nudging every dial in the direction that lowers the loss is called **gradient descent**. This lab
  adds **momentum**, which lets the nudges build up speed while they keep pointing the same way.
- One full round of practice over all the dots is an **epoch**, and here every epoch is exactly one
  nudge of every dial.
- Working out how far each dial should move is the job of **backpropagation**, which runs backward
  through the same layers after the forward pass.

You do not need the details of any of these yet, because the next two labs, Loss & gradient descent
and Backpropagation, take them apart. For now, read training as nudging every dial to shrink one miss
number.

Each run lasts 300 epochs, and the lab keeps the dials from every one of them, so the **Training
epochs** slider replays the run instead of training again. Dragging it to 0 shows the random start, and
dragging it to 300 shows the end.

### Two cards that run extra networks

Two more cards train networks of their own beside the main one, and each is built to answer one
question.

**Depth against width** uses the same puzzle, activation and seed as the main controls, and compares
two shapes with the same budget of 85 dials. One has a single hidden layer of 21 neurons, and the other
has two hidden layers of 7 each, which you can count the same way as before:

- One wide layer: each of the 21 neurons has 3 dials, which makes 63. The output neuron reads all 21
  numbers and has a bias, which adds 22, for a total of 85.
- Two narrow layers: the first 7 neurons have 3 dials each, which makes 21. Each neuron in the second
  layer reads all 7 numbers from the first layer and has a bias, so it has 8 dials, and seven such
  neurons make 56. The output neuron adds 8 more, for a total of 85.

Since the size is the same, any difference between the two comes from shape alone.

**Held-out data** asks a different question: did the network learn the pattern, or did it memorize its
practice dots? It trains two networks on the Ring's 60 dots, then scores them on 120 fresh ring dots
that they never practice on.

One is a narrow network with a single hidden layer of 3 neurons, which has 13 dials. The other is a
large network with two hidden layers of 6 neurons, which has 67 dials, so it is bigger in both width
and depth. Dots kept out of training like this are called **held-out data**, because
they are held back from practice. On the card, the practice dots are called training points and the
fresh dots are called held-out points.

The card can also flip the labels on 12 of the 60 practice dots, turning some "yes" dots into "no" and
the other way around. Those flipped labels, 20% of the practice set, are called label noise, and since
they do not repeat in the fresh dots, memorizing them gains nothing. When a network fits its practice
dots so closely that it copies their mistakes too, and so does worse on fresh dots, that is called
**overfitting**.

Picture a classmate who studies for a quiz from a practice sheet where a few answers in the key were
misprinted. If they memorize the sheet word for word, misprints included, they ace the sheet, but on
the real quiz the misprints are useless or worse.

A classmate who learns the method instead answers the misprinted questions correctly, so the sheet's
key marks them wrong. They score a little lower on the sheet but just as well on the quiz. The
memorizer is the one whose sheet score is much higher than their quiz score, so the gap between the
two scores is what gives them away.

In a network, memorizing a flipped label means curving the boundary around that one dot. The curve
then gets nearby fresh dots wrong, because their labels follow the real ring.

## Why it’s here

### The puzzle one neuron could not solve

The single neuron lab ended on XOR, whose "yes" clumps sit in opposite corners, top left and bottom
right, while the "no" clumps fill the other two. Try placing one straight line across the square so
that both yes corners fall on the same side. Wherever you put it, at least one "no" corner lands on
that side too, so the best one line can do is 3 clumps out of 4.

You can watch this happen here: with **Neurons per layer** at 1, the network stalls at 75%, which is 3
clumps out of 4. Stacking more layers after that one neuron does not help, because **Neurons per
layer** sets every layer to 1. Each later layer only reshapes that same single number again, which can never turn the one straight
cut into a bend.

### Nobody tells the hidden neurons what to look for

Look again at the trained XOR network at the starting settings, where h1 is negative only on the
bottom-right side of its dashed line. That side holds only one clump of dots, the bottom-right "yes"
clump. In the same way, h2 is negative only on the top-left side of its line, which holds only the
top-left yes clump.

You could call h1 a "bottom-right corner" detector, though that name is only a story people put on it.
Its tile in **Network builder** shows what it actually does across the whole square.

Nobody wrote those jobs into the network, since the dials started out random and the only pressure on
them was that the final answer be right. A pair of corner detectors happened to make the final answer
right, so that is where training ended up, unlike a real workshop, where someone decides each
station's job. This is what people mean when they say a network learns its own features: useful in-between quantities that
nobody asked for by name.

A different random start can split the work differently, and some starts never find a good split at
all. With two neurons on XOR, one press of **New initialization** takes you from seed 2 to seed 3.
That start ends at 68.8% after 300 epochs, even though a perfect answer exists. The note under the
chart says its best was 75%, a score it reached early on before slipping lower.

That is worse than a single neuron's 75%, which seems strange at first. Training works to lower the
miss score, not to raise the number of dots it gets right. From this start, lowering the miss score
meant becoming more sure about most dots while giving up a few. The two lines settled in an unhelpful place, and training had no way to climb back out.

### Why both width and depth matter

A wider layer gives the next layer more straight cuts to combine, which is how three neurons close the
Ring. A deeper network lets later layers combine the shapes that earlier layers made, rather than only
straight cuts. That lets their boundaries bend in ways that one layer would need many more straight cuts
to copy.

The Checkerboard gives one way to picture this, though the lab does not prove it is what the trained
network does. On a checkerboard, the answer flips every time you step one square across or one square
up, which makes it like XOR repeated all over the board.

A first layer could draw straight cuts along the grid lines. A second layer could then combine those
cuts to keep track of the flips, the same way the XOR network's output neuron combined h1 and h2. One
wide layer has to do all of that in a single step, with nothing but straight cuts to combine. With
`tanh`, training in this lab did not manage that.

Which one helps more at the same size depends on the puzzle, the activation, and how long training
runs. The Checkerboard in **Depth against width** is the one place in this lab where the two shapes
come apart. There, depth wins clearly with `tanh`, while with `ReLU` the wide layer ties or wins.

### The same plan in bigger models

Every later design in this course answers the question "what should the layers be?", including the
transformer block that later labs build on. That block is a small stack of layers with extra
structure: one part mixes information between the words in a text, and another reworks each word on its own. Underneath
that extra structure, the stacking works the same way as in this lab.

### Checking on fresh dots

This is also the first lab in the course that scores a network on dots it never practiced on. That
habit matters because a score on practice dots can look perfect while hiding memorized mistakes, and
later labs, starting with Training dynamics, build on it.

## How to play with it

The numbers below come from the lab's starting seed, which is 2, and the note under the loss chart
always tells you which seed you are on. After a press of **New initialization** your exact numbers will
differ, and a few runs may get stuck. **Reset interactive** always takes you back to the starting

### Solve XOR

**Try this:** With **Hidden layers** at 1, set **Neurons per layer** to `1`, and the network scores 75%
and stalls, because one neuron gives one straight cut. Now set it to `2`: two dashed lines, h1 and h2,
appear on the **Decision surface**, and the red band between them catches both "no" clumps. Every dot is on the correct side from epoch 28 onward, which the green dashed line on the loss chart marks.

Next, drag **Training epochs** to `0` to see the random start, where only about half the dots are
right. Then press **Train 50 epochs** a few times and watch the loss fall while the boundary forms.

Press **New initialization** a few times as well, since each press draws a new random start and sends
you back to epoch 0. Train each one forward, and you will find that some starts get stuck below 100%.

### Look inside

If you pressed **New initialization** above, press **Reset interactive** first to get back to seed 2
at epoch 150. With one hidden layer of two neurons, move **Probe x₁** and **Probe x₂** to each XOR
corner in turn, such as (0.6, 0.6) and then (−0.6, −0.6). Read h1 and h2 under the tiles in
**Network builder**, where, at the starting seed, both "no" corners should give numbers near 1 for both neurons.

Then raise **Hidden layers** to `2` and compare the columns of tiles, where each tile shows one neuron's
number over the whole square. Look for how the tiles in the second column differ from the ones in the
first.

### Remove the bend

Set **Hidden activation** to `none (linear)`, then try any mix of **Hidden layers** and **Neurons per
layer**, even 3 layers of 6. Keep an eye on the **Accuracy** readout and the **Training loss** as you
change them.

### Need more width

Set **Dataset** to `Ring`, put **Hidden activation** back to `tanh`, and set **Hidden layers** to 1.
Compare 2 neurons per layer with 3, and watch whether the green "yes" area closes around the middle disc.

### Match the size

Set **Dataset** to `Checkerboard`, keep `tanh`, and drag **Training epochs** to `300`. Then, in **Depth
against width**, compare one layer of 21 neurons with two layers of 7, which both have 85 dials.

Next, switch **Hidden activation** to `ReLU` and press **New initialization** a few times. Each press
resets **Training epochs** to 0, so drag it back to 300, or read the chart's legend, which always gives
each network's score at epoch 300.

### Hold data out

On **Network training controls**, drag **Training epochs** to `300`. Then, in **Held-out data**,
switch **Training labels** between `Clean` and `20% flipped`. Watch the readouts under each chart:
**Training accuracy**, **Held-out accuracy**, **Gap**, and **Held-out loss**.

Then set **Curves** to `Loss` and watch the large network's dashed line, which tracks the fresh dots
while the solid line tracks the practice dots.

## What to notice

- Each tile in **Network builder** is one neuron's view of the whole square, shaded green where its
  number is positive and red where it is negative with `tanh`. First-layer tiles are always split by
  one straight edge, because those neurons read x₁ and x₂ directly. Second-layer tiles can have curved
  edges, because they read the already-bent outputs of the first layer.
- At the starting settings, the hidden layer moves both "no" corners to about (1, 1), so one straight
  rule can separate them from the "yes" corners. You can check every corner yourself by moving the
  probe and reading h1 and h2.
- With `none (linear)`, adding layers or neurons stops helping, and XOR stays at 50%, the score of guessing at
  random (often called chance), however big you make the network. The loss sits at 0.693, the score for answering 0.5 everywhere. The stacked
  layers collapse into one multiply-and-add with no flat part, and on XOR no tilt beats answering 0.5.
- On `Ring`, two neurons carve a wedge and reach 86.7%, while a third neuron closes the circle and gets
  every dot right from epoch 74 onward.
- Width also changes what training manages to find, because with two neurons on XOR some starts get
  stuck. With three or more neurons, every seed from 1 to 6 solves it.
- On the Checkerboard with `tanh`, the two narrow layers beat the one wide layer at every seed from 1 to
  6. At the starting seed, the two narrow layers finish at 87.5% while the wide one stops at 56.3%.
- With `ReLU`, though, the one wide layer does at least as well as the two narrow ones, so the winner
  depends on the activation.
- The badge at the top of **Depth against width** shows "two layers minus one layer" in percentage
  points, which means the plain difference between the two scores. With `tanh` at the starting seed
  and **Training epochs** at 300, it reads about 31 points in favor of the two layers.
- Adding layers in the main controls also adds dials, so it is not a fair test of depth. At the
  starting seed on the Checkerboard, three layers of six neurons beat one layer of six, but they also
  carry more than four times as many dials.
- With `Clean` labels, both networks score about the same on fresh dots as on practice dots, so the
  **Gap** readout sits near zero.
- With `20% flipped`, the large network scores 96.7% on its practice dots but only 79.2% on fresh ones.
  The narrow network scores 90.0% and 91.7%, so its two scores stay close.
- A network that followed only the true ring could score at most 80% on these flipped practice dots,
  since 12 of the 60 labels are wrong. The narrow network's 90% means it has fitted some mistakes
  too, but with only three straight cuts it cannot curve far, so its score on fresh dots holds up.
- Across the first six seeds, the large network's gap is always at least 15 points, while the narrow
  network's never passes 7.
- With **Curves** on `Loss` and labels flipped, the large network's training loss keeps falling, down
  to 0.07. Its held-out loss, though, bottoms out near 0.28 around epoch 71. After that it climbs all
  the way to 0.99, a rise the training loss alone would never show you.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- The main network is tiny, with two inputs, at most 64 practice dots, and at most 3 layers of 6
  neurons. Even the biggest network anywhere in the lab has only 109 dials.
  The **Decision surface** can only be drawn because there are two inputs, while a real model reads far
  more numbers at once than any flat picture can show.
- Training always uses the same size of nudge, called the learning rate, with the same momentum, for
  300 epochs. Different settings would change which random starts succeed and how quickly they get there.
- The **Decision surface** and **Network training controls** score only the practice dots, so a 100%
  there says nothing about how the network would do on new dots.
- **Held-out data** is the only card that uses fresh dots, and it works only on the Ring, always with
  `tanh` neurons. It also flips the same 12 labels every time, and flipping a different 12 would change
  how big the gap is.
- **Depth against width** has only one puzzle where the two shapes come apart. Depth wins there only
  with `tanh`, within 300 epochs, and a longer run might close the gap. With `ReLU` the result flips
  at some seeds, where the wide layer wins by up to 31 points, so read it as one case, not a rule.
- That card also does not prove that one wide layer is unable to draw a checkerboard, only that this
  training method, in this much time, did not find a way.
- Stacking layers after a single neuron adds nothing here, because **Neurons per layer** sets every
  layer to 1. Each later layer only reshapes the same single number again, so the cut stays one
  straight line.
- The dots are made up and fixed, and the fresh dots come from the same made-up ring as the practice
  dots, with every label correct. Real fresh data may have wrong labels too, and may not look like the
  data a model practiced on.

### Where real networks struggle

The assembly-line picture is too tidy, because workers on a real line each have one clear job. Real
hidden neurons are messier: one neuron often responds to several unrelated things at once, and a single
idea is usually spread across many neurons. That spreading is called a **distributed representation**,
and it is why a single hidden neuron rarely has a clean human label.

The XOR network's neat corner detectors are the exception that a tiny toy allows, and the
interpretability labs take up the harder real case.

More capacity does not mean more learning, because past a point a network starts fitting the noise in
its practice data, as the large network does on **Held-out data**. A held-out set also stops being held
out once you use it again and again to choose between models, because your choices slowly start to fit
it too. Picture checking dozens of study methods against the same practice quiz and keeping whichever
scores best. In the end you have picked the method that suits that one quiz, not one that works on any
quiz.

Deep stacks bring a training problem too, because backpropagation works out each dial's share of the
blame by passing it backward through every layer. In a deep stack, that blame signal can fade away or
blow up on its way back.

In the Backpropagation lab's Gradient flow card, twelve sigmoid layers with sensible starting dials
leave the first layer's nudges millions of times weaker than the last layer's. Very deep stacks were
hard to train until extra design pieces, called normalization and residual connections, became
standard.

**A common mix-up:** Bigger is not reliably better, and adding layers is no exception. On **Held-out
data**, the large network has both more layers and more dials. With flipped labels, it memorizes more
of the mistakes and does worse on fresh dots than the narrow one.

Adding layers also does not rescue a bad start. With two neurons per layer on XOR, seed 3 gets stuck
whether there is one hidden layer or two, even though a perfect answer exists. And very deep stacks
bring the fading-blame problem above, which makes them harder to train at all.

## Going deeper

A mathematical result called **universal approximation** says that one hidden layer, made wide enough
and given a bend like `tanh`, can copy any shape that has no sudden jumps. The copy can be as close as
you like, as long as you stay within a limited area of inputs.

The result is true but not very useful in practice, because it says nothing about how wide the layer
must be. It also says nothing about whether training will ever find the right dials, and the
Checkerboard with `tanh` may be a small example of that second gap.

The lab does not show that one wide `tanh` layer is unable to draw the board, only that this training
method did not find a way in 300 epochs. With `ReLU`, though, the same 21-neuron layer gets 86% to 100% of
the board across seeds 1 to 6. That hints that the hard part is finding the dials rather than having too few.

Depth is often the practical shortcut, because later layers reuse what earlier layers built. For many
shapes, a deep network needs far fewer neurons than a shallow one would, and the Checkerboard card is
one small case of this, with the limits above.

That reuse is why the layers of big models tend to form a progression from simple to complex, called a
**feature hierarchy**. In image models, early layers react to edges and later ones to parts of objects.
In language models, early layers handle surface form, meaning the exact words and how they look, later
ones the grammar, and later still the structure of the task.

Some layer designs go further, sharing or routing their dials in special ways:

- A convolution layer slides one small set of dials across every position of its input, reusing the
  same dials everywhere.
- A recurrent layer reuses one layer again and again, once at each step of a sequence.
- A mixture-of-experts layer sends each token, a small piece of text, to only a few of its many smaller
  layers.
