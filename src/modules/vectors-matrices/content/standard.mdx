## How do you do arithmetic on a word?

Is a cat more like a dog or a bicycle? You know at once, but a language model can only add and
multiply. The way out is to represent each thing as a **vector**, an ordered list of numbers,
chosen so that similar things get similar lists. Questions about meaning then become questions
about geometry, and geometry is arithmetic.

### Where it shows up

Everything a neural network handles is a vector: a token’s **embedding**, the activations of a
layer, the gradient of the loss. Everything a network does to a vector between its nonlinearities
is multiplication by a **matrix**. This lab builds both objects in two dimensions, where they can
be drawn.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end you can read a vector as an arrow and separate its length (how much) from its direction
(what kind). You can add and scale vectors and predict the result. You can compute a dot product by
hand and say why cosine similarity discards length. You can read a matrix by its columns, explain
why matrix products do not commute and what a rank-deficient matrix destroys, and count the
parameters of a low-rank factorization, which is the saving LoRA exploits.

### What the lab shows

Eight words, each given two hand-written coordinates, *size* and *alive*. Nothing is learned or
measured: every readout is exact arithmetic on those sixteen authored numbers, on 2×2 matrices you
set with sliders, and on one 8×8 table built from four authored rank-one pieces. Real embeddings
have hundreds to thousands of dimensions with no named axes; *Tokens & embeddings* shows those.

Keep one question in mind: if a matrix can destroy information irreversibly, why would a model use
such matrices on purpose?

## Build the map, one idea at a time

The lab has eight cards, one idea each, and shows one per step; **Next** under a card opens the
following one.

### A word as two numbers

The card opens on horse = (0.9, 1.2): 0.9 along the size axis, 1.2 along the alive axis. The list
and the arrow from the origin to that point are the same object. Its **length**, or Euclidean norm,
is ‖v‖ = √(0.9² + 1.2²) = √(0.81 + 1.44) = √2.25 = 1.5, which the card prints as 1.50.

**Pause and predict:** drag the tip straight outward to (1.8, 2.4). Which readout changes, **How
much: length** or **What kind: direction**?

Only the length changes, to 3.00; the direction still reads “big, living”. Scaling a vector by a
positive number changes its norm and leaves its direction alone. Mouse, (−1.6, 1.0), is longer than
horse at 1.89: length here measures distance from the middle of the map, not importance.

### Add and stretch

Vectors support two operations, and linear algebra is the study of what follows from them.
Addition is componentwise, drawn tip to tail: horse + (1, 0) = (1.9, 1.2), which the card reports
as 0.32 from whale. Scalar multiplication multiplies every component, so it stretches, shrinks, or
(for a negative scalar) reverses an arrow along its own line.

**Pause and predict:** which multiple of the step “bigger”, (1, 0), takes horse to cat,
(−0.8, 1.2)?

Set **Times** to × −1.7: (0.9, 1.2) − 1.7·(1, 0) = (−0.8, 1.2), exactly cat. Learned embeddings show
the same structure. Mikolov, Yih and Zweig reported that the offset between word vectors encodes
relations, with “King − Man + Woman” landing very close to “Queen”, and Ilharco and colleagues add
and negate whole fine-tuning updates as “task vectors”.

### How alike are two arrows?

The **dot product** multiplies matching components and adds: horse · whale = 0.9×2.0 + 1.2×0.9 =
1.80 + 1.08 = 2.88. Each product is positive when the two vectors agree in sign on that axis. With
bus, (1.6, −1.2), the terms are +1.44 and −1.44, so the dot product is 0 and the arrows are
orthogonal (90°).

**Pause and predict:** turn the second arrow by 180°. What happens to the dot product?

It becomes −2.88: negating a vector negates every term. Geometrically, a · b = ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ, which
is also the signed length of the **projection** of a onto b (the shadow, 1.31 here) times ‖b‖
(2.19).

The dot product therefore confounds agreement with magnitude. Move **Stretch the second arrow**
from × 1.0 to × 1.5 and it rises from 2.88 to 4.32 with no change in direction. **Cosine
similarity** divides both norms out: 2.88 / (1.50 × 2.19) = 0.88, invariant to either stretch and
bounded in [−1, 1].

### A matrix moves every arrow at once

A 2×2 **matrix** maps a vector to a vector; each output component is the dot product of one row
with the input. The card opens on the shear with rows (1.0, 0.5) and (0.0, 1.0), which sends horse
to (1.0×0.9 + 0.5×1.2, 0.0×0.9 + 1.0×1.2) = (1.5, 1.2).

The column view is the one to keep. The matrix sends the basis vector (1, 0) to its first column
and (0, 1) to its second. Since horse = 0.9·(1, 0) + 1.2·(0, 1), its image is 0.9 × first column +
1.2 × second column, drawn tip to tail on the card. That works because the map is **linear**: it
preserves addition and scaling, so grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the
origin stays fixed.

**Pause and predict:** raise **Top right** from 0.5 to 1.0. Which words move right?

Only the five animals. That entry multiplies each word’s alive coordinate into its size, so words
with negative alive move left: horse goes to (2.1, 1.2) and bicycle to (−1.8, −1.0). The presets
give the standard maps by their columns. Rotate is (0, 1), (−1, 0), a quarter turn sending horse to
(−1.2, 0.9); stretch is (1.5, 0), (0, 1); flip is (−1, 0), (0, 1); squash is (1, 0), (0, 0.4).

The card also reports the area of the image of the unit square, |det M|, where the **determinant**
of a 2×2 matrix is ad − bc: 1.00 for the shear and 1.50 for the stretch.

### One after another

Applying A and then B is a single linear map, the **matrix product** BA; its columns are the
images of the basis vectors under both steps. Order matters. Rotate then stretch sends horse to
(−1.2, 0.9) and then (−1.8, 0.9), and the product has rows (0.0, −1.5) and (1.0, 0.0). Stretch then
rotate sends horse to (1.35, 1.2) and then (−1.2, 1.35), with rows (0.0, −1.0) and (1.5, 0.0).
**Swap the order** shows 8 of 8 words landing elsewhere.

Non-commutativity is the general case, not a rule without exceptions: stretch and flip are both
diagonal, and swapping them moves 0 of 8 words. A deep network is a composition of such maps with
nonlinearities between them, so the order of its layers is part of what it computes.

### When a matrix forgets

The matrix diag(1, s) multiplies alive by s. At s = 0.50 the area is 0.50 and all eight words stay
distinct. At s = 0 the determinant is 0, the image is the size axis, and cat, (−0.8, 1.2), and
bicycle, (−0.8, −1.0), both map to (−0.8, 0.0): 7 of 8 distinct points remain.

**Pause and predict:** which matrix separates cat and bicycle again?

None. A function gives one output per input, so no later map can split two equal vectors. At
s = 0.20 the inverse exists (multiply alive by 5) and **stretch it back** restores every word. At
s = 0 it would require 1/0. The **rank** of a matrix is the dimension of its image; it has fallen
from 2 to 1, and what lay along the null space (here the alive axis) is unrecoverable.

That answers the opening question. Discarding a direction is how a model becomes invariant to it:
drop size and keep alive, and animals separate from machines regardless of how large they are.
Projection to fewer dimensions is routine in real models, and its cost is exactly this loss.

### A table from a few pieces

A rank-one matrix is an outer product, one column times one row. The 8×8 table on this card is the
sum of four such pieces with strengths 8, 4, 2 and 1, so it has rank 4. Storing one piece takes
8 + 8 = 16 numbers, not 64.

**Pause and predict:** how many numbers store 2 pieces?

Thirty-two. **Pieces kept** trades storage against error: 16, 32, 48 and 64 numbers stored for an
average miss per cell of 4, 2, 1 and 0. At four pieces the factorization costs as much as the
table, so low rank pays only when r is small relative to the dimensions.

The pieces were authored with mutually orthogonal column patterns and row patterns, so the sum is
a singular value decomposition in disguise. By the Eckart–Young theorem, keeping the r strongest
pieces is then the best rank-r approximation, not merely a convenient one.

At scale the saving is large. A 4,096 × 4,096 weight matrix has 16,777,216 entries, while a rank-8
update costs 8 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 65,536, about 0.4% of that. **LoRA** freezes the pretrained
matrix and trains only such a low-rank update, on the hypothesis that the change fine-tuning needs
has low intrinsic rank.

## Your turn: aim the map

On **Challenge: aim the map**, dashed marks show where each word should land, and you set the four
entries until **Average distance from the targets** reaches zero. Round 1 is a diagonal scaling,
round 2 a rotation, round 3 a shear composed with a flip.

You can solve each round by reading the columns: the hint draws the two target basis images, which
are (0, −1) and (1, 0) in round 2, and (−1, 0) and (0.5, 1) in round 3. Or you can adjust one entry
at a time and keep any change that lowers the distance.

The second strategy is a coordinate-by-coordinate search that lowers the average distance, and it
is what training a linear layer amounts to: find the matrix that carries inputs onto targets. Real training differs in
scale and in using the gradient to choose every adjustment at once, which *Slopes & gradients*
takes up next.

## What this picture leaves out

### What the lab simplifies

- The sixteen word coordinates are authored and tuned (‖horse‖ = 1.5 exactly, horse ⟂ bus exactly),
  and the axes have names. Learned embeddings have no privileged, labelled basis.
- Every map is 2×2 with no bias term. A real layer is affine, Wx + b, followed by a nonlinearity.
- The 8×8 table is exactly rank 4 by construction, with orthogonal pieces, so truncation errors are
  whole numbers. Real weight matrices have a full spectrum of singular values.
- The challenge targets are exactly reachable; real objectives are not driven to zero.

### Where the real ideas run into trouble

- **Cosine similarity can mislead.** Steck, Ekanadham and Kallus show that for embeddings learned
  under some regularization schemes the cosine can be arbitrary, because training leaves a free
  rescaling of the dimensions.
- **Analogy arithmetic is a tendency.** GloVe reports 75% on one word-analogy benchmark, far from
  every case, and results depend on the benchmark.
- **Features are not axes.** Meaning usually lies along directions that mix many coordinates, and
  networks can store more sparse features than they have dimensions, in superposition.
- **Linear maps cannot bend.** A stack of matrices collapses to one matrix, as two steps did here,
  which is why networks interleave nonlinearities.
- **Low rank is a hypothesis.** A rank-r update is exact only if the needed change has rank at most
  r; otherwise the remainder is lost, as it was when you kept 2 of 4 pieces.
