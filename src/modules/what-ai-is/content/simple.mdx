## The big picture

People use the word “AI” for many different things. They use it for a chatbot that writes a poem. They also use it for a filter that sorts email into spam or not spam. Even a chess program that follows rules a person typed in gets the name.

That chess program works in a completely different way from the other two. So one name for all three hides more than it tells you. The name says nothing about how each one actually works.

This module sorts out those words. It starts with one idea: AI, short for artificial intelligence, is a large family of methods. Smaller groups sit inside it, one inside the next. The groups fit together like your street, your town and your country. Your street sits inside your town, and your town sits inside your country.

One of those groups, machine learning, fits its rules to examples instead of having people write them. Inside that group sits deep learning. It learns with many stacked layers.

The module also shows you what “learning from examples” really means. You’ll fit a straight line to three dots on a graph, one step at a time.

### Where you have met it

Have you ever typed a question into a chat assistant? Then you have used the smallest, most specific group in this module. That group is called the large language model: a program trained to guess the next piece of text. The assistant also belongs to every larger group around it. That goes all the way out to AI itself, the largest group of all.

Some of the lab’s other examples are close to everyday tools. One sorts emails into spam or not spam. Another predicts a house’s sale price from past sales. Others are less familiar, such as repairing a photo that has a patch blanked out. All of these are jobs a model can learn from examples. Here, a model means the rule a program uses to turn an input into an answer.

A list of chess rules typed in by a person counts as AI too. Yet that list never learned anything at all.

### Why it matters

When people mix up these words, claims about AI turn into mush. You can no longer ask the questions that matter. Which numbers inside the model changed during learning? Which examples did they change to match? Where did the right answers come from? Asking these questions is how you check a claim about a model. Without them, you have to take the claim on trust.

There is a second reason. Smooth, confident writing can make a program seem to have a mind of its own. This module shows you what is actually inside “learning.” Learning means adjusting a handful of numbers. The goal is to shrink a score for how wrong the answers are. Once you see that, you can tell what the math really does. You can also spot the things it never claims, such as having a mind.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- Why AI, machine learning, deep learning and large language models are not four names for one thing. You’ll also see how each one fits inside the one before it.
- What “learning from examples” means. You’ll break it into four parts and try each one. First comes a menu of allowed rules. Next is a score for how wrong a rule is. Then comes a search for better numbers. Last is a gap that practice scores can’t show.
- How three families of methods differ. One family uses rules people wrote. One fits numbers to examples. One fits deep stacks of layers. You’ll also say what none of them claims, such as having a mind.
- Where a model’s right answers come from during training. They can be answers people wrote down. They can be patterns found in data that has no answers. They can be answers hidden inside the data itself. Or they can be a reward for how things turned out.
- How to tell two kinds of models apart. One kind answers with a category, such as spam or not spam. The other answers with a number, such as a price.

## What it is

### Five rings, one inside the next

Think about how a mailing address works. Your home is on a street. The street is in a town, and the town is in a country. Your home is in all of those places at once. Still, the smallest part of the address is the most useful. It says the most about where you are.

The **Nested methods** card draws AI the same way. It shows five rings, one inside the next. Here is what each ring adds, from the outside in:

- **AI**, short for **artificial intelligence**, is the outer ring. It names a goal, not one particular method. The goal is to get a machine to do a task that used to need human judgment. So even a list of written rules can count.
- **ML** is short for **machine learning**. It is the part of AI where nobody writes the rule by hand. Instead, the method keeps adjusting some numbers to fit a set of examples. Often it tries to match the answers the examples came with. It can also group similar examples together.
- **DL** is short for **deep learning**. It is the part of machine learning that stacks many layers of adjustable steps. Later layers can build on what earlier layers picked out.
- **Gen** stands for **generative** models. Training teaches these models to make new samples, meaning new text, pictures or sounds. The new samples look like the examples the model learned from. In this lab, the Gen ring holds only generative models that are also deep learning models.
- **LLM** is short for **large language model**. It is the innermost ring. An LLM is a generative model. Its training job is to predict the next **token**. A token is a small piece of text, such as a whole word or part of a word.

A layer in the deep learning ring is one step. It turns a list of numbers into a new list of numbers. It uses adjustable numbers of its own to do this.

Why does stacking layers help? Think about a photo. A photo reaches a computer as a long list of numbers. Each number gives the brightness of one tiny colored dot in the photo, called a **pixel**.

Picture a model that sorts photos. Its first layer might turn those numbers into a list that marks the edges. The next layer might combine edges into shapes, such as circles and corners. A later layer might combine shapes into something like an eye or a wheel. Nobody tells the layers to look for edges. Instead, training tunes their numbers to fit the examples. Looking for edges turns out to help.

The lab also calls these stacked-layer models neural networks, or neural nets for short. That’s why it says the Gen ring holds neural generative models.

On its own, an LLM is not yet the chat assistant you talk to. People train it further afterward, so it answers in a helpful style. That’s why the lab’s note for the LLM ring says assistant behavior comes later.

### One product, several rings

The rings nest, so one product can sit in several of them at once. Your home is on your street and in your country at the same time. A next-token chat assistant works the same way. It is an LLM, so it is also generative, deep learning, machine learning and AI.

The smallest ring that still holds a method is its **tightest ring**. That ring tells you the most about how the method works. Here are the five systems the lab asks you to sort, with the tightest ring for each one:

- **Hand-written chess rules** belong in AI and nowhere smaller. A person wrote every rule. Nobody fitted anything from examples.
- **Linear regression on house prices** belongs in ML. “Linear” means straight-line, and “regression” here means predicting a number. This method fits a straight-line rule to past sales, much like the line you’ll fit below. It has no stack of layers, though, so it is not deep.
- The **Convolutional image classifier** belongs in DL. A classifier sorts things into categories. This one sorts photos into labels such as cat or dog. “Convolutional” means its stacked layers slide small learned filters across the photo. Each filter looks for a pattern, such as an edge or a stripe. This model labels pictures. It doesn’t make new ones, so it is not generative.
- **A GAN that samples faces** belongs in Gen. GAN is short for generative adversarial network. It is a deep learning model that learns to draw new pictures. This one draws faces. It is not an LLM, though. It never predicts the next token of text.
- **A next-token chat assistant** belongs in LLM. Predicting the next token is its training job. The assistant style comes from further training added afterward.

### Where the rings stop matching

The address picture works for one reason. Every street lies inside exactly one town. Every town lies inside exactly one country. Now think about a label such as “by the sea.” It fits some towns in one country and some towns in another. You can’t draw “by the sea” as one ring inside a single country. The label cuts straight across the borders.

The word “generative” cuts across the rings in the same way. An older method called an **n-gram** shows why. An n-gram counts which words tend to follow which. Then it picks each next word from those counts. It gets those counts from example text. Nobody writes them as rules. So the n-gram belongs in machine learning. It makes new text, so it is generative. But it has no stack of layers, so it is not deep learning.

The Gen ring sits entirely inside DL. Putting the n-gram there would wrongly claim that it is deep. So the lab marks the n-gram with a small dot instead. The dot sits inside the ML ring and outside the DL ring. A note under the rings explains why. The rings are a common way to describe today’s methods. They are not a law of nature.

### Learning from examples, with one straight line

In science class, you may have drawn a line of best fit. That’s the straight line that passes as close as it can to some plotted dots. Machine learning does the same job, but it doesn’t judge by eye. It scores how wrong the line is, then searches for a better score.

The **Learning problem** card gives you three dots. They sit at (0.5, 1.4), (1.5, 2.1) and (2.5, 3.2). Each dot is one example. It has an input, x, and its right answer, y. You fit the dots with a straight line written as ŷ = wx + b. Here ŷ, read “y-hat,” is the line’s guess for y. The line does well when ŷ comes out close to y.

In math class, you may have seen this written as y = mx + b. There, m is the slope. The slope tells you how steep the line is. Here, the slope is w, the **weight**. The height of the line where x is 0 is b, the **bias**. This “bias” only means a starting height. It has nothing to do with the unfair kind of bias you’ll meet later.

You set these two numbers with the sliders labeled **Weight w** and **Bias b**. The line on the left-hand plot moves as you drag them.

### The menu of allowed rules

Every line those two sliders can draw is one possible rule. The full set of them has a name: the **hypothesis class**. A hypothesis here is one guess about the rule. So the hypothesis class is the menu of every rule the method may pick.

On this card, the menu is every straight line. No setting of the sliders can ever bend the line into a curve. A different menu might hold curves, or a tree of yes-or-no questions. That would be a different search, with different possible answers.

The menu picture stops matching in one way. A café menu lists a few dishes. This menu holds every straight line, far too many to write down.

### Scoring a line

To score a line, first measure how far it misses each dot. The miss is ŷ − y, the line’s guess minus the right answer. The lab calls this miss a **residual**. It draws each one as a short up-and-down stroke from the dot to the line.

Next, square each miss. Squaring does two jobs. It makes every miss positive. It also makes a big miss count far more than a small one. A miss of 2 becomes 4, but a miss of 1 stays 1. So the bigger miss now counts four times as much, not twice as much.

The average of the three squared misses is the **loss**, written L. The loss is a score for how wrong the line is. This kind of loss is called the **mean squared error**, or MSE for short. “Mean” is another word for average.

The card draws each squared miss as a shaded square beside its dot. So L is the average area of those three squares. A smaller L means the line fits these three dots better. A perfect fit would make L zero.

Under the plots, a table has one row for each dot. Each row lists the dot’s x and its right answer y. It also lists the line’s guess ŷ, the miss ŷ − y, and the squared miss. The worked example below matches those rows.

Here is the score for the line the card starts with. On that line, w is 1.00 and b is 0.90.

1. Take the first dot, at x = 0.5. The line’s guess is 1 × 0.5 + 0.9. That works out to 1.4. The right answer is also 1.4. So the miss is 0, and so is its square.
2. Take the second dot, at x = 1.5. The line guesses 2.4, but the right answer is only 2.1. That’s a miss of 0.3. Squaring it gives 0.09. That is the area of the second shaded square.
3. Take the third dot, at x = 2.5. The line guesses 3.4, but the right answer is 3.2. That’s a miss of 0.2. Squaring it gives 0.04, a smaller square than the second one.
4. Add up the three squares: 0 + 0.09 + 0.04 makes 0.13. Divide by 3 to get about 0.043. That is the L the card shows when it opens.

### Model, training and inference

The two numbers w and b are the line’s **parameters**. Parameters are the numbers inside a rule that training sets from data. This line has only two parameters. A large language model has billions of them. The idea is the same.

A **model** is the rule with its parameters fixed. Every input x then gets exactly one answer ŷ. **Training** is the search that picks the parameters. It works by shrinking the loss on examples. **Inference** means using the finished rule on a new input. During inference, the numbers stay fixed.

A recipe can help here. Think of a cookie recipe. The amounts of sugar and flour are like the parameters. Training is like baking test batches and changing the amounts until the cookies come out right. The finished recipe, with its amounts written down, is like the model. Inference is like baking from that recipe for a party. Baking from it doesn’t change the amounts.

The recipe picture stops matching in one way. A baker changes the amounts by taste. Training changes the numbers by a formula that shrinks the loss.

Here is inference with real numbers. Suppose training settled on the best line. You’ll meet that line below. It has w = 0.90 and b of about 0.88. Now ask that line about a new input of 2. It works out 0.90 × 2 + 0.88, and answers about 2.68. Asking doesn’t change w or b at all.

In math, a rule that turns each input into exactly one output is called a **function**. So a model is a function with its numbers set. A vending machine works a bit like a function. Each button you press gives you exactly one snack. The comparison has a limit, though. A vending machine has a few buttons. The line accepts any number x as its input.

Now picture searching for numbers that bring a function’s answers close to the right answers. People call that search **function approximation**. “Approximation” means getting close. Most of modern AI is this kind of search. None of it is a model of a mind.

A setting you choose before the search starts is called a **hyperparameter**. Training never changes it. In the recipe, it’s like deciding to change the sugar half a spoon at a time. On this card, the step size of 0.12 is a hyperparameter. It sets how far each downhill step moves. You’ll see how shortly.

### The map seen from above

The right-hand plot has the label “Parameter space · every point is a line.” It shows the same search from above. Each spot on this map is one pair of numbers. The w number runs across, and the b number runs up. So each spot stands for one whole line on the left-hand plot.

A filled dot on this map shows where your current line sits. It works like a “you are here” pin on a mall map. The dot moves when you drag a slider or press the downhill button. On a mall map, the pin marks a place. Here, the dot marks a whole line. This dot is a different thing from the three data dots on the left-hand plot.

Now imagine working out L for every spot on the map. Treat each L as a height, like the height of the ground. You would get a valley shaped like a bowl. It is high at the edges, where the lines miss badly. It is low in the middle, where they fit well.

The map draws that valley the way a hiking map shows hills, using **contour** lines. Each oval line joins all the spots that share the same L. The number printed on the line tells you that L. On a hiking map, height means real ground. Here, height means how wrong a line is.

The green × marks the bottom of the bowl. That spot is the best line these three dots allow. It sits at w = 0.90 and b ≈ 0.88. The sign ≈ means “about.”

Under the plots, the card shows several numbers, called readouts. The **Minimum L** readout says this best line still has a loss of about 0.0089. That is small, but not zero. Even the best line misses a little. The three dots don’t lie on one straight line.

### Walking downhill

Picture standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can’t see the bottom of the valley. But you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill. Then you feel the slope again and repeat. The **Take a downhill step** button does exactly that on the map.

The two readouts **∂L/∂w** and **∂L/∂b** are the slopes under your feet. There is one for each parameter. You can read ∂L/∂w as “how fast L changes when you nudge w up a tiny bit.” Say ∂L/∂w is negative. Then raising w lowers L, so the step raises w. If ∂L/∂w is positive, the step lowers w instead. Together, the two slopes are called the **gradient**. The lab writes the gradient as ∇L.

The card’s Optimization stage is about this search for better numbers. It prints the rule for one step: θ ← θ − η ∇L. Here θ, the Greek letter theta, stands for both parameters at once. The letter η, called eta, stands for the step size of 0.12. The arrow means “becomes.” So each parameter becomes its old value minus 0.12 times its own slope.

That rule moves each number downhill, as long as the step size is small enough. A step size of 0.12 is small enough for this bowl. A much bigger step could jump right past the bottom. It would land higher up on the other side, like a giant stride across the valley floor.

Here is one step worked out. It starts from the flat line where w is 0 and b is 0.

1. That line lies along the bottom of the graph. It misses all three dots badly, so L reads about 5.54.
2. The slope ∂L/∂w reads −7.900. It is negative and large. So raising w would lower L quickly.
3. The step changes w to 0 − 0.12 × (−7.9). Subtracting a negative number adds. So w comes to about 0.95.
4. The ∂L/∂b readout shows about −4.47. The same rule gives b = 0 − 0.12 × (−4.47). That comes to about 0.54.
5. With this new line, L drops to about 0.086. One press has taken the you-are-here dot most of the way down the bowl.

A formula sets each step, with no guessing involved. This nudge of two numbers is the whole of what “learning” means on this card.

The fog picture stops matching in one way. This card can draw the whole map for you. That works only with two numbers, one for across and one for up. A model with billions of numbers would need a map with billions of directions. Nobody can draw or fill in a map like that. So real training feels its way by the slopes alone, like the hiker in the fog.

### Practice scores and new examples

Picture a friend who memorizes the answer key to a practice quiz. They learn all ten answers in order. On that exact quiz, they score perfectly. Then the teacher swaps in new questions. Now the memorized answers are no help at all.

A model can fall into the same trap. So the score that really matters is how it does on examples it has never seen. Doing well on new examples from the same source as the training examples is called **generalization**. The **generalization gap** is the difference between two scores. One is how the model does on its training examples. The other is how it does on new ones.

The quiz picture stops matching in one way. Your friend decided to memorize. A model makes no plan like that. Training pushes its numbers toward a smaller loss on the examples it has. Nothing in that search checks new examples.

The Learning problem card names this gap in its Generalization stage. But the card never tests a fourth dot, so it never measures the gap. A small L tells you the line fits these three dots. It doesn’t tell you how far off the line would be at a new x.

A wigglier menu of rules could even pass exactly through all three dots. It could still be badly wrong at a fourth one. A later lab, How we know a model works, measures the gap. It scores examples the fit never saw.

### Three families of methods

Picture two friends who both want to sort apples into fresh ones and bruised ones. Each one does it a different way. The first friend writes a list of rules. One rule might be: “A soft brown spot bigger than a coin means bruised.” The second friend studies hundreds of apples someone has already sorted. They measure a few things about each apple. Then they keep adjusting some numbers until their sorting matches.

Those two friends are using two of the three families of methods. **What the word hides** names all three when you set **Distinction** to `family`:

- **Symbolic** methods use rules, search or logic that a person wrote. A person writes the decision down. Nobody fits it from examples. There are no parameters to learn. So these methods are AI, but not machine learning. The first apple friend works this way.
- In **Statistical ML**, “statistical” means working out numbers from many examples. These methods start with **features**, the measurable things a person chooses to look at. An apple’s color and a house’s floor area are features. Then the method fits its numbers to match examples that already carry the right answer. Linear regression belongs to this family, and so does the second apple friend.
- **Deep learning** methods fit a whole stack of layers. So they learn which features to look at. They also learn how to combine them, both at once. They can start from raw inputs, such as the pixels of a photo. The photo-sorting layers from earlier worked this way.

You could add a third friend for that last family. This friend gives a deep model photos of apples that someone already sorted. Training tunes its layers until they pick out patterns that help the sorting.

The apple picture stops matching in one place. Real friends know what an apple is. The second and third friends’ methods have only numbers and sorted examples.

All three families count as AI. Only the last two fit numbers from examples. So only they are machine learning. Rule-based methods didn’t disappear when fitting took over, either. A spelling checker or a route planner built from written rules is still AI. It has no learned numbers at all.

### What the word does not claim

Set **Distinction** to `not`, and three tiles appear. Their names are Not consciousness, Not folk understanding and Not a verdict on use.

Consciousness means having feelings and awareness. Nothing in the line, the loss or the downhill steps stands for those. So fitting numbers is not the same as being conscious.

Folk understanding means the everyday kind of understanding a person has. Matching the answers on some examples doesn’t show that kind of grasp.

A verdict is a final judgment. A well-fitted model can still be the wrong tool. It might have trained on the wrong answers. It might also be unsafe to use.

The other settings come at the same point from different sides. An example helps with each one.

Under `scope`, a chat assistant looks general. You can ask it about almost anything in words. A spam filter, by contrast, only ever answers one question about emails. Still, the assistant is a next-token predictor. Further training on top gives it an assistant style.

Under `job`, the card keeps predicting apart from reasoning. Predicting means giving one quick answer, such as guessing the next word. Reasoning means working through steps. Think of a chess program that tries several moves ahead and checks where each one leads. A predictor can be one part of that search. For instance, it might score each board position the search reaches. But the predictor alone is not the search.

The same setting gives a warning. A smooth answer can read like a careful argument. Yet it may prove nothing at all. Sounding sure is not the same as checking the work. A confident classmate’s answer still needs checking, after all. A fluent paragraph, one that reads smoothly, may only be blending patterns. Those patterns come from the text the model learned from.

Under `memory`, the card keeps fitting the training examples apart from working on new ones. That’s the generalization gap from earlier. The answer-key friend fits the practice quiz perfectly. That says nothing about the next quiz.

AGI is short for artificial general intelligence. It names a system that could do any thinking task a person can. People define it in different ways. There is no agreed test for it. So it is a goal, not something anyone has measured. Nothing in these labs claims it. Fluent text is not evidence of it either.

### Before the neural thread

The **Era timeline** starts before neural nets. Its story begins with a proposal from 1955 by four researchers: McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon. They asked for a two-month summer study of artificial intelligence at Dartmouth College. That workshop is the timeline’s first stop, in 1956. The four bet that a machine could copy any feature of intelligence. First, though, people had to describe it precisely enough.

The proposal listed seven open problems. It hoped for a big step forward on one or more of them in a single summer. It named problems, not a method for solving them. That’s why the timeline counts it as a stall.

Then came two **AI winters**: long stretches when money and interest for AI research dried up.

Sir James Lighthill was a British scientist. In 1973, he wrote a review of AI for a UK research council. He reported that discoveries had not made the promised big impact in any part of the field. Funding fell after his report. The first AI winter lasted from roughly 1974 to 1980.

The timeline’s Why it stalled note for that 1974 stop gives a deeper reason. Some methods worked on small, tidy problems, such as puzzles with a few fixed pieces. But they didn’t grow to handle realistic problems. A realistic problem needed a huge amount of detailed knowledge. Nobody could type it all in by hand.

Next came **expert systems**. They stored a specialist’s knowledge as written rules. Each rule had a shape like “if this is true, then do that.” One of them, XCON, helped the computer maker Digital Equipment Corporation fill orders. It first went into use in 1980. People wrote its knowledge as rules. They fitted nothing from examples. So it was AI without being machine learning.

Picture a help desk that answers every question from a binder of written rules. Each new product means new rules. Each new rule can clash with an old one. So keeping the binder correct takes more and more work as it grows. Expert systems ran into that kind of problem. They cost too much to keep up to date. They also broke on cases their authors had not expected.

The help desk picture has one gap. A real help desk worker can use common sense on a strange question. An expert system had only its rules.

Lisp machines were special computers for running AI programs. Those programs used a programming language called Lisp. By 1987, cheaper ordinary computers could do the same job. So the market for Lisp machines collapsed.

Two things began a second AI winter. One was that collapse. The other was the cost of keeping rule binders up to date. Both winter dates are rough. Read them as landmarks, not exact start and end points.

### The neural thread

The other seven stops on the timeline follow **neural nets**. That’s the family of models that grew into the stacked layers of the DL ring. These stops mix in by year with the rule-based ones. Each one has its own stall.

- **1958, Perceptron.** The perceptron was a single layer of numbers. It learned them from labeled examples, meaning examples that came with right answers. It worked much like your line. It said yes or no by drawing one straight boundary between two kinds of example. Its stall was **XOR**, a rule that says yes when exactly one of two switches is on. Draw the four on/off combinations of the two switches as the corners of a square. Off-off and on-on are the no cases. Off-on and on-off are the yes cases. The two yes cases sit in opposite corners. So no single straight line can split them from the two no cases.
- **1969, Perceptrons critique.** A critique is a close look at what’s wrong with something. Minsky and Papert wrote a book called Perceptrons. It proved some things a single layer cannot work out, with XOR among them. Money and attention then moved away from neural nets. For over a decade, they went to rule lists and search instead.
- **1986, Backpropagation.** Three researchers showed how to train a stack of layers. Their method, backpropagation, sends the slopes backward from the loss through each layer. A stack can handle XOR. But there wasn’t yet enough computing power or labeled data for deep nets to win.
- **1997, LSTM memory.** LSTM is short for long short-term memory. It carried information along a text from one word to the next. It read one piece at a time, though. So training couldn’t work on a whole text at once. Very distant words still faded, too.
- **2012, AlexNet.** A deep network called AlexNet won a big photo-sorting contest by a wide margin. It trained on about 1.2 million labeled photos. It used two graphics chips, the kind of chip built to draw video-game scenes fast. Its stall: each new task still needed its own big labeled set and its own design.
- **2017, Transformer.** A transformer is a newer kind of model that uses **attention**. Attention lets every word in a text look at every other word at the same time. Its cost grows with the square of the text’s length, though. So doubling the length makes that cost about four times as large. That’s 2 × 2.
- **2022, Assistants.** Extra training turned next-token models into tools people can talk to. Part of that training used feedback from people. Fluent text is still not a check on whether something is true.

### Where the right answers come from

When a model trains, something has to tell it how it is doing. Whatever supplies the right answers or the score is called the **learning signal**. Sometimes there are no answers at all. Then the score can measure how well the model describes the patterns in the data.

Four signals cover most of this course. The **What is the learning signal** card shows them as four tiles, one for each signal.

### Supervised: answers from people

Picture a workbook where a teacher has already written the right answer beside every question. In **supervised learning**, each example comes with its answer. That answer is called a **label**. A person wrote it down, or a record already held it. Training scores the model against those labels. The model gets better by matching them more closely.

The workbook picture stops matching in one way. A student can read an answer and ask why it’s right. The model only gets a score for how far off its answer was.

In the House sale prices scenario, every sale record already lists the price the house sold for. So that scenario is supervised. Spam emails are supervised too. People marked each email as spam or not spam. The three dots on the Learning problem card are also supervised. Each y is the label for its x.

The labels decide what counts as correct. So a biased set of labels makes a biased rule. This is the unfair kind of bias mentioned earlier. Say the people marking emails kept marking one harmless newsletter as spam. The model would learn to do the same.

**Semi-supervised learning** is the same idea when only some of the examples carry labels. The rest have none.

### Unsupervised: patterns with no answers

Picture tipping a jar of mixed buttons onto a table. You group them by size and color. No list tells you which groups are right.

Now picture the data as a spreadsheet with one row for each example. Supervised data has a column holding the answers. Unsupervised data has no such column.

In **unsupervised learning**, the model looks for patterns, called structure, in the inputs themselves. It might find which records sit close together. It might find which readings are far from the usual pattern. The Customer records scenario groups customers by similar behavior. The Sensor readings scenario flags readings that look unusual.

The k-means method in the Classical machine learning lab works this way. It groups records without ever reading a label. There is no answer to check against. So a person still has to decide whether the groups mean anything useful.

The button picture stops matching here. You can look at your button groups and see if they make sense. The model can’t judge its own groups that way. That job falls to a person.

### Self-supervised: answers hidden in the data

Picture playing fill-in-the-blank with a book. You cover the next word and guess it. Then you uncover it to check. The book is its own answer key. Nobody has to write any answers for you.

In **self-supervised learning**, training hides part of the data. The model has to restore the hidden part. So that hidden part becomes the label.

In the Raw sentences scenario, the next word of each sentence is the label. It is already sitting in the text. In the Blanked-out photos scenario, the label is the hidden patch of pixels. Those pixels were part of the original photo all along.

The model still trains against labels, but nobody has to write them. So the practice set can be as big as the raw pile of data. That’s how language models can train on so much text. No team of people could ever label that much by hand.

The book game stops matching in one way. You might guess a word from understanding the story. A good guess from the model doesn’t show that kind of understanding.

### Reinforcement: a reward after acting

Picture a friend learning a new video game with no tutorial. The only feedback is a “You win” or “Game over” screen at the very end. They have to work out for themselves which earlier moves actually earned the win.

In **reinforcement learning**, no example ever shows the right move. The learner acts first. Afterward, it gets a reward, or a penalty that takes points away. Then it has to work out which earlier choices earned that reward. Your friend with the game faced the same puzzle. In the Charger robot scenario, the robot gets +1 for reaching its charger. It gets −1 for bumping into a wall.

The game picture fits well, with one difference. Your friend gets a single result at the end. Some learners get rewards and penalties for separate events. The Charger robot is one of them.

A reward scores how things turned out. It doesn’t label each step. It often arrives late, and only now and then. So this signal is harder to train with than labeled pairs. In labeled pairs, every input comes paired with its own answer.

The Reinforcement learning foundations lab runs this signal later. The Post-training lab shows how rewards tune assistants. Those rewards come from which answers people preferred.

### Two kinds of answer

What kind of answer a model gives is a separate question. It doesn’t depend on where the right answers came from during training.

**Classification** means the model picks one category from a fixed set, such as spam or not spam. **Regression** means the model gives a number on a scale, such as a house price. So the line you fit on the Learning problem card is regression too.

Think of two kinds of test questions. A multiple-choice question asks you to pick one option from a fixed list. That’s like classification. A question such as “How many meters tall is the tree?” asks for a number. That’s like regression.

The answer to one question doesn’t decide the answer to the other. The two self-supervised scenarios show this. Predicting the next token is classification. The model picks one token from its **vocabulary**, the fixed list of every token it knows. The card describes this with whole words. That keeps the example clear without changing the idea.

Repairing a blanked photo is regression. The answer is a number for each hidden pixel. So the two scenarios share a signal but not a kind of answer.

The test picture stops matching in one way. A multiple-choice question might offer four options. A language model picks from its whole vocabulary, a much longer list.

The card’s **Output type** readout names the kind of answer for each scenario. It has two more options besides these two. A target is the right answer a model aims for. The readout says “no target” for the unsupervised scenarios. Those predict no answer. For the reinforcement scenarios, the readout says “an action.” Their output is a move.

## Why it’s here

This is where the guide starts. Every later lab uses these words to mean exact, specific things. They are not loose labels on a box.

Suppose someone tells you, “The AI learned to spot bad reviews.” That sounds clear until you try to check it. To test the claim, you need to know which menu of rules the search used. You need to know which loss it shrank, and on which examples. You also need to know which learning signal it used. Now suppose “AI,” “machine learning” and “chatbot” all mean the same thing to you. Then there’s nothing definite left to check.

The line fit sits beside the rings for a reason. It turns “learns from examples” into something you can move with your own hands. On that card, two sliders, a score and a button make up the whole learning process. Nothing important hides behind the word.

The timeline sits beside both for another reason. It makes history read as a series of stalls, not a parade of winners. Each era hit a limit that a later idea had to work around. The stalls form a chain. Here it is, by year:

- A goal with no method yet, at Dartmouth in 1956.
- A single straight boundary that can’t handle XOR, from 1958 to 1969.
- Results that didn’t grow from tidy problems to realistic ones. That led to the first winter in 1974.
- Rule binders too costly to keep up to date, from the 1980 expert systems on. That led to the second winter in 1987.
- Too little computing power and labeled data for stacked layers, after 1986.
- Reading one word at a time, a habit that made LSTM training slow from 1997.
- A new labeled set and a new design for every task, even after AlexNet in 2012.
- Attention, with a cost that grows with the square of the text’s length, from 2017.
- Fluent assistants in 2022. Their smooth words are still not a check on truth.

Each link in that chain explains why the next idea mattered. Stacking layers got past XOR. Graphics chips and big labeled sets got past the shortage of computing power and labeled data. Attention got past reading one word at a time.

The learning-signal sort is here for one more reason. The four signals decide where a model’s right answers come from. The course keeps using their names. The Classical machine learning, Next-token prediction and Post-training labs each lean on a different signal. Knowing which is which now saves confusion later.

Press **Take a downhill step** a few times. Watch three things change together:

2. On the right, the you-are-here dot walks down across the contour lines toward the green ×. A dotted path traces its recent steps. The path keeps only about the last 30 steps. Press many times, and the oldest steps drop off the start of the path.

## What to notice

- The same product can sit in several rings at once. An assistant is an LLM. That makes it a generative model, and so deep learning too.
- A spelling checker built from written rules can be AI without being machine learning. Nobody fitted it from examples.
- The table, the shaded squares and L all come from the same three squared misses. So they always agree with each other.
- The downhill button moves the line using the slopes printed in the readouts. It uses no separate trick.
- The first downhill step from the flat line covers most of the distance. Later steps shrink. A slope that keeps flattening sets the size of each one.
- Every era has a stall. Even the 2022 stop is still next-token prediction plus later training. Nothing about it claims the field has no problems left.
- The n-gram dot sits in ML and outside DL. It is generative without being deep. So it can’t sit inside the Gen ring.
- The Expert systems stop is AI with nothing fitted. The first winter followed results that didn’t grow to bigger problems. The second followed methods that cost too much to keep up.
- Raw sentences and Blanked-out photos are both self-supervised. Yet one answer is a category, and the other is a number.
- Customer records and Sensor readings have no answer column. So their Output type reads no target.
- Placing Raw sentences under supervised draws a correction. The next word is already in the text. No person had to label it.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies or makes up

- The lab’s authors wrote the five rings and their captions by hand. They also chose where the n-gram dot sits. Nothing on the card sorts a real product.
- The authors wrote the eleven eras for the timeline card, too. Their dates, including the rough winter dates, are landmarks. They are not a complete history.
- The timeline follows only two threads: rule-based AI and neural nets. No model from any era actually trains on the card.
- The five leftovers and the eight learning-signal scenarios are hand-written too. Each one has a single best answer that the card chose.
- When you place something wrong, the card looks up the right answer in a fixed table. No model judges your answer.
- The authors chose the three dots by hand. The card never tests a fourth one. So it names the generalization gap but never measures it.
- The downhill step is one small update to a two-number line. It always uses the same fixed step size. Each slider stops its number at the edge of its range.
- The slopes come from a formula worked out by hand for this one line. Real training uses an automatic method that works for any model.
- The tiles under **Distinction** are written explanations. The card scores no system as conscious, general or understanding.

### Where the real ideas break

Product descriptions still blur these words together. A chatbot demo doesn’t make every method underneath it accurate or safe. Real products also mix methods. A search tool plus an LLM plus a rule-based filter is still three different rings. They work side by side.

In supervised learning, labels still decide what counts as correct. So biased labels still make a biased rule. A tiny training loss still doesn’t mean the rule works on new cases. You have to measure the generalization gap separately.

Real training uses the same parts you met here. It has a menu of rules, a loss and a search. It also has a hope that the result generalizes, or works on new examples. The differences are size and scoring. Real training uses billions of numbers instead of two. It often uses a loss that isn’t squared error.

The learning signals blend in real systems, too. So one product is not one box. A chat assistant first trains self-supervised on raw text. Next, training tunes it on answers that people wrote. After that, the assistant trains further with rewards. Those rewards come from which of its answers people preferred.

History is messier than any timeline. A method can stall for lack of money, data or computing power. It can also stall on a hard mathematical limit. A newer idea doesn’t erase an older problem, either. Fluent text is still not a check on truth. AGI still has no agreed test. So no lab here can say that a system has reached it.

**A common mix-up:** people often treat a chat assistant as the definition of AI. Others treat a low training loss as a sign of understanding. Both are mistakes. A large language model is one generative method, deep inside the rings. A low L on the training sample is a fit, not a mind.

People also read “unsupervised” as “no human involved.” Some read “self-supervised” as another word for “unsupervised.” Neither reading is right. Unsupervised learning has no target at all. Self-supervised learning still trains against labels. Those labels come free from the data itself. And a person is still involved in unsupervised learning. Someone has to decide whether the groups it finds mean anything.

## Going deeper

### The slopes, written out

The slopes on the Learning problem card come from exact formulas. They don’t come from trial and error. The slope ∂L/∂b is the average of 2 × (ŷ − y) over the three dots. The slope ∂L/∂w is the average of 2 × (ŷ − y) × x.

Try the first formula at w = 0 and b = 0. The three misses are −1.4, −2.1 and −3.2. They add up to −6.7. Doubling that gives −13.4. Dividing by 3 gives about −4.467. That matches the ∂L/∂b readout at that spot.

The second formula adds one step. First, multiply each miss by its dot’s x. That gives −0.7, −3.15 and −8.0. They add up to −11.85. Doubling gives −23.7, and dividing by 3 gives −7.9. That matches the −7.900 reading from the downhill walk.

Each guess wx + b changes in a straight-line way as you change w or b. Then L adds up the squares of those misses. Together, those two facts make L an exact bowl with one smooth bottom. They also make every contour line an oval. Squared error on a deep network doesn’t give such a simple bowl. Its guesses don’t change in a straight-line way.

The exact bottom is the line w = 0.90 and b ≈ 0.883. Its name is the **least-squares** line. The name means its squared misses are as small as they can be. No other straight line gives a smaller mean squared error on these three dots. So the legend labels the green × as the least-squares bottom. The legend is the key that explains the plot’s marks.

### Other settings you will meet

The step size of 0.12 is the hyperparameter this card names. Later labs add others. One is the batch size: how many examples each step looks at. Another is the epoch count: how many times training passes through the data.
