## The big picture

This module is about **synthetic data**, training text a model wrote rather than a person. It asks
what survives when models learn from models: which patterns a model fitted to generated text
keeps, which it loses for good, and what a filter, the original data, or a stronger writer
changes.

*Building a dataset* cleaned scraped text with deduplication and decontamination, and both apply
to generated text unchanged. What is new here is the loop, **recursive training**: each model is
fitted to text the previous one wrote, starting from human-written **seed data**.

### Where it shows up

- **The web crawl.** Generated text is cheap and unlimited, and a growing share of the web is
  already model-written, so the next crawl contains text a model produced.
- **Verified training sets.** Many training sets are generated on purpose. The ones that
  work pair a strong generator with a check, such as a code test or a matching math answer.
- **Distillation.** A large model writes a smaller one's training data, as OpenAI's
  text-davinci-003 did for Stanford's Alpaca.
- **Data governance.** Generated text carries the licence and policy of the model that wrote it.

### Why it matters

Synthetic data can only contain what its generator produces, so careless use fails in ways that
can look clean:

- **Replacing the data loses the tail.** A model trained only on the last model's output loses the
  rarest patterns first (in real systems, rare facts, dialects, and edge cases), then variety.
  That is **model collapse**. Sampling sharpened toward likely text, at low temperature
  or with top-p, speeds it.
- **Filtering can trade variety for quality.** A **verifier**, a check that accepts or rejects
  generated examples, raises quality but can only remove text.
- **Leaks pass fluency checks.** A generator that saw a benchmark can write its items back out.

What helps is verified synthetic data added to the original data. New patterns need a writer that
knows more than the student.

### What you will be able to explain

- What synthetic data is: model-generated text that becomes another model's training set.
- Why model collapse happens: models trained on finite samples of their own output lose rare
  patterns first, and the losses compound.
- How to judge a filter or verifier by what it keeps, and why it cannot restore what the generator
  no longer writes.
- What a fixed, stronger teacher changes: it breaks the loop and can add patterns the seed lacked,
  but only patterns it knows.

### What the lab shows

Every generation is the track's 30 × 30 character-bigram table, which predicts each character from
the one before it and is fitted in closed form by counting. Generation 0 is fitted to a fixed seed
of 499 characters of harbor notes, and ten generations follow. Sampling is real.

The **Recursive training** card plots two regimes at once, each as eight runs with different
sampling seeds plus their mean: **Synthetic only**, where each batch replaces the data, and
**Real + synthetic**, where it joins the seed. **Training data** highlights one regime and picks
which one's run 1 feeds **Which transitions survive** and **What a generation writes**, the cards
that show that run's pairs and text.

**Measure** picks **Transitions**, **Coverage**, **Entropy**, **Real words**, or **Unseen text**,
and **Generation** steps from 0 to 10. **Generator and filter** sets **Generator** (**The model
itself** or **A stronger teacher**), **Temperature**, **Filter**, and **Sample size**.

The key experiment: at the defaults, under Synthetic only, drag **Generation** to 10 and watch
Transitions, the number of distinct character pairs the table has seen, fall from 157 to about
51, while the Real + synthetic line stays at 157. On Unseen text, the model itself decays under
Synthetic only, and the teacher with Real + synthetic climbs.

Some parts are fixed or simplified. The teacher is a bigram too, trained once by SGD on the seed
plus 1,515 characters of extra notes the student never sees. The **Real words** measure and the
**Real words only** filter, the lab's verifier, are both a spelling check against the seed's 57
words. **Unseen text** asks only whether the pairs in 586 characters of hand-written held-out
sentences can be produced.

## What it is

**Synthetic data** is training text a model wrote. This lab runs the loop that
question raises: fit a model, let it write the next model's training text, and
repeat for ten generations of **recursive training**.

The model is the 30 × 30 character-bigram table used across this track, fitted
exactly by counting what follows each character. Generation 0 is fitted to 499
characters of harbor notes, the **seed data**. Its table holds 157 distinct
character pairs, which the chart calls **Transitions**.

Each later generation drafts text at a chosen **Temperature**, an optional
**Verifier** keeps what it accepts, and the next table is fitted to the result.
**Training data** decides what that fit sees:

- **Synthetic only**: the latest generated batch replaces the data.
- **Real + synthetic**: every batch is added to the human seed, which is
  **data accumulation**.

**Generator** decides who does the writing. **The model itself** is the loop
above. **A stronger teacher** is a fixed **teacher model**: a bigram trained
with the track's SGD trainer on the seed plus 1,515 characters of extra harbor
notes, 2,014 characters in all. It writes every generation's data. The student
is the same counting table as before and never sees those notes.

## Why it’s here

What survives when models learn from models turns on one mechanism. A model
fitted to a sample gives probability
zero to anything the sample missed, and it can never write what it gives zero.
Losses compound across generations, and the first things lost are the rarest.
This is the model collapse named in Shumailov et al. (Nature, 2024).

The same mechanism explains the success case. When the writer is a fixed model
that knows more than the student, nothing the student misses can reach the next
round, and what the writer knows can flow in.

## How to play with it

Start at **Generation** 0 and read what the seed-fitted model writes, then
step to 1. Keep **Training data** on **Synthetic only** and drag **Generation**
to 10. At the defaults (temperature 1.0, no filter, 500 characters per
generation) the mean of eight runs reads:

- **Transitions**: 157, 125, 105 at generations 0, 1 and 2, then 75 at 5 and 51
  at 10. The eight runs end between 46 and 58.
- **Coverage**: 100% falls to 74.1% at generation 5 and 58.9% at 10.
- **Entropy**: 1.787 nats falls to 0.912.

Switch to **Real + synthetic**. Transitions hold at 157 and Coverage at 100% at
every generation; Entropy only drifts to 1.713.

Now set **Filter** to **Real words only** and **Measure** to **Real words**.
Then lower **Temperature** to 0.6 and compare both regimes again.

Last, set **Filter** back to **No filter** and **Measure** to **Unseen text**. It
asks what share of the character pairs in 586 characters of held-out harbor
sentences a generation can produce at all. The seed-fitted model scores 92.6%.
With **The model itself** and **Synthetic only** it falls to 55.6% by generation 10.

Switch **Generator** to **A stronger teacher** with **Real + synthetic**, and the
line rises instead: 96.2%, 98.1%, 98.8%, and 99.4% at generations 1, 3, 5, and 10.
With **Synthetic only** it stays level.

## What to notice

- **The tail goes first.** In **Which transitions survive**, run 1 at
  generation 10 has lost 63 of the 72 pairs the seed used once, 35 of 58 used 2–4
  times, and 8 of 27 used five or more times. That is **finite-sample error**: a
  once-seen pair appears about once per 500 characters and is often simply missed.
- **Nothing new appears.** The grid only ever loses cells. Under Synthetic only
  the Transitions line can never rise, in any run.
- **Sample size is the brake.** At generation 10, Synthetic only keeps about 34
  transitions with 250 characters per generation, 51 with 500, and 109 with 2000.
- **Temperature is the accelerator.** At 0.8 the generation-10 mean is about 11;
  at 0.6 every run has fallen into the four-pair `the ` loop by generation 6. That
  is **distribution narrowing** taken to its end. At 1.2 the loss slows to 73 but
  does not stop.
- **Quality and variety come apart.** Under Synthetic only the verifier keeps
  about 5% of drafted characters at generation 1, pushes **Real words** above
  99%, and leaves 16 transitions, fewer than with no filter. `the the the` is 100%
  real words.
- **Keeping the seed is what works.** Under Real + synthetic the same verifier
  raises Real words from about 7% to 82% while all 157 transitions survive.
  Verified synthetic data plus the original data is the combination that helps.
- **A fixed writer stops the compounding.** With the teacher on Synthetic only,
  Unseen text is level from generation 1 instead of decaying, because the
  teacher is never refitted to a student's mistakes.
- **A level line is not a high line.** At 500 characters per generation the
  teacher's Synthetic only student sits near 87%, below the seed-fitted model's
  92.6%: one finite sample of the teacher still misses pairs. At **Sample size**
  2000 it reaches 97.5% with no human text in any round, where the model itself
  has decayed to 80.7%.
- **A verifier built from the seed throws the gain away.** With the teacher and
  **Real words only**, Synthetic only scores 42.2% at generation 1, and Real +
  synthetic ends at 93.0% instead of 99.4%. The teacher's extra knowledge is
  mostly words the seed never used, which the verifier rejects.
- **A teacher can only teach what it knows.** The seed plus the extra notes cover
  99.5% of the held-out pairs, the most this teacher's data can offer. **Transitions**
  can pass the data's 258 pairs, reaching 275 at generation 10, because SGD leaves
  about 1% of the teacher's probability on pairs its data never contained.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

Every number is computed from real sampling and exact refits, averaged over
eight sampling seeds. What is small is everything else: one character of
context, 900 numbers, a 499-character seed, ten generations. **Real words** is a
spelling check against 57 seed words, not a quality score, and it rewards
repetition.

The fit is by counting, so a missed pair is exactly zero. A table trained by SGD
from zero weights never reaches zero; retrained on its own samples it drifts
toward noise instead, emitting pairs the seed never had while its loss on the
real text rises. That is a second face of the same loop, and this lab does not
show it.

The teacher is a bigram too, fitted to four times the text, so it is stronger in
coverage and in nothing like understanding. **Held-out coverage** asks only
whether a pair can be produced, not how likely it is. The held-out sentences were
written by hand in the seed's style and share its theme, as the teacher's extra
notes do.

### In real systems

The mechanism carries over: rare facts, dialects, and edge cases are the tail,
and a model trained only on another model's finite output under-represents them.
Low-temperature or top-p sampling for "clean" data accelerates it. Gerstgrasser et
al. (2024) found that accumulating synthetic data alongside the original, rather
than replacing it, avoids the collapse in the models they tested, which is what
the Real + synthetic line shows.

**Contamination** is the other risk. A generator that saw a benchmark can write
its items back out, and the leak passes any fluency filter. Synthetic data needs
the same decontamination checks as scraped data.

## Going deeper

**Where synthetic data works.** Its successes pair a strong generator with a
check: code kept only if its tests pass, math kept only if the answer matches,
reasoning traces kept only when they reach a correct result (Zelikman et al.,
STaR, 2022). That is **rejection sampling**, and the acceptance rate is its cost.

**A stronger model as the writer.** Distilling a large model's outputs into a smaller
one is also synthetic data, with the teacher as the generator, which is what the
Generator control stages. Stanford's Alpaca fine-tuned a 7B LLaMA model on 52K
instruction-following examples generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003, for less
than $500 of API use.

**What a verifier can and cannot do.** A verifier moves probability toward
outputs that pass. It cannot restore an output the generator never writes, so
diversity has to come from the prompts, the seed data, or fresh human data.

**Whose terms the student inherits.** The Alpaca authors released it for academic
research only. They cited LLaMA's non-commercial licence and the terms of use of
text-davinci-003, which prohibit developing models that compete with OpenAI
(Stanford CRFM, 2023). Synthetic data carries the licence and policy of the
model that wrote it.

**Connections.** Building a dataset covers deduplication and decontamination,
which apply to generated text unchanged. Train one from scratch trains the same
table by gradient descent. The Distillation & pruning lab trains a student against a teacher's
probabilities rather than its sampled text, and Evaluation is where a leaked
benchmark item would show up.

### Toy versus real

- **Scale.** The lab writes 250 to 2000 characters per generation for ten
  generations. Alpaca's data was 52K instruction-following examples, generated from
  text-davinci-003 for less than $500 (Stanford CRFM, 2023).
- **The teacher.** The lab's teacher is a bigram trained on four times the seed.
  A real teacher is a far larger model, and the student's gain is bounded by what
  it knows, as the 99.5% ceiling is here.
- **Collapse.** The lab loses 106 of 157 pairs under Synthetic only and none under
  Real + synthetic. Shumailov et al. report that tails of the original
  distribution disappear when models train on model-generated content (arXiv
  2305.17493), and Gerstgrasser et al. that accumulating data avoids it in their
  models (arXiv 2404.01413).
- **Verifiers.** The lab's is a 57-word list that keeps about 5% of the first
  generation's draft. Real ones are test suites and answer checkers.
