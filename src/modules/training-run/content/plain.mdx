## The big picture

The language model behind any chatbot you have used was made by a **training run**. A program showed
the model a huge amount of text, checked its guesses about what comes next, and adjusted its numbers
by a tiny amount. That cycle repeated many thousands of times, and this module takes one training
run apart so you can watch every piece of it at work.

The model in this lab is tiny on purpose. It is a table of 900 numbers that guesses the next
character of a text from the one character before it. It learns from a short passage of notes about
the weather in a harbor town, which is only 499 characters long. Because the model is so small, the
lab retrains it from scratch whenever you change a training setting. Every number on the first four
cards therefore comes from a real run, not from a drawing.

The fifth card, about memory, is different. It works out its totals by multiplying, because no model
with billions of numbers is trained here. A few things also stay fixed. The learning rate, a number
that scales how big each adjustment is, is always 0.6. Every score is measured on the same text the
model learned from.

### Where you have met it

Every trained model came out of a loop like this one, including the very large models behind
chatbots. Their runs are vastly bigger, often use a different adjusting rule called Adam, and save
their progress more carefully. Even so, they go through the same seven stages in the same order. One
published run, for a family of models called Llama 2, made about 500,000 adjustments, and each one
was based on about 4 million pieces of text.

Size is where the difference shows most: the largest model on this lab's memory card has 70 billion
numbers. Training it with Adam needs room for those numbers plus several extra numbers about each
one. That comes to more than a trillion bytes, where one byte is about the space a single letter of
text takes. The card counts how many memory chips that would fill if each chip holds 32 billion
bytes, and it calls each such chip a "device". The answer is 35 devices.

### Why it matters

Without this loop, a model's numbers would stay where they started, and its guesses would be no
better than picking a character at random. The loop is what turns a pile of text into better
guesses, one small correction at a time.

Knowing how the loop works also protects you from three common mistakes. You might compare two runs
by how many times they read the text, when what actually counts is how many times they adjusted
their numbers. You might trust a single adjustment because it helped the examples it was built from,
even though it can make everything else worse. And you might plan a run around the size of the model
alone, when the extra numbers kept for training take several times more memory than the model does.

Saved copies of the model, called **checkpoints**, made along the way, matter too, because they let
you look inside a run partway through. They also let a run that crashes start again from the last
copy instead of from nothing.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- the seven stages of a training loop, in order, and what each stage hands on to the next one;
- the difference between a **batch**, a **step** and an **epoch**, and why two runs with the same
  number of epochs can end up far apart;
- how to load a saved copy of the model from partway through a run and test what it had learned by
  then;
- what one single update changes, meaning which rows of the table move, and why it helps its own
  small group of examples far more than the entire text;
- how to estimate a run's memory from three things: the model's size, how many bytes it uses for
  each number, and which adjusting rule it uses. You will also be able to say why big runs spread
  that memory across many chips.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab has five cards, and each one shows a different side of the same run:

- **Measured training loss** draws how the model's error score fell from the first update to the

## What it is

### The model: a sheet of guesses

The model works with 30 symbols: the space, the letters `a` to `z`, a full stop, and two angle
brackets, `<` and `>`, that only a later lab uses. Its job is to guess which of those symbols comes
next in a text. It does that with a grid of numbers that has one row for each symbol, so you can
think of it as a printed sheet of guesses.

Each row answers a single question: if the last character was this one, how likely is each character
to come next? A row holds 30 raw scores, one for every possible next character, and these scores are
called **logits**. A higher logit means a likelier guess, and to read a row the model turns its 30
logits into 30 chances, or **probabilities**, that add up to 100%. The rule that does this
conversion is called softmax, and it always gives the highest probability to the highest logit.

Thirty rows of 30 numbers makes 900 numbers, and those 900 numbers are the entire model. The numbers
a model learns are called its **weights**, or its parameters, and training a model means changing
them until its guesses improve.

Here is one row at work: after the text `the fo`, the model looks only at the last character, `o`,
and reads the `o` row. At the end of the default run, that row gives `r` a probability of 23.5%.
That is because the training text has words like `harbor`, `morning`, `storm` and `before`, where
`r` follows `o`.

A model that looks back only one character is called a **bigram** model, from "bi", meaning two,
because it works with pairs of characters. It cannot use the word or the sentence that the character
sits in, which is the main reason its guesses stay rough.

### The training loop as a study session

Training is one cycle repeated over and over, and the lab's **Training loop stages** strip shows the
seven stages of one trip around it. Before looking at each stage, it helps to have a picture of the
whole cycle.

Picture the guess sheet on a desk, next to a big stack of flashcards made from the training text.
Each card shows one character on the front and, on the back, the character that came right after it,
so making the deck is the job of the **Dataset** stage. Making the deck happens once, before the
first round. At the start of each pass through the deck, you shuffle it once. A single round of
study, which is one trip around the loop, then goes like this:

1. Deal yourself the next small handful of cards from the top of the shuffled deck, which is what
   the **Batch** stage does. When the deck runs out, that pass is over, and you shuffle again.
2. For each card, find the row for the front character and read off the sheet's guesses, which is
   the **Forward** stage.
3. Flip each card over and mark how badly the sheet did on the real answer, which is the **Loss**
   stage.
4. Calculate how each number on the sheet should change so that it would do better next time, which
   is the **Backward** stage.
5. Rub out the old numbers on the sheet and pencil in the new ones, which is the **Optimizer step**
   stage.
6. Every so often, photocopy the whole sheet and file the copy away somewhere safe, which is the
   **Checkpoint** stage.

The picture stops matching in two places. A person studying these cards would use the whole word and
its topic, but the guess sheet only ever looks at the single character on the front. You would also
use judgement to fix the sheet, whereas the training program calculates every correction by exact
arithmetic, the same way every time.

### The seven stages, one at a time

**Dataset** turns the text into flashcards, which the lab calls training pairs, where each pair is
one character plus the character that followed it. The lab draws a space as `␣`, so the words "the
fog", which have seven characters counting the space, give six pairs: `t→h`, `h→e`, `e→␣`, `␣→f`,
`f→o` and `o→g`. Every character except the very last one starts a pair, so there is always one pair
fewer than there are characters. That is why the stage displays **Characters** 499 and **Training
pairs** 498, while **Vocabulary** counts the 30 symbols.

**Batch** shuffles the pairs once at the start of every pass, then deals them out in handfuls in
order. A handful is called a **minibatch**, or a batch for short. The **Batch size** slider sets how
many pairs each handful holds, starting at 16, and the part of the training program that shuffles
and deals is called the **dataloader**. The shuffle matters because the text runs in order, so
without it a string of updates could all come from the sentence about winter. Those updates would
drag the model toward that one passage, while a shuffled handful is a fair mix of the entire text.
The stage's readouts are **Batch size**, **Batches / epoch** and **Total steps**, and the counting
part below explains how they fit together.

**Forward** takes each pair in the handful, reads the row for its first character, and turns that
row's 30 logits into 30 probabilities. Nothing in the model changes here, because this stage only
reads, and it is called the **forward pass**. Its readouts show the **Top prediction** and
**Runner-up** after `the fo`, plus **Row width**, which is 30 logits.

**Loss** checks each guess against the back of the card by looking at the probability the model gave
to the character that actually came next. It turns that probability into a penalty and averages the
penalties over the handful, and that average is called the **cross-entropy**. The next part explains
how the penalty is scored, and the stage's readouts are **Loss at this checkpoint**, **Its
perplexity** and the **Uniform baseline**.

**Backward** calculates, for every one of the 900 weights, how the penalty would change if that
weight went up a little. A positive number means raising the weight would make the penalty worse,
while a negative number means raising it would make the penalty smaller. That list of 900 numbers is
called the **gradient**, and the stage that works it out is called the **backward pass**. Like the
forward pass, the backward pass only reads the weights and does not change anything yet.

**Optimizer step** is the stage that finally changes the model, by multiplying each weight's
gradient by the **Learning rate** and subtracting the result from that weight. Subtracting moves
every weight the opposite way to its gradient, which is the direction that lowers the penalty, and
the learning rate is fixed at 0.6 in this lab.

The learning rate sets how big each change is: a larger one makes bigger jumps, and a smaller one
makes more careful ones. For example, picture a weight of 0.50 with a gradient of +0.10. The step
takes away 0.6 × 0.10 = 0.06, so the weight becomes 0.44. If the gradient were −0.10 instead, taking
away −0.06 would raise the weight to 0.56.

The rule that applies these changes is called the optimizer, and one use of it is one **optimizer
step**, or one **step** for short. This lab uses the plainest optimizer there is, called SGD, which
is short for stochastic gradient descent, where "stochastic" means working from randomly chosen
handfuls. The word "descent" fits if you picture the penalty as your height on a hill. Each step
moves the weights a little way downhill for the handful it was calculated from. As you will see
later, that is not always downhill for the whole text.

**Checkpoint** saves a copy of all 900 weights so that you can come back to them later. At the
default settings the run saves nine copies (some settings save ten), including the untrained model
at step 0, and the stage displays **Checkpoints**, **Selected step** and **Its loss**.

### Scoring a guess: cross-entropy and nats

The penalty follows one rule: the smaller the probability the model gave to the right answer, the
bigger the penalty. Every time that probability is cut in half, the penalty grows by the same
amount, about 0.69. Here is the ladder that rule builds, from a perfect guess down to a blind one:

- Giving the right character 100% costs nothing, since the model could not have done any better.
- Giving it 50% costs about 0.69, which is one halving away from being certain.
- Giving it 25% costs about 1.39, which is two halvings away from being certain.
- Keep halving: 12.5%, then 6.25%, then about 3.1% after five halvings, which costs about 5 × 0.693,
  or 3.47. (The 0.69 above is 0.693 rounded off.)
- Giving it 1 in 30, about 3.3%, sits just above that 3.1%, so it costs a little less: 3.401, which
  is what blind guessing scores.

The unit of this penalty is called the nat, so the lab reports the loss in **nats per token**. A
token is one piece of text the model reads, and in this lab every token is a single character. Nats
per token therefore means the average penalty for each character the lab scores. The Loss stage's
title calls the penalty the "negative log-probability", which is a name from a part of maths you may
meet later. The halving ladder above is all you need to read the numbers this lab displays.

Lower is better, as in golf, and a model that has learned nothing spreads its guesses evenly across
the symbols, so it scores 3.401 on every character. That score is the **Uniform baseline**, and the
loss chart draws it as a dotted line labelled "knows nothing, ln 30". The ln there is a button on a
scientific calculator, and ln 30 works out to 3.401.

Read the ladder backwards and you get a handy rule that the Loss stage mentions. Whenever the loss
falls by about 0.69 nats, the probability given to the right character has doubled, on average
across the characters scored.

The loss has a friendlier twin called **perplexity**, which turns the penalty back into a number of
options. It asks this question: if the model were choosing evenly among some number of characters,
how many would give this same score? Blind guessing among 30 symbols has a perplexity of 30, since
that is exactly the situation the question describes. The default run ends at a loss of 2.277, which
matches choosing evenly among 9.75 characters, so **Perplexity** reads 9.75.

You can check that with the ladder. Three halvings, or 1 in 8, cost about 2.08, and four halvings,
or 1 in 16, cost about 2.77. A score of 2.277 sits between them, so it matches choosing among a
number of options between 8 and 16, which works out to 9.75. In everyday terms, the trained model's
guesses are about as spread out as a multiple-choice question with ten options instead of thirty.

**Better than chance by** says the same thing a third way, by measuring how far the loss has come
down from blind guessing toward a perfect zero. The default run brought the loss from 3.401 down to
2.277, a drop of 1.124. That drop is 33% of the full 3.401, so the readout displays 33%. It is not
the share of guesses the model gets right, which is a different measurement that this lab does not
show.

One more detail matters when you read these numbers, because the lab scores the loss in two
different ways. During training, each handful's loss is averaged over its own pairs, and each point
on the chart's thin, jagged line is the average of a few handfuls' losses. The Loss stage instead
scores the checkpoint you picked on all 498 pairs. The whole body of training text is called the
**corpus**, so the lab calls that score the whole-corpus loss.

### Calculating the gradient: the backward pass

For this kind of model and this kind of penalty, the backward pass follows a rule short enough to do
by hand. For each of the 30 logits in a row, take the probability the model gave that character,
written as a decimal. Then subtract 1 from the probability of the character that actually came next,
and the 30 results are that row's part of the gradient.

The Backward stage draws this for one real pair from the text, `o` followed by `g`, from the words
"the fog". At step 0, before any training, the model gives every character the same probability of 1
in 30, which is about 0.033. So every other bar stands the same small height, +0.033, above the
line, while the bar for `g` drops below it. Only the `g` bar carries a printed number, which is
0.033 minus 1, or −0.967.

The strip has its own key: a bar above the line means that logit will be pushed down, while a bar
below it means the logit will be pushed up. That is because the optimizer step subtracts the
gradient, so taking away −0.967 raises the score for `g`. Taking away each small +0.033 lowers every
other score a little, and raising the right answer while lowering the others is the whole of what
learning means for this row.

On the **Run controls and checkpoint timeline** card, move **Checkpoint** to the end of the run,
step 384, and the `g` bar still reads −0.957. That is 0.043 minus 1, because the trained model gives
`g` only 4.3% after `o`. In this text, `r` follows `o` much more often than `g` does.

The strip shows one pair, but a real step uses a whole handful. Each pair's gradient is added into
the row for its first character, and then the total is divided by the number of pairs in the
handful. For a full handful that number is 16. A shorter handful, like the one you will meet at the
end of each pass, is divided by its own smaller count, so each of its pairs counts for more. A row that
starts three of the pairs can therefore get up to three times the push of a row that starts one. A
row gets nothing at all unless at least one pair in the handful starts with its character.

The stage also measures how big a gradient is overall, as one number called the **gradient norm**.
To calculate it, square every number in the gradient, add up the squares, and take the square root.
For the `o→g` strip at step 0, each of the 29 small bars of 0.033 squares to about 0.001, and
together they add up to about 0.03. The `g` bar of −0.967 squares to about 0.94. The total is about
0.97, and its square root is about 0.98. A bigger norm means the step built from that gradient
changes the weights more. **This pair's gradient norm** measures the strip, and **Last batch
gradient norm** measures the run's final handful. **Peak batch norm in the run** displays the
largest gradient norm that any handful in the run produced.

### Three ways to count: batch, step and epoch

A training run has three counts that sound alike but measure different things:

- A **batch** is one handful of pairs dealt out together, and the **Batch size** slider sets how
  many pairs each handful holds.
- A **step** is one update to the weights, calculated from one batch, and steps are the only thing
  that actually changes the model.
- An **epoch** is one complete pass through all 498 pairs, and the **Epochs** slider sets how many
  complete passes the run makes.

Plain division ties the three counts together, as you can see with the default setting of 16 pairs
per handful. One pass through 498 pairs needs 498 ÷ 16 = 31.125 handfuls, and since you cannot deal
part of a handful, that rounds up to 32. Thirty-one full handfuls use 496 pairs, which leaves 2 for
a small last handful. That is why **Batches / epoch** reads 32, and why the last handful of each
pass holds only those 2 pairs. The default run makes 12 passes, and 12 × 32 = 384 steps, which is
what **Total steps** displays.

### Two cards that zoom in

Below the loop, two more cards show what the stages hide, and **Step one batch** is the first. It
replays one real optimizer step from the checkpoint you pick. It shows how many weights moved and
lists the rows that moved most. The second, **Where the memory goes**, calculates how much memory a
training run needs for a model of any size, and the next part explains what it adds up.

### What training has to hold in memory

Computers store every number as a pattern of 0s and 1s, where each 0 or 1 is called a **bit**. Eight
bits make a **byte**, which is about the space one letter of plain text takes up. A KB is a thousand
bytes, a GB is a billion bytes, and a TB is a trillion bytes, which is the same as 1,000 GB.

To take a step, training has to keep three things in memory for every weight:

- the **weight** itself, which the forward pass reads every time it makes a guess;
- its **gradient**, which is the number that the backward pass calculated for it;
- its **optimizer state**, meaning any extra numbers the optimizer remembers about that weight from
  one step to the next.

How much each of these costs depends on two choices, and the first is **Precision**, which means how
many bytes each number gets. The full-size choice, fp32, gives every number 4 bytes, which is 32
bits. The letters "fp" stand for floating point, the normal way computers store numbers with
decimals. The other choice on the card, **Mixed 16-bit**, stores the weights and gradients in 2
bytes each, which speeds up the arithmetic.

More bytes let a number keep more digits, which is why this choice is called precision. A 2-byte
number keeps only about three digits or fewer, so a tiny update, like adding 0.0001 to 1.5, gets
rounded away and lost. So the optimizer also keeps a full 4-byte copy of every weight, called the
master copy, where those tiny updates can build up. Doing the arithmetic in 16-bit numbers while
keeping that 4-byte master copy is called **mixed precision**.

The second choice is the **Optimizer**, and SGD, the kind this lab trains with, remembers nothing at
all between steps. The **Momentum** optimizer keeps one extra 4-byte number for each weight. That
number is an average of the weight's recent gradients, updated after every step, and it helps the
weight keep moving in a steady direction. Adam keeps that same number, which the card's table also
calls momentum. It adds a second number, called variance, which tracks how big the recent gradients
have been.

Add up the bytes that one weight needs, multiply by the number of weights, and you have the memory
training needs before anything else is counted. That multiplication is all that the **Where the
memory goes** card does.

## Why it’s here

### Each stage feeds the next

The seven stages come in this order for a reason: each one uses exactly what the stage before it
produced. The dataset makes pairs, the batch picks a few of them, the forward pass turns them into
guesses, and the loss scores those guesses. The backward pass turns the score into a gradient, and
the optimizer step uses it to change the weights. Every stage before the optimizer step only reads
the weights, so that one stage is the only place where the model changes. Bigger runs change the
scale, the optimizer and the plumbing around saving, but they do not change this order.

### Why handfuls, and not the entire text

Why not update the weights from all 498 pairs at once, or from one pair at a time? Using the entire
text for every update would give the steadiest direction, but the model would change only once per
pass. Using a single pair would change the model often, but every update would chase whatever that
one pair happened to contain. A handful sits between these two extremes, and the **Batch size**
slider lets you move along that trade-off and measure what happens.

### Counting passes is not counting learning

The loop is also where two counts get mixed up. Picture two friends studying the same deck of 498
flashcards, and both of them go through the whole deck 12 times. One friend stops to fix their notes
after every 2 cards, which adds up to 2,988 rounds of fixes. The other fixes their notes only after
every 64 cards, which adds up to 96. Both can honestly say they went through the deck 12 times, yet
one of them corrected their notes about 30 times as often as the other.

The lab works the same way, since twelve epochs at a **Batch size** of 2 make 2,988 steps, while the
same twelve epochs at 64 make only 96. The two runs end at very different losses, which is why
**Total steps** is the honest number to compare between runs, and **Epochs** is not.

### One step is only a rough guess

A step is built from one handful, not from the entire text, so it is only a rough estimate of what
would help everywhere. The lab measured this in its 12-epoch runs at batch sizes 2, 16 and 64. Every
step lowered the loss on its own handful, but some steps made the model worse on the rest of the
text.

Picture a teacher who rewrites the class study guide after marking only two quizzes. The changes
lean the guide toward the mistakes those two students made, but they might confuse everyone else in
the class, who never had those problems. Marking sixteen quizzes first would give a fairer picture
of what the whole class needs. Training has the same problem, but its steps point the right way on
average, so the run still improves over many steps even when single steps misfire. The picture stops
matching in one way: a teacher can rewrite anything, while a step only nudges the numbers a small,
calculated amount toward the pairs in its handful.

### Checkpoints let you look back

Picture a long video game that only lets you save at the very end. If the power cuts out halfway,
you lose everything, and you can never go back to see how you played on an earlier level. A training
run without checkpoints is like that, because it reports one number at the end and nothing else.

Saved copies fix both of those problems, because you can load the model from partway through and
test it, which shows you when and how it improved. In a large run, a crash far into the run would
otherwise throw away all of that work. With a recent saved copy, the run loses only the steps taken
since that copy was made.

### Memory is more than the model

The last problem is space, because to take a step, training must hold the weights, the gradient and
everything the optimizer remembers, all at the same time. Under Adam, the weights themselves take up
only a small fraction of that total memory. For a large model the total is far more than one of the
card's 32 GB devices can hold, so large runs have to plan carefully where every byte goes.

## How to play with it

### Walk the loop

Start with the **Training loop stages** strip and click all seven stages in order, reading the
title, the explanation and the three readouts that each one prints. At **Backward**, look at the
strip of 30 bars for the pair `o→g` and find the one long bar, which belongs to `g`. The default run
makes 12 passes at **Batch size** 16, which gives 32 **Batches / epoch** and 384 **Total steps**.
Over on the **Measured training loss** card, **Final loss** reads 2.277 nats per token, while
**Perplexity** reads 9.75.

On that same card, compare the three lines that the chart draws, and notice how differently they
behave. The thin line is the loss on handfuls, the thick line is the whole-corpus loss at each
checkpoint, and the dotted line is the "knows nothing" baseline at 3.401.

### Resize the batch

**Try this:** leave **Epochs** at 12 and drag **Batch size** from one end to the other, watching
**Total steps** and **Final loss** as you go. Before you drag, predict which end of the slider will
finish with the lower loss, and decide why you expect it.

- At a batch size of 2, the run takes 2988 steps and lands at a final loss of 1.877 nats.
- At a batch size of 16, the run takes 384 steps and lands at a final loss of 2.277 nats.
- At a batch size of 64, the run takes only 96 steps and lands at a final loss of 2.808 nats.

The text and the learning rate never changed between these runs, so the difference in final loss
comes mainly from the number of updates each run was allowed to make. Each small-batch update is
also noisier, as the backfire experiment below shows.

Then set **Batch size** back to 16 and drag **Epochs** on its own to watch the same effect from the
other side. One epoch ends at 3.166 nats, while sixty epochs reach 1.921.

### Reload a checkpoint

Set the run back to 12 epochs at **Batch size** 16, then scrub **Checkpoint** from step 0 to
step 384. Four separate readouts move together as you scrub, because all of them come from the same saved
copy. They are **Its loss** on the Checkpoint stage, the big dot on the loss chart, the sample text,
and the **Next character after "the fo" at this checkpoint** list. At step 0 every option in that
list reads 3.3%, and by step 384 `r` leads with 23.5%.

The stages card shows one stage at a time, so scrub once with the Checkpoint stage open. Then scrub
again with the Backward stage open, and watch the `g` bar move from −0.967 at step 0 to −0.957 at
step 384. It barely changes, because `g` is not what usually follows `o` in this text.

The sample is 96 characters that the lab writes by picking each next character at random, weighted
by the model's probabilities. A setting called temperature, fixed at 0.8 here, tilts each pick a
little toward the likelier characters. The picks also use a fixed seed, which is a starting number
that makes the computer's random choices come out the same every time. That means any change between
checkpoints comes from the weights and not from luck.

### Step one batch

Now set **Checkpoint** on the Run controls card to step 0, scroll to **Step one batch**, and press
the **Step one batch** button once. This applies the run's very first handful of 16 pairs to the
untrained model. The pairs appear as small labels, such as `o→r` and `e→a`, drawn from all over the
text. Then read the four readouts on the card, which report what that single update did:

- **Batch loss, before → after** goes from 3.401 to 3.362, measured only on the 16 pairs in that
  handful.
- **Batch gradient norm** reads 0.2567, which measures how big that handful's whole gradient is.
- **Whole-corpus loss, before → after** moves only from 3.401 to 3.396, because it is measured
  across all 498 pairs in the text.
- **Weights changed** reads 270 of 900 in 9 rows, one row for each character that started a pair in
  the handful.

The count of 270 comes from whole rows, because every weight in a touched row moves. Softmax gives
every character some probability, so all 30 logits in that row get part of the gradient. Nine rows
of 30 weights is 270, and the other 630 weights stay exactly as they were.

Now look at the gap between the two losses. The step was calculated to fit those 16 pairs, so it
helps them a lot. But 16 pairs are only about 3% of the 498, so their gain shrinks a lot once it is
averaged over the whole text. The nine rows that moved also hold 308 of the 498 pairs, and inside
them the effect is mixed. About 78 pairs, including the handful's own, get a little better, because
they ask for the same next character as the handful. About 230 that needed a different next
character get slightly worse. The other 190 pairs sit in rows that did not move, so they do not
change at all, and the whole-corpus average barely moves.

The table below the readouts lists up to eight rows with the biggest gradients, and its top row is
`s`. It started 3 of the 16 pairs, and two of them asked for a space, so their pushes add up. The
space row also started 3 pairs, but they asked for `f`, `m` and `r`, so their pushes partly cancel
and its gradient is smaller. The **Target** column displays the next character a row was asked for
most often, which for `s` is a space. **Target probability** shows the space's probability in the
`s` row rising from 3.3% to 3.6%. On this first press nine rows moved, so the caption mentions one
more row that changed less.

Press twice more, and a different set of rows moves each time, because each press applies the next
handful the run actually used. **Back to the checkpoint** returns you to the saved copy whenever you
want to start over.

### Watch a step backfire

Now set **Batch size** to 2, put **Checkpoint** at step 1492, and press **Step one batch** again and
again, up to its limit of 24 presses. **Batch loss** still falls on every press, but **Whole-corpus
loss** rises on 17 of the first 24. For comparison, at **Batch size** 16, 24 presses from the
checkpoint at step 192 never raise it.

Here is how a tiny handful can backfire, using a made-up example. Picture a handful of two that holds
the pair `e→a`, from a word like "sea". The step raises `a` in the `e` row, and because the
probabilities in a row always add up to 100%, every other choice in that row loses some probability.
That includes the space after `e`, which the text needs after every "the". The two pairs in the
handful improve, while the many other pairs that need that space get a little worse. (Your 24 presses
hold other `e` pairs, such as `e→v` and `e→r`, and they make the same kind of trade.)

Every step makes this kind of trade, so why does it backfire so often here? By step 1492, the `e`
row already gives `a` about 7%, which is close to its share of the text: 4 of the 55 pairs that
start with `e`. A full push for one `e→a` pair overshoots that share. In a handful of two, that one
pair also drives half the step, eight times the share it would get in a handful of 16.

### Count the memory

Last, scroll to **Where the memory goes**, which starts at **Model size** 7.5 billion, with
**Precision** set to **Mixed 16-bit** and **Optimizer** set to **Adam**. Here is what a single
weight costs at those settings:

- The weight itself takes 2 bytes, because Mixed 16-bit stores it in 16 bits.
- Its gradient also takes 2 bytes, because the backward pass stores it in the same 16-bit format.
- The **optimizer state** takes 12 bytes: the 4-byte master copy, plus 4 for momentum and 4 for
  variance.

That adds up to 16 bytes, which is what **Bytes per parameter** displays. Multiply 16 bytes by 7.5
billion weights and you get 120 billion bytes, or 120 GB. Weights and gradients take 15 GB each, and
the optimizer state takes the other 90 GB. That is six times as much as the weights, which is what
**Optimizer state ÷ weights** reports.

A device here means one chip with its own memory, like the graphics chips, called GPUs, that big
runs train on. Here the card uses the word "state" for everything training holds for each weight:
the weight, its gradient and its optimizer state together. The card assumes each device holds 32 GB,
and 120 GB ÷ 32 GB is 3.75, which rounds up to 4 whole devices. So **32 GB devices for state alone**
reads 4 for this model.

Now switch **Precision** to fp32 and predict the new total before you look. It stays at 120 GB. The
weight and its gradient each grow from 2 bytes to 4, which adds 4 bytes, but fp32 needs no separate
master copy, which takes 4 away. Each weight now costs 4 + 4 + 8 bytes, where the 8 is Adam's two
4-byte numbers, so the total is still 16. Only the split between the parts changes.

**A common mix-up:** "16-bit" sounds as if it should halve the memory, but it does not shrink what
Adam needs at all. In both precisions each weight costs 16 bytes. Mixed precision only moves bytes.
The weight and the gradient each shrink from 4 bytes to 2, and the 4 bytes saved go into the master
copy in the optimizer state. Its gain is faster arithmetic, not a smaller total on this card.

Then switch **Optimizer** to SGD, and momentum and variance disappear, so the total falls to 8 bytes
per weight, or 60 GB. With fp32 still selected, the optimizer-state segment vanishes from the bar
completely. Switch back to **Mixed 16-bit** and that segment returns at 30 GB, half the bar, because
SGD in mixed precision still needs the 4-byte master copy. That copy is twice the size of the 2-byte
weight, so each weight costs 2 + 2 + 4 = 8 bytes.

Finally, switch **Optimizer** back to Adam and set **Model size** to 70 billion, then watch the
total climb to 1.12 TB while the device readout jumps to 35.

## What to notice

- The thin batch-loss line on the chart stays jagged while the thick whole-corpus line drops
  smoothly. Each point on the thin line averages only a few handfuls of 16 pairs, so it wobbles with
  whichever pairs those handfuls happened to hold.
- The last handful of each pass holds only 2 pairs, so it is the noisiest of all. The small ticks
  under the chart mark where each pass ends, which is every 32 steps at the default setting.
- Scrub through the nine checkpoints and the loss reads 3.401, 3.068, 2.816, 2.641, 2.530, 2.438,
  2.374, 2.320 and then 2.277. The first 48 steps cut it by about 0.33, while the last 48 cut it by
  only about 0.04. Each stretch of steps buys less than the one before, right from the start.
- **Rows updated** on the Optimizer step stage reads 26 / 30, because four symbols never start a
  pair in this text. Those four are `j`, `x` and the two angle brackets, so their rows are never
  changed at all.
- **Peak batch norm in the run** reads 0.749 at **Batch size** 16, but that peak comes from one of
  the 2-pair handfuls left over at the end of a pass. No full handful of 16 reaches even half of
  that. At a batch size of 2 every handful has 2 pairs, and the peak reaches about 1.09.
- Averaging over more pairs makes each gradient shorter and steadier. Each pair supplies only one
  sixteenth of a 16-pair gradient, and because the pairs push on different rows and characters,
  their pushes add up only partly. Pairs in the same row that ask for different next characters also
  partly cancel.
- **Better than chance by** reads 33%, which is the same fact as **Perplexity** falling from 30
  options to 9.75.
- The Forward stage prints "the fo" but uses only the final `o`, because a bigram throws the rest
  away. **Top prediction** is `r` at 23.5%, from words like `harbor`, `morning`, `storm` and
  `before`, while at step 0 every option reads 3.3%.
- The sample uses the same seed at every checkpoint, so early checkpoints print almost the same
  string, and later ones still share their opening letters. Any change comes from the weights, not
  from luck. Even at step 384, most of it is gibberish with real fragments inside, such as `the`,
  `and` and `thin`.
- One sample is not a measurement, so a 96-character sample can look worse at a later checkpoint
  even while the loss fell. Trust **Its loss** over your eyes when the two seem to disagree.
- One step helps its own handful far more than it helps the entire text: 0.039 against 0.005 nats at
  step 0. Only rows that started a pair in the handful move, so 630 of the 900 weights are not
  touched. The nine rows that did move still cover most of the text.
- A step can make the entire text worse, and the smaller the batch, the more often it happens. At
  **Batch size** 2, the whole-corpus loss rose on 1,174 of the 2,988 steps in the 12-epoch run. At
  16 it rose on 5 of 384 steps, and at 64 it rose on none of the 96. The loss on each step's own
  handful fell every single time, so the run improves on average, not at every step.
- **Mixed 16-bit** does not shrink what **Adam** needs, which is 16 bytes per weight in both
  precisions. In mixed precision the optimizer state is six times the weights, and in fp32 it is
  twice the weights.
- At 70 billion weights, the weights, gradients and optimizer state together come to 1.12 TB, which
  fills 35 devices of 32 GB before a single activation is stored. The optimizer state on its own is
  840 GB of that. An activation is an in-between result that a large model calculates during the
  forward pass and keeps for the backward pass.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Everything on screen is computed for real: the pairs, the shuffle, the probabilities, the loss,
every gradient, every update and every saved copy of the weights. The run saves nine copies at the
default settings and ten at some others. What is missing is structure, because the model sees one
character of context and has only 900 numbers. It has no hidden layers, which are the extra stages
of number-crunching that real models run between reading and guessing. It also has no tokenizer to
split text into word pieces, and its training text is only 499 characters long. The **Learning
rate** is stuck at 0.6, and there is no control to change it.

Keep in mind that the stages do not all describe the same moment in the run. The Forward and Loss
stages, and the Backward strip, describe the **Checkpoint** you picked. The counts, the batch
gradient norms and the step totals describe the whole finished run.

The loss is also measured on the training text itself, and there is no held-out text, meaning text
the model never trained on. Nothing in this lab can therefore tell real learning apart from
memorising these exact sentences, and the next lab, **Building a dataset**, adds that check.

**Step one batch** is a replay, not a second run, because it rebuilds the handful that the run's
seeded shuffle dealt at that step. It applies the trainer's own arithmetic, so pressing it again
always gives the same numbers. Like the run itself, its update leaves out three extras that big runs
use: momentum, gradient clipping and weight decay. The "Toy versus real" list at the end of the
lesson explains the last two. A press past the final checkpoint deals the next pass's handful, which
is a step the saved run never took, and the card says so.

The Backward strip shows one pair's gradient at a saved checkpoint, which is not the same as the
full handful's gradient that the optimizer applied at that step.

**Where the memory goes** is multiplication, since nothing is set aside or measured. Activations are
left out on purpose, so each total is the least a run could need rather than a forecast. The only
real memory in this lab belongs to its own trainer: 3,600 bytes of weights and 3,600 bytes of
gradient, with no optimizer state under plain SGD. Splitting a run across devices is described here
but not simulated, so the card never shows how the bytes would be divided between chips.

More epochs is not the same as more learning. Epochs count passes through the
text, and only steps change the weights. Twelve epochs at **Batch size** 64 make 96 updates, while
twelve at 2 make 2988 and end nearly a whole nat lower. Small batches win here by updating more
often per pass, but the cost is that each update comes from fewer pairs. That is why, at **Batch
size** 2, the whole-corpus loss rose on 1,174 of the 2,988 steps.

### Where real training runs break

Real runs differ in ways this one cannot show, starting with how they count. They count tokens seen
rather than epochs, and the biggest make only one pass through their text, so the epoch stops
meaning much. The **dataloader** also becomes a real bottleneck, meaning it can turn into the slow
part that holds the whole run back.

A run that restarts must carry on exactly as before, and Adam's remembered numbers shape every step
it takes. So a **checkpoint** that can restart a run must save the optimizer state as well as the
weights. That optimizer state can be twice the size of the weights, or six times in mixed precision.
The checkpoint also needs the dataloader's place in the text and the state of the random shuffles.

Memory is also more than the three parts of the card's bar, as a research paper called ZeRO points
out, and the card's numbers follow that paper. It looks at a 1.5 billion weight model reading 32
examples at a time, each 1,000 tokens long. In that case the activations take about 60 GB, which is
more than twice the 24 GB of weights, gradients and optimizer state. Working the activations out
again during the backward pass, instead of storing them, cuts them to about 8 GB. The paper also
names short-term scratch space and wasted gaps in memory as costs that appear at large scale.

Real loss curves also throw sudden spikes that a small, smooth toy run like this one never produces.
The usual fix is to roll back to the last checkpoint and skip the handfuls that caused the spike,
which only works if someone saved one.

## Going deeper

### Adam and the optimizer step

Adam divides each weight's gradient by a running estimate of how large that weight's gradients
usually are. That lets one learning rate work for weights whose gradients differ enormously in size,
at the cost of two extra numbers to remember for every weight.

### Building big batches

Big batches are usually built by adding up: the run does several forward and backward passes, adds
their gradients together, and then takes one **optimizer step**. This is called gradient
accumulation, and the loss curve responds to the number of steps, not to how many smaller handfuls
were added together for each one.

### Saving checkpoints at scale

At large scale, saving happens in the background and is split across many machines. Stopping
everything to write out a huge optimizer state would leave every machine in the run sitting idle.
Deciding how many checkpoints to keep becomes a policy: a few for restarting, a thin history for
study, and a record of which one a released model came from.

### Memory and parallelism

The 16 bytes per weight have to live somewhere, so big runs divide them up rather than shrink them.
Real models are built from many layers stacked one after another, each with its own grids of
numbers, and there are three main ways to split the work across chips:

- Data parallelism gives each chip a different handful of examples to work through.
- Tensor parallelism splits each layer's grids of numbers into pieces held by different chips.
- Pipeline parallelism gives each chip a different slice of the stack of layers, like stations on an
  assembly line.

A paper by Narayanan and colleagues shows how the three combine to train on thousands of GPUs. Data
parallelism on its own still leaves every chip holding a full copy of the 16 bytes per weight.

### Sharding

**Sharding** is the fix for that wasted space, because it stops every chip from storing the same
full copy. Picture a group project where, instead of everyone printing the whole binder of notes,
each member keeps one chapter and borrows the others when needed. Sharding works the same way,
because each chip keeps only a slice of the weights, gradients and optimizer state, and fetches the
rest when it needs them. A method called ZeRO splits all three across the chips, and FSDP, a feature
of the PyTorch training toolkit inspired by ZeRO, does the same kind of splitting. The card's 120 GB
for 7.5 billion weights is the ZeRO paper's own example.

### Why this matters for LoRA

Fine-tuning means training an already trained model further on new examples, and only the weights
that are being trained need a gradient and optimizer state. Training a small add-on, called an
adapter, beside weights that stay frozen, meaning unchanged, therefore removes most of those two
costs. The LoRA paper compares this with fine-tuning all of GPT-3, a 175 billion weight model, with
Adam. It reports 10,000 times fewer trained numbers and 3 times less GPU memory.

A related method, QLoRA, also stores the frozen weights in 4 bits each. Its paper reports
fine-tuning a 65 billion weight model on a single 48 GB GPU, and the **LoRA & adapters** lab trains
one of these adapters.

### Toy versus real

- **Updates:** this lab's default run takes 384 steps of 16 pairs each, which is tiny next to a
  published run. Llama 2 reports 2.0 trillion training tokens at 4 million tokens per batch, which
  works out to about 500,000 updates.
- **Step size:** this lab uses plain SGD at a learning rate of 0.6, while runs that use Adam pick
  far smaller rates. An Adam rate and an SGD rate work differently, though, so the two cannot be
  compared directly. GPT-3's published rates run from 0.0006 for its smallest model down to 0.00006
  for its largest. Llama 2 used 0.0003 for its two smaller models, and half that for its two
  largest.
- **Memory:** the lab's whole model is 3.6 KB, which is 900 weights at 4 bytes each. At 16 bytes per
  weight, 7.5 billion weights take the card's 120 GB, before any activations are counted.
- **What the loop adds:** the GPT-3 and Llama 2 papers both wrap these seven stages in three extras
  that this lab leaves out. A warmup-and-decay schedule starts the learning rate small, raises it,
  and then lowers it again over the run. Gradient clipping shrinks any gradient whose norm is over
  1.0 back down to a length of 1.0. Weight decay, set to 0.1, pulls every weight a little toward
  zero at each step.
