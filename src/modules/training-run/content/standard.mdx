## The big picture

A training run cuts text into training pairs, then turns untrained weights into a model by
repeating one cycle over them: draw a batch, predict, score the prediction, compute a gradient,
update the weights, and now and then save them. This module names those seven stages and asks
what each hands to the next, how many updates a run really makes, and what it costs in memory.

It builds on **Next-token prediction**, which showed what a trained character model outputs,
and **Training dynamics**, which showed how loss curves respond to settings. Here the loop
underneath both is taken apart one stage at a time.

### Where it shows up

A frontier run keeps the same stage order as the lab's toy run, plain SGD on a 900-weight
character model, and differs from it in scale, in the optimizer, and in the machinery around
checkpointing.

- **Pretraining.** Llama 2 reports 2.0 trillion tokens at a global batch of 4 million tokens,
  which works out to about 500,000 updates. Runs that large are counted in tokens seen, not
  epochs (passes over the data).
- **Memory.** Under Adam, weights, gradients and **optimizer state** (what the optimizer keeps
  between steps) cost 16 bytes per parameter: 120 GB at 7.5 billion parameters in the ZeRO
  paper. ZeRO **shards** all three across devices so each holds a slice, and PyTorch's FSDP,
  motivated by ZeRO, shards the parameters.
- **Recovery.** When a production loss curve spikes, the standard response is to roll back to
  the last checkpoint and skip the offending batches.
- **Fine-tuning.** LoRA and QLoRA, which train a small adapter beside frozen weights, follow
  from the same accounting: only parameters that are trained need a gradient and optimizer
  state.

### Why it matters

The **optimizer step**, which uses the gradient to update each weight (in this lab, by
subtracting the gradient times the learning rate), is the only stage that changes the weights.
A final loss does not say how many updates produced it; that takes the step count, not the epoch
count.

Without checkpoints, a run that fails has nothing to roll back to. Without a memory estimate, you
cannot tell in advance whether a run fits: at 70 billion parameters under Adam, model state alone
is 1.12 TB.

### What you will be able to explain

- The seven stages of a training loop, from Dataset to Checkpoint, and what each produces.
- How an epoch, a step and a batch differ, and how **Batch size** links epochs to steps.
- How a checkpoint lets you inspect a model partway through training.
- What one optimizer step changes: which rows move, and how the loss on its own batch differs
  from the whole-corpus loss.
- How training memory follows from parameters, precision and optimizer, and why large runs
  split it across devices.

### What the lab shows

The lab trains a real character bigram, which predicts each character from the one before it:
a 30 by 30 table of next-character scores, 900 weights, fitted by minibatch SGD to the
499-character **Harbor weather notes** corpus at a fixed **Learning rate** of 0.6. Changing
**Batch size** or **Epochs** retrains it. Every loss is scored on the training text; there is no
held-out set.

- **One pass through the training loop** walks the **Training loop stages**; click each stage
  to read what it reports for this run.
- **Measured training loss** plots the batch loss, the whole-corpus loss, and the dotted "knows
  nothing" line at ln 30.
- **Run controls and checkpoint timeline** holds those two sliders and **Checkpoint**, which
  reloads a saved copy of the weights and samples text from it.
- **Step one batch** applies one real optimizer step to that checkpoint's weights, using the
  batch the run drew next. From the final checkpoint, the default, it draws the next epoch's
  batch, a step the saved run never took.
- **Where the memory goes** is closed-form arithmetic over **Model size**, **Precision** and
  **Optimizer**: nothing is allocated or measured, activations are left out, and sharding is
  described, not simulated.

The first four cards are computed from the run, not illustrated. The key experiment holds
**Epochs** at 12 and moves **Batch size**: across its range, a larger batch generally means
fewer **Total steps** and a higher **Final loss**, though neighbouring sizes can tie on steps.

## What it is

A training run is one cycle repeated until you stop it. The **Training loop stages** flow walks
that cycle once: Dataset, Batch, Forward, Loss, Backward, Optimizer step, Checkpoint. Each
stage consumes exactly what the stage before it produced.

The model is a character bigram — a 30 by 30 table of logits, where the row for the current
character holds the scores for the character that comes next. **Weights** reads 900, and that
is the entire model.

- **Dataset** cuts text into pairs: one context character, and the character after it.
  **Characters** and **Training pairs** differ by exactly one, because every character except
  the last begins a pair.
- **Batch** shuffles the pairs with a seeded generator and slices them. That is the
  **dataloader**'s work. The shuffle makes each batch a fair sample of the whole text, so
  consecutive updates do not all pull toward one passage.
- **Forward** reads one row and softmaxes it into 30 probabilities. A **forward pass** changes
  nothing.
- **Loss** averages the negative log-probability of the character that actually followed over
  the **minibatch**. That is **cross-entropy**, reported in **nats per token**.
- **Backward** produces one number per weight. For softmax cross-entropy a row's gradient is
  the predicted distribution minus a one at the correct character. The Backward stage draws it
  for one real pair, `o` followed by `g` in "the fog".
- **Optimizer step** subtracts that gradient scaled by the **Learning rate**, held at 0.6.
- **Checkpoint** copies the weights so you can return to them.

**Vocabulary** is 30 symbols: space, `a` to `z`, a full stop, and two reserved angle brackets
that only a later lab uses. Spread probability evenly across 30 and you score ln 30 = 3.401
nats per token — the **Uniform baseline** and the dotted "knows nothing" line.

Two cards below the loop zoom in on what the stages hide. **Step one batch** replays a single
real **optimizer step** from the checkpoint you select and shows the rows it moved. **Where the
memory goes** counts the bytes a training run needs per parameter, for a model of any size.

## Why it’s here

Every claim about a trained model rests on this loop having run.

The loop is also where two units get conflated. An **epoch** is one pass over the data; a
**step** is one weight update. Steps per epoch are the pair count divided by **Batch size**, so
the same **Epochs** setting buys very different amounts of learning at different batch sizes.
**Total steps** is the number worth comparing across runs. Checkpoints matter for a separate
reason: without them a run reports one number at the end and nothing else.

Two more facts about the loop get lost at scale. A step is a noisy estimate built from one
batch, so it can help the batch and hurt everything else. And the bytes a run needs are set by
the optimizer as much as by the model: the weights are the smallest part of what training holds.

## How to play with it

Click all seven stages and read the detail line at each. The default run is 12 epochs at
**Batch size** 16: 32 **Batches / epoch**, **Total steps** 384, **Final loss** 2.277 nats per
token, **Perplexity** 9.75.

Hold **Epochs** at 12 and move **Batch size**. At 2 the run takes 2988 steps and ends at 1.877
nats; at 16, 384 steps and 2.277; at 64, 96 steps and 2.808. Same corpus, same epoch count,
same learning rate — the only change is how many updates the run was allowed. Then move
**Epochs** alone: one epoch ends at 3.166, sixty epochs reach 1.921.

Finally scrub **Checkpoint** from step 0 to step 384 and watch **Its loss**, the large dot on
the loss chart, the sample text, and the `Next character after "the fo"` ranking move together.
Open the Backward stage while you do: the bar at `g` reads −0.967 at step 0, where every
character has probability 1/30, and −0.957 at step 384, because the model still gives `g` only
4.3 percent after "o".

Then open **Step one batch**. With **Checkpoint** at step 0, press the button once. The run's
first batch, 16 pairs, is applied to the untrained table. **Batch loss** goes 3.401 → 3.362 and
**Batch gradient norm** reads 0.2567, but **Whole-corpus loss** moves only 3.401 → 3.396.
**Weights changed** reads 270 of 900: nine rows, one for each character that began a pair in
that batch. Press twice more and watch a different set of rows move.

Now set **Batch size** to 2, put **Checkpoint** at step 1492, and press again and again.
**Batch loss** still falls on every press, but **Whole-corpus loss** rises on 17 of the first
24. At batch size 16, 24 presses from the checkpoint at step 192 never raise it.

Last, scroll to **Where the memory goes**. At **Model size** 7.5 billion with **Mixed 16-bit**
and **Adam**, the total is 120 GB: 15 GB of weights, 15 GB of gradients, and 90 GB of
**optimizer state**. That is 16 bytes per parameter. Switch **Precision** to fp32 and the total
does not move. Switch **Optimizer** to SGD and it falls to 8 bytes per parameter, 60 GB.

## What to notice

- The thin batch-loss line stays jagged while the thick whole-corpus line falls smoothly: each
  batch point estimates the loss from a few batches of 16 pairs. 32 batches over 498 pairs also
  leaves a final batch of only 2 pairs per epoch, the noisiest point of each pass. The ticks
  under the axis mark those epoch boundaries, every 32 steps.
- Checkpoint losses run 3.401, 3.068, 2.816, 2.641, 2.530, 2.438, 2.374, 2.320, 2.277. The
  first 48 steps buy more than the last 48. Diminishing returns start immediately.
- **Rows updated** reads 26 / 30. Four symbols — `j`, `x`, and the two reserved angle brackets
  — never appear as a context character here, so those rows get no gradient.
- **Peak in the run** reads 0.749 at **Batch size** 16 and about 1.09 at 2. Averaging over more
  pairs shortens the gradient as well as steadying it.
- **Better than chance by** reads 33 percent, the same fact as **Perplexity** falling from 30
  effective options to 9.75.
- The Forward stage prints the context "the fo" but uses only the final `o`: a bigram
  conditions on one character and discards the rest. **Top prediction** is `r` at 23.5 percent
  — `harbor`, `morning`, `storm`, `before`. At step 0 all 30 options read 3.3.
- The sample uses a fixed sampling seed, so consecutive checkpoints print nearly the same
  string with a few characters swapped. Even at step 384 most of it is nonsense with real
  fragments inside — `the`, `and`, `thin`.
- One step improves the batch it was fitted to by about ten times what it improves the corpus:
  0.039 against 0.005 nats at step 0. Because only rows that began a pair move, the other rows'
  pairs are barely touched.
- A step can hurt the corpus. Over the 12-epoch run, the whole-corpus loss rises on 1,174 of
  2,988 steps at batch size 2, on 5 of 384 at batch size 16, and on none of 96 at batch size 64.
  The loss on the step's own batch fell on every one of them. The run improves on average, not
  at every step.
- **Mixed 16-bit** does not shrink model state under **Adam**: 16 bytes per parameter in both
  precisions. In mixed precision the optimizer state is six times the weights; in fp32, twice.
- At 70 billion parameters the state alone is 1.12 TB, which fills 35 devices of 32 GB before
  a single activation is stored.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Everything on screen is computed: the pairs, the shuffle, the softmax, the
cross-entropy, every gradient, every update, and nine genuine copies of the weights. What is
missing is structure — one character of context, 900 parameters, no hidden layers, no
tokenizer, a 499-character corpus. The **Learning rate** is fixed at 0.6 and has no control.

Read the stage panel with the timeline in mind: the Forward and Loss stages both describe the
**Checkpoint** you selected, while the counts, gradient norms and step totals describe the whole
run. And the loss is measured on the training text: there is no held-out set here, so nothing in
this lab separates learning from memorizing.

**Step one batch** is a replay, not a second run. It rebuilds the batch the seeded shuffle gave
the run at that step and applies the trainer's own arithmetic, so pressing it again gives the
same numbers. The update has no momentum, no clipping, and no weight decay.

**Where the memory goes** is multiplication. Nothing is allocated or measured, and it leaves out
activations on purpose, so its totals are a floor. The only real buffers here are the trainer's
own: 3,600 bytes of fp32 weights and 3,600 bytes of gradient, with no optimizer state under
plain SGD. Data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism and ZeRO sharding are described, not simulated.

**In real systems.** Large runs are counted in tokens seen rather than epochs, and the largest
make a single pass, so the epoch stops being a useful unit. The **dataloader** becomes a real
bottleneck.

Memory is more than the three bars. The ZeRO paper counts the activations of a 1.5 billion
parameter model at sequence length 1,000 and batch size 32 at about 60 GB, more than twice its 24
GB of model state, and about 8 GB with activation recomputation. It also names temporary buffers
and memory fragmentation as costs that appear at scale.

Optimizer state can be twice the size of the weights, or six times in mixed precision, so a
**checkpoint** that can restart a run must save the optimizer, the dataloader position, and the
random state, not just the parameters.
Production loss curves also throw spikes no smooth toy run reproduces; the standard response is
to roll back to the last checkpoint and skip the offending batches, which is only possible
because checkpoints exist.

## Going deeper

**Optimizer step.** Adam divides each coordinate by a running estimate of its own gradient
magnitude, which makes a single learning rate workable across parameters whose gradients differ
by orders of magnitude, at a cost of two extra numbers per weight.

**Batch.** A large effective batch is usually assembled by gradient accumulation: several
forward and backward passes, gradients summed, then one **optimizer step**. The loss curve
responds to the step count, not to how many passes fed it.

**Checkpoint.** At scale, saving is asynchronous and sharded across ranks, because a
synchronous write of a large optimizer state would stall every accelerator in the job.
Retention becomes policy: a few for restart, a sparse history for analysis, and a record of
which one a released model came from.

**Memory and parallelism.** The 16 bytes per parameter are needed somewhere, so large runs
divide them rather than shrink them. Data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism split the work: the
batch, each layer's matrices, or the stack of layers. Narayanan and colleagues show how the three
compose to reach thousands of GPUs (arXiv 2104.04473). Data parallelism on its own leaves each
worker with a full copy of the state.

**Sharding.** ZeRO partitions the optimizer state, gradients, and parameters across the workers
(arXiv 1910.02054), and PyTorch's FSDP, motivated by ZeRO, shards model parameters (arXiv
2304.11277). The per-parameter figures above follow the ZeRO paper, which gives 120 GB for 7.5
billion parameters, the number the card reproduces.

**Why this justifies LoRA.** Only parameters that are trained need a gradient and optimizer state.
Training a small adapter beside frozen weights removes most of those two rows. The LoRA paper
reports 10,000 times fewer trainable parameters and 3 times less GPU memory than fine-tuning
GPT-3 175B with Adam (arXiv 2106.09685).

QLoRA also stores the frozen weights in 4 bits, and its paper reports fine-tuning a 65 billion
parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU (arXiv 2305.14314). The **LoRA & adapters** lab trains one.

### Toy versus real

- **Updates.** This run takes 384 steps of 16 pairs. Llama 2 reports 2.0 trillion pretraining
  tokens at a global batch of 4 million tokens (Touvron et al., arXiv 2307.09288, Table 1), which
  is 2.0 trillion ÷ 4 million, about 500,000 updates of that size.
- **Step size.** The toy uses plain SGD at 0.6. Adam-style runs use far smaller rates, and they are
  not comparable with SGD's. GPT-3 lists learning rates from 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ at 125 million parameters
  to 0.6 × 10⁻⁴ at 175 billion, with batches of 0.5 to 3.2 million tokens (Brown et al., arXiv
  2005.14165, Table 2.1). Llama 2 lists 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ for its 7B and 13B models and 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ for
  its 34B and 70B models.
- **Memory.** The lab's whole model is 3.6 KB. At 16 bytes per parameter, 7.5 billion parameters
  is the card's 120 GB, before activations.
- **What the loop adds.** Both papers wrap these seven stages in a warmup-and-decay learning-rate
  schedule, gradient clipping at a norm of 1.0, and weight decay of 0.1.
