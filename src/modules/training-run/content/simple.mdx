## The big picture

Every chatbot you have used came from a **training run**. A program showed the model a huge pile of text and checked its guesses about what comes next. Then it nudged the model's numbers by a tiny amount. That cycle repeated many thousands of times. This module takes one training run apart so you can watch every piece of it work.

The model in this lab is tiny on purpose. It is a table of 900 numbers that guesses the next character from the one character before it. It learns from a short passage of notes about the weather in a harbor town. The passage is only 499 characters long. The model is so small that the lab retrains it from scratch whenever you change a training setting. So every number on the first four cards comes from a real run, not from a drawing.

The fifth card, about memory, is different. It finds its totals by multiplying, because this lab never trains a model with billions of numbers. A few things also stay fixed. The learning rate is a number that sets how big each adjustment is, and here it is always 0.6. Every score is measured on the same text the model learned from.

### Where you have met it

Every trained model came out of a loop like this one, including the huge models behind chatbots. Their runs are far bigger and save their progress more carefully. Many of them use a different adjusting rule called Adam. Even so, they go through the same seven stages in the same order. One published run, for a family of models called Llama 2, made about 500,000 adjustments. Each adjustment was based on about 4 million pieces of text.

Size is where the difference shows most. The largest model on this lab's memory card has 70 billion numbers. Training it with Adam needs room for those numbers plus several extra numbers about each one. That adds up to more than a trillion bytes. One byte is about the space a single letter of text takes. The card counts how many memory chips that would fill if each chip holds 32 billion bytes. It calls each chip a "device". The answer is 35 devices.

### Why it matters

Without this loop, a model's numbers would stay where they started. Its guesses would be no better than picking a character at random. The loop turns a pile of text into better guesses, one small correction at a time.

Knowing the loop also protects you from three common mistakes:

- You might compare two runs by how many times they read the text. But what counts is how many times they adjusted their numbers.
- You might trust a single adjustment because it helped the examples it was built from. But it can make everything else worse.
- You might plan a run around the size of the model alone. But the extra numbers kept for training take several times more memory than the model does.

Saved copies of the model, made along the way, are called **checkpoints**. They matter too, because they let you look inside a run partway through. They also let a run that crashes start again from the last copy instead of from nothing.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- the seven stages of a training loop, in order, and what each stage hands on to the next one;
- the difference between a **batch**, a **step** and an **epoch**. You will also be able to say why two runs with equal epochs can end up far apart;
- how to load a saved copy of the model from partway through a run. You will also be able to test what it had learned by then;
- what one single update changes, meaning which rows of the table move. You will also be able to say why it helps its own examples far more than the whole text;
- how to estimate a run's memory from three things. They are the model's size, the bytes used for each number, and the adjusting rule. You will also be able to say why big runs spread that memory across many chips.

## What it is

### The model: a sheet of guesses

The model works with 30 symbols. They are the space, the letters `a` to `z`, a full stop, and two angle brackets, `<` and `>`. A later lab is the only one that uses the brackets. The model's job is to guess which symbol comes next in a text. It does that with a grid of numbers that has one row for each symbol. You can think of the grid as a printed sheet of guesses.

Each row answers one question. If the last character was this one, how likely is each character to come next? A row holds 30 raw scores, one for every possible next character, and these scores are called **logits**. A higher logit means a likelier guess. To read a row, the model turns its 30 logits into 30 chances that add up to 100%. These chances are called **probabilities**. The rule that makes the switch is called softmax. It always gives the highest probability to the highest logit.

Thirty rows of 30 numbers makes 900 numbers, and those 900 numbers are the entire model. The numbers a model learns are called its **weights**, or its parameters. Training a model means changing them until its guesses improve.

Here is one row at work. After the text `the fo`, the model looks only at the last character, `o`, and reads the `o` row. At the end of the default run, that row gives `r` a probability of 23.5%. That happens because the training text has words like `harbor`, `morning`, `storm` and `before`, where `r` follows `o`.

A model that looks back only one character is called a **bigram** model. The name comes from "bi", meaning two, because it works with pairs of characters. It cannot use the word or the sentence that the character sits in. That is the main reason its guesses stay rough.

### The training loop as a study session

Training is one cycle repeated over and over. The lab's **Training loop stages** strip shows the seven stages of one trip around it. First, it helps to picture the whole cycle.

Picture the guess sheet on a desk, next to a big stack of flashcards made from the training text. Each card shows one character on the front. On the back is the character that came right after it. Making the deck is the job of the **Dataset** stage, and it happens once, before the first round. At the start of each pass through the deck, you shuffle it once. One round of study, which is one trip around the loop, then goes like this:

1. Deal yourself the next small handful of cards from the top of the shuffled deck. This is what the **Batch** stage does. When the deck runs out, that pass is over and you shuffle again.
2. For each card, find the row for the front character and read off the sheet's guesses. This is the **Forward** stage.
3. Flip each card over and mark how badly the sheet did on the real answer. This is the **Loss** stage.
4. Work out how each number on the sheet should change so that it does better next time. This is the **Backward** stage.
5. Rub out the old numbers on the sheet and pencil in the new ones. This is the **Optimizer step** stage.
6. Every so often, photocopy the whole sheet and file the copy away somewhere safe. This is the **Checkpoint** stage.

The picture stops matching in two places. A person studying these cards would use the whole word and its topic. The guess sheet only ever looks at the single character on the front. A person would also use judgement to fix the sheet. The training program works out every correction with exact arithmetic, the same way every time.

### The seven stages, one at a time

**Dataset** turns the text into flashcards, which the lab calls training pairs. Each pair is one character plus the character that followed it. The lab draws a space as `␣`. The words "the fog" have seven characters, counting the space. They give six pairs: `t→h`, `h→e`, `e→␣`, `␣→f`, `f→o` and `o→g`. Every character except the very last one starts a pair, so there is always one pair fewer than characters. That is why the stage shows **Characters** 499 and **Training pairs** 498. **Vocabulary** counts the 30 symbols.

**Batch** shuffles the pairs once at the start of every pass, then deals them out in handfuls, in order. A handful is called a **minibatch**, or a batch for short. The **Batch size** slider sets how many pairs each handful holds, and it starts at 16. The part of the training program that shuffles and deals is called the **dataloader**.

The shuffle matters because the text runs in order. Without it, a string of updates could all come from the sentence about winter. Those updates would drag the model toward that one passage. A shuffled handful is a fair mix of the whole text. The stage's readouts are **Batch size**, **Batches / epoch** and **Total steps**. A later part explains how they fit together.

**Forward** takes each pair in the handful and reads the row for its first character. It turns that row's 30 logits into 30 probabilities. Nothing in the model changes here, because this stage only reads. It is called the **forward pass**. Its readouts show the **Top prediction** and **Runner-up** after `the fo`. They also show **Row width**, which is 30 logits.

**Loss** checks each guess against the back of the card. It looks at the probability the model gave to the character that actually came next. It turns that probability into a penalty. It then averages the penalties over the handful, and that average is called the **cross-entropy**. A later part explains how the penalty is scored. The stage's readouts are **Loss at this checkpoint**, **Its perplexity** and the **Uniform baseline**.

**Backward** looks at each of the 900 weights in turn. It works out how the penalty would change if that weight went up a little. A positive number means that raising the weight would make the penalty worse. A negative number means that raising it would make the penalty smaller. That list of 900 numbers is called the **gradient**. The stage that works it out is called the **backward pass**. Like the forward pass, it only reads the weights and does not change anything yet.

**Optimizer step** is the stage that finally changes the model. It multiplies each weight's gradient by the **Learning rate** and subtracts the result from that weight. Subtracting moves every weight the opposite way to its gradient, which is the direction that lowers the penalty. The learning rate is fixed at 0.6 in this lab.

The learning rate sets how big each change is. A larger one makes bigger jumps, and a smaller one makes more careful ones. For example, picture a weight of 0.50 with a gradient of +0.10. The step takes away 0.6 × 0.10 = 0.06, so the weight becomes 0.44. If the gradient were −0.10 instead, taking away −0.06 would raise the weight to 0.56.

The rule that applies these changes is called the optimizer. One use of it is one **optimizer step**, or one **step** for short. This lab uses the plainest optimizer there is, called SGD. That is short for stochastic gradient descent, and "stochastic" means working from randomly chosen handfuls. The word "descent" fits if you picture the penalty as your height on a hill. Each step moves the weights a little way downhill for the handful it came from. As you will see later, that is not always downhill for the whole text.

**Checkpoint** saves a copy of all 900 weights so that you can come back to them later. At the default settings the run saves nine copies, and some settings save ten. One of them is the untrained model at step 0. The stage shows **Checkpoints**, **Selected step** and **Its loss**.

### Scoring a guess: cross-entropy and nats

The penalty follows one rule. The smaller the probability the model gave to the right answer, the bigger the penalty. Every time that probability is cut in half, the penalty grows by the same amount, about 0.69. Here is the ladder that rule builds, from a perfect guess down to a blind one:

- Giving the right character 100% costs nothing, because the model could not have done any better.
- Giving it 50% costs about 0.69, which is one halving away from being certain.
- Giving it 25% costs about 1.39, which is two halvings away from being certain.
- Keep halving to 12.5%, then 6.25%, then about 3.1%. After five halvings the cost is about 5 × 0.693, or 3.47. (The 0.69 above is 0.693 rounded off.)
- Giving it 1 in 30, about 3.3%, sits just above that 3.1%, so it costs a little less. The cost is 3.401, and that is what blind guessing scores.

The unit of this penalty is called the nat, so the lab reports the loss in **nats per token**. A token is one piece of text the model reads. In this lab every token is a single character. So nats per token means the average penalty for each character the lab scores. The Loss stage's title calls the penalty the "negative log-probability". That name comes from a part of maths you may meet later. The halving ladder is all you need to read the numbers this lab shows.

Lower is better, as in golf. A model that has learned nothing spreads its guesses evenly across the symbols. So it scores 3.401 on every character. That score is the **Uniform baseline**. The loss chart draws it as a dotted line labelled "knows nothing, ln 30". The ln is a button on a scientific calculator, and ln 30 works out to 3.401.

You can also read the ladder backwards, and the Loss stage mentions this rule. Whenever the loss falls by about 0.69 nats, the probability given to the right character has doubled. This holds on average across the characters scored.

The loss has a friendlier twin called **perplexity**, which turns the penalty back into a number of options. It asks a question. If the model chose evenly among some number of characters, how many would give this same score? Blind guessing among 30 symbols has a perplexity of 30, because that is exactly the situation the question describes. The default run ends at a loss of 2.277. That matches choosing evenly among 9.75 characters, so **Perplexity** reads 9.75.

You can check that with the ladder. Three halvings, or 1 in 8, cost about 2.08. Four halvings, or 1 in 16, cost about 2.77. A score of 2.277 sits between them. So it matches choosing among between 8 and 16 options, and the exact number is 9.75. In everyday terms, the trained model is as unsure as a quiz question with ten options instead of thirty.

**Better than chance by** says the same thing a third way. It measures how far the loss has come down from blind guessing toward a perfect zero. The default run brought the loss from 3.401 down to 2.277, a drop of 1.124. That drop is 33% of the full 3.401, so the readout shows 33%. It is not the share of guesses the model gets right. This lab does not show that measurement.

One more detail matters when you read these numbers, because the lab scores the loss in two different ways. During training, each handful's loss is averaged over its own pairs. Each point on the chart's thin, jagged line is the average of a few handfuls' losses. The Loss stage scores the checkpoint you picked on all 498 pairs instead. The whole body of training text is called the **corpus**, so the lab calls that score the whole-corpus loss.

### Calculating the gradient: the backward pass

For this kind of model and penalty, the backward pass follows a rule short enough to do by hand. Take the 30 logits in a row. For each one, write down the probability the model gave that character, as a decimal. Then subtract 1 from the probability of the character that actually came next. The 30 results are that row's part of the gradient.

The Backward stage draws this for one real pair from the words "the fog". The pair is `o` followed by `g`. At step 0, before any training, the model gives every character the same probability. That is 1 in 30, or about 0.033. So every other bar stands the same small height, +0.033, above the line. The bar for `g` drops below it. Only the `g` bar carries a printed number, which is 0.033 minus 1, or −0.967.

The strip has its own key. A bar above the line means that logit will be pushed down. A bar below it means it will be pushed up. That is because the optimizer step subtracts the gradient, so taking away −0.967 raises the score for `g`. Taking away each small +0.033 lowers every other score a little. Raising the right answer while lowering the others is the whole of what learning means for this row.

On the **Run controls and checkpoint timeline** card, move **Checkpoint** to the end of the run, step 384. The `g` bar still reads −0.957. That is 0.043 minus 1, because the trained model gives `g` only 4.3% after `o`. In this text, `r` follows `o` much more often than `g` does.

The strip shows one pair, but a real step uses a whole handful. Each pair's gradient is added into the row for its first character. Then the total is divided by the number of pairs in the handful. A full handful has 16. The last handful of each pass is shorter. It is divided by its own smaller count, so each of its pairs counts for more. A row that starts three pairs can get up to three times the push of a row with one. A row gets nothing at all unless at least one pair in the handful starts with its character.

The stage also measures how big a gradient is overall, as one number called the **gradient norm**. To work it out, square every number in the gradient, add up the squares, and take the square root. Take the `o→g` strip at step 0. Each of the 29 small bars of 0.033 squares to about 0.001. Together they add up to about 0.03. The `g` bar of −0.967 squares to about 0.94. The total is about 0.97, and its square root is about 0.98.

A bigger norm means the step built from that gradient changes the weights more. **This pair's gradient norm** measures the strip. **Last batch gradient norm** measures the run's final handful. **Peak batch norm in the run** shows the largest gradient norm that any handful in the run produced.

### Three ways to count: batch, step and epoch

A training run has three counts that sound alike but measure different things:

- A **batch** is one handful of pairs dealt out together. The **Batch size** slider sets how many pairs each handful holds.
- A **step** is one update to the weights, worked out from one batch. Steps are the only thing that actually changes the model.
- An **epoch** is one complete pass through all 498 pairs. The **Epochs** slider sets how many passes the run makes.

Plain division ties the three counts together. Take the default setting of 16 pairs per handful. One pass through 498 pairs needs 498 ÷ 16 = 31.125 handfuls. You cannot deal part of a handful, so that rounds up to 32. Thirty-one full handfuls use 496 pairs, which leaves 2 for a small last handful. That is why **Batches / epoch** reads 32. It is also why the last handful of each pass holds only those 2 pairs. The default run makes 12 passes, and 12 × 32 = 384 steps. That is what **Total steps** shows.

### Two cards that zoom in

Below the loop, two more cards show what the stages hide. **Step one batch** is the first. It replays one real optimizer step from the checkpoint you pick. It shows how many weights moved and lists the rows that moved most. The second card is **Where the memory goes**. It works out how much memory a training run needs for a model of any size. The next part explains what it adds up.

### What training has to hold in memory

Computers store every number as a pattern of 0s and 1s. Each 0 or 1 is called a **bit**. Eight bits make a **byte**, which is about the space one letter of plain text takes. A KB is a thousand bytes, and a GB is a billion bytes. A TB is a trillion bytes, which is the same as 1,000 GB.

To take a step, training has to keep three things in memory for every weight:

- The **weight** itself, which the forward pass reads every time it makes a guess.
- Its **gradient**, which is the number that the backward pass worked out for it.
- Its **optimizer state**, meaning any extra numbers the optimizer remembers about that weight from one step to the next.

How much each of these costs depends on two choices. The first is **Precision**, which means how many bytes each number gets. The full-size choice, fp32, gives every number 4 bytes, which is 32 bits. The letters "fp" stand for floating point, the normal way computers store numbers with decimals. The other choice on the card is **Mixed 16-bit**. It stores the weights and gradients in 2 bytes each, which speeds up the arithmetic.

More bytes let a number keep more digits, and that is why this choice is called precision. A 2-byte number keeps only about three digits or fewer. So a tiny update, like adding 0.0001 to 1.5, gets rounded away and lost. To fix this, the optimizer also keeps a full 4-byte copy of every weight. This is called the master copy, and tiny updates can build up there. Doing the arithmetic in 16-bit numbers while keeping that 4-byte master copy is called **mixed precision**.

The second choice is the **Optimizer**. SGD, the kind this lab trains with, remembers nothing at all between steps. The **Momentum** optimizer keeps one extra 4-byte number for each weight. That number is an average of the weight's recent gradients, and it updates after every step. It helps the weight keep moving in a steady direction. Adam keeps that same number, which the card's table also calls momentum. It adds a second number, called variance, which tracks how big the recent gradients have been.

Add up the bytes that one weight needs and multiply by the number of weights. That gives the memory training needs before anything else is counted. This multiplication is all that the **Where the memory goes** card does.

## Why it’s here

### Each stage feeds the next

The seven stages come in this order for a reason. Each one uses exactly what the stage before it produced. The dataset makes pairs, the batch picks a few of them, and the forward pass turns them into guesses. The loss scores those guesses. The backward pass turns the score into a gradient, and the optimizer step uses it to change the weights.

Every stage before the optimizer step only reads the weights. So the optimizer step is the only place where the model changes. Bigger runs change the scale, the optimizer and the saving, but they keep this order.

### Why handfuls, and not the whole text

Why not update the weights from all 498 pairs at once, or from one pair at a time? Using the whole text for every update would give the steadiest direction. But the model would change only once per pass. Using a single pair would change the model often. But every update would chase whatever that one pair happened to hold. A handful sits between these two extremes. The **Batch size** slider lets you slide along this trade-off and measure what happens.

### Counting passes is not counting learning

The loop is also where two counts get mixed up. Picture two friends studying the same deck of 498 flashcards. Both of them go through the whole deck 12 times. One friend stops to fix their notes after every 2 cards, which adds up to 2,988 rounds of fixes. The other fixes their notes only after every 64 cards, which adds up to 96. Both can honestly say they went through the deck 12 times. Yet one of them corrected their notes about 30 times as often as the other.

The lab works the same way. Twelve epochs at a **Batch size** of 2 make 2,988 steps. The same twelve epochs at 64 make only 96. The two runs end at very different losses. So **Total steps** is the honest number to compare between runs, and **Epochs** is not.

### One step is only a rough guess

A step is built from one handful, not from the whole text. So it is only a rough guess at what would help everywhere. The lab measured this in its 12-epoch runs at batch sizes 2, 16 and 64. Every step lowered the loss on its own handful. But some steps made the model worse on the rest of the text.

Picture a teacher who rewrites the class study guide after marking only two quizzes. The changes lean the guide toward the mistakes those two students made. They might confuse everyone else in the class, who never had those problems. Marking sixteen quizzes first would give a fairer picture of what the whole class needs.

Training has the same problem, but its steps point the right way on average. So the run still improves over many steps, even when single steps misfire. The picture stops matching in one way. A teacher can rewrite anything. A step only nudges the numbers a small, calculated amount toward the pairs in its handful.

### Checkpoints let you look back

Picture a long video game that only lets you save at the very end. If the power cuts out halfway, you lose everything. You can never go back to see how you played on an earlier level. A training run without checkpoints is like that, because it reports one number at the end and nothing else.

Saved copies fix both of those problems. You can load the model from partway through and test it, which shows you when and how it improved. In a large run, a crash far into the run would otherwise throw away all of that work. With a recent saved copy, the run loses only the steps taken since that copy was made.

### Memory is more than the model

The last problem is space. To take a step, training must hold the weights, the gradient and everything the optimizer remembers. It holds all of them at once. Under Adam, the weights themselves take up only a small part of that total. For a large model, the total is far more than one of the card's 32 GB devices can hold. So large runs have to plan carefully where every byte goes.

## How to play with it

### Walk the loop

Start with the **Training loop stages** strip and click all seven stages in order. Read the title, the explanation and the three readouts that each one prints. At **Backward**, look at the strip of 30 bars for the pair `o→g`. Find the one long bar, which belongs to `g`. The default run makes 12 passes at **Batch size** 16, which gives 32 **Batches / epoch** and 384 **Total steps**. Over on the **Measured training loss** card, **Final loss** reads 2.277 nats per token, and **Perplexity** reads 9.75.

On that same card, compare the three lines that the chart draws and notice how differently they behave. The thin line is the loss on handfuls. The thick line is the whole-corpus loss at each checkpoint. The dotted line is the "knows nothing" baseline at 3.401.

### Resize the batch

**Try this:** leave **Epochs** at 12 and drag **Batch size** from one end to the other. Watch **Total steps** and **Final loss** as you go. Before you drag, predict which end of the slider will finish with the lower loss. Decide why you expect it.

- At a batch size of 2, the run takes 2988 steps. It lands at a final loss of 1.877 nats.
- At a batch size of 16, the run takes 384 steps. It lands at a final loss of 2.277 nats.
- At a batch size of 64, the run takes only 96 steps. It lands at a final loss of 2.808 nats.

The text and the learning rate never changed between these runs. So the difference in final loss comes mainly from how many updates each run could make. Each small-batch update is also noisier, as the backfire experiment below shows.

Then set **Batch size** back to 16. Drag **Epochs** on its own to watch the same effect from the other side. One epoch ends at 3.166 nats, while sixty epochs reach 1.921.

### Reload a checkpoint

Set the run back to 12 epochs at **Batch size** 16. Then scrub **Checkpoint** from step 0 to step 384. Four separate readouts move together as you scrub, because all of them come from the same saved copy. The first is **Its loss** on the Checkpoint stage. The others are the big dot on the loss chart and the sample text. The last is the **Next character after "the fo" at this checkpoint** list. At step 0 every option in that list reads 3.3%. By step 384, `r` leads with 23.5%.

The stages card shows one stage at a time, so scrub once with the Checkpoint stage open. Then scrub again with the Backward stage open. Watch the `g` bar move from −0.967 at step 0 to −0.957 at step 384. It barely changes, because `g` is not what usually follows `o` in this text.

The sample is 96 characters long. The lab writes it by picking each next character at random, weighted by the model's probabilities. A setting called temperature, fixed at 0.8 here, tilts each pick a little toward the likelier characters. The picks also use a fixed seed, which is a starting number. It makes the computer's random choices come out the same every time. So any change between checkpoints comes from the weights and not from luck.

### Step one batch

Now set **Checkpoint** on the Run controls card to step 0 and scroll to **Step one batch**. Press the **Step one batch** button once. This applies the run's very first handful of 16 pairs to the untrained model. The pairs appear as small labels, such as `o→r` and `e→a`, drawn from all over the text. Then read the four readouts on the card, which report what that single update did:

- **Batch loss, before → after** goes from 3.401 to 3.362, measured only on the 16 pairs in that handful.
- **Batch gradient norm** reads 0.2567, which measures how big that handful's whole gradient is.
- **Whole-corpus loss, before → after** moves only from 3.401 to 3.396. It is measured across all 498 pairs in the text.
- **Weights changed** reads 270 of 900 in 9 rows. That is one row for each character that started a pair in the handful.

The count of 270 comes from whole rows, because every weight in a touched row moves. Softmax gives every character some probability, so all 30 logits in that row get part of the gradient. Nine rows of 30 weights is 270, and the other 630 weights stay exactly as they were.

Now look at the gap between the two losses. The step was built to fit those 16 pairs, so it helps them a lot. But 16 pairs are only about 3% of the 498. Their gain shrinks a lot once it is averaged over the whole text.

The nine rows that moved also hold 308 of the 498 pairs, and inside them the effect is mixed. About 78 pairs, including the handful's own, get a little better. They ask for the same next character as the handful. About 230 pairs that needed a different next character get slightly worse. The other 190 pairs sit in rows that did not move, so they do not change at all. That is why the whole-corpus average barely moves.

The table below the readouts lists up to eight rows with the biggest gradients. Its top row is `s`. That row started 3 of the 16 pairs. Two of them asked for a space, so their pushes add up. The space row also started 3 pairs. But they asked for `f`, `m` and `r`, so their pushes partly cancel and its gradient is smaller.

The **Target** column shows the next character a row was asked for most often. For `s` that is a space. **Target probability** shows the space's probability in the `s` row rising from 3.3% to 3.6%. On this first press nine rows moved, so the caption mentions one more row that changed less.

Press twice more, and a different set of rows moves each time. Each press applies the next handful the run used. **Back to the checkpoint** returns you to the saved copy whenever you want to start over.

### Watch a step backfire

Now set **Batch size** to 2 and put **Checkpoint** at step 1492. Press **Step one batch** again and again, up to its limit of 24 presses. **Batch loss** still falls on every press, but **Whole-corpus loss** rises on 17 of the first 24. For comparison, at **Batch size** 16, 24 presses from the checkpoint at step 192 never raise it.

Here is how a tiny handful can backfire, using a made-up example. Picture a handful of two that holds the pair `e→a`, from a word like "sea". The step raises `a` in the `e` row. The probabilities in a row always add up to 100%. So every other choice in that row loses some probability. That includes the space after `e`, which the text needs after every "the". The two pairs in the handful improve. But the many other pairs that need that space get a little worse. (Your 24 presses hold other `e` pairs, such as `e→v` and `e→r`. They make the same kind of trade.)

Every step makes this kind of trade, so why does it backfire so often here? By step 1492, the `e` row already gives `a` about 7%. That is close to its share of the text. The share is 4 of the 55 pairs that start with `e`. A full push for one `e→a` pair overshoots that share. In a handful of two, that one pair also drives half the step. That is eight times the share it would get in a handful of 16.

### Count the memory

Last, scroll to **Where the memory goes**. It starts at **Model size** 7.5 billion, with **Precision** set to **Mixed 16-bit** and **Optimizer** set to **Adam**. Here is what a single weight costs at those settings:

- The weight itself takes 2 bytes, because Mixed 16-bit stores it in 16 bits.
- Its gradient also takes 2 bytes, because the backward pass stores it in the same 16-bit format.
- The **optimizer state** takes 12 bytes. That is the 4-byte master copy, plus 4 for momentum and 4 for variance.

That adds up to 16 bytes, which is what **Bytes per parameter** shows. Multiply 16 bytes by 7.5 billion weights and you get 120 billion bytes, or 120 GB. Weights and gradients take 15 GB each, and the optimizer state takes the other 90 GB. That is six times as much as the weights, which is what **Optimizer state ÷ weights** reports.

A device here means one chip with its own memory. Big runs train on chips like this, and the graphics chips called GPUs are one kind. The card uses the word "state" for everything training holds for each weight. That means the weight, its gradient and its optimizer state together. The card assumes each device holds 32 GB. Then 120 GB ÷ 32 GB is 3.75, which rounds up to 4 whole devices. So **32 GB devices for state alone** reads 4 for this model.

Now switch **Precision** to fp32 and predict the new total before you look. It stays at 120 GB. The weight and its gradient each grow from 2 bytes to 4, which adds 4 bytes. But fp32 needs no separate master copy, which takes 4 away. Each weight now costs 4 + 4 + 8 bytes, where the 8 is Adam's two 4-byte numbers. The total is still 16. Only the split between the parts changes.

**A common mix-up:** "16-bit" sounds as if it should halve the memory. But it does not shrink what Adam needs at all. In both precisions each weight costs 16 bytes. Mixed precision only moves bytes. The weight and the gradient each shrink from 4 bytes to 2. The saved bytes go into the master copy in the optimizer state. Its gain is faster arithmetic, not a smaller total on this card.

Then switch **Optimizer** to SGD. Momentum and variance disappear, so the total falls to 8 bytes per weight, or 60 GB. With fp32 still selected, the optimizer-state segment vanishes from the bar completely. Switch back to **Mixed 16-bit** and that segment returns at 30 GB, half the bar. SGD in mixed precision still needs the 4-byte master copy. That copy is twice the size of the 2-byte weight, so each weight costs 2 + 2 + 4 = 8 bytes.

Finally, switch **Optimizer** back to Adam and set **Model size** to 70 billion. Watch the total climb to 1.12 TB while the device readout jumps to 35.

## What to notice

- The thin batch-loss line on the chart stays jagged while the thick whole-corpus line drops smoothly. Each point on the thin line averages only a few handfuls of 16 pairs. So it wobbles with whichever pairs they happened to hold.
- The last handful of each pass holds only 2 pairs, so it is the noisiest of all. The small ticks under the chart mark where each pass ends. That is every 32 steps at the default setting.
- Scrub through the nine checkpoints. The loss reads 3.401, 3.068, 2.816, 2.641, 2.530, 2.438, 2.374, 2.320 and then 2.277. The first 48 steps cut it by about 0.33, but the last 48 cut it by only about 0.04. Each stretch of steps buys less than the one before, right from the start.
- **Rows updated** on the Optimizer step stage reads 26 / 30. Four symbols never start a pair in this text. Those four are `j`, `x` and the two angle brackets, so their rows never change at all.
- **Peak batch norm in the run** reads 0.749 at **Batch size** 16. That peak comes from one of the 2-pair handfuls left over at the end of a pass. No full handful of 16 reaches even half of it. At a batch size of 2, every handful has 2 pairs, and the peak reaches about 1.09.
- Averaging over more pairs makes each gradient shorter and steadier. Each pair supplies only one sixteenth of a 16-pair gradient. The pairs push on different rows and characters, so their pushes add up only partly. Pairs in the same row that ask for different next characters also partly cancel.
- **Better than chance by** reads 33%. That is the same fact as **Perplexity** falling from 30 options to 9.75.
- The Forward stage prints "the fo" but uses only the final `o`, because a bigram throws the rest away. **Top prediction** is `r` at 23.5%, from words like `harbor`, `morning`, `storm` and `before`. At step 0, every option reads 3.3%.
- The sample uses the same seed at every checkpoint. So early checkpoints print almost the same string, and later ones still share their opening letters. Any change comes from the weights, not from luck. Even at step 384, most of the sample is gibberish. It has real fragments inside, such as `the`, `and` and `thin`.
- One sample is not a measurement. A 96-character sample can look worse at a later checkpoint even while the loss fell. Trust **Its loss** over your eyes when the two seem to disagree.
- One step helps its own handful far more than it helps the whole text. At step 0 the gains are 0.039 against 0.005 nats. Only rows that started a pair in the handful move, so 630 of the 900 weights are not touched. The nine rows that did move still cover most of the text.
- A step can make the whole text worse, and the smaller the batch, the more often it happens. At **Batch size** 2, the whole-corpus loss rose on 1,174 of the 2,988 steps in the 12-epoch run. At 16 it rose on 5 of 384 steps, and at 64 it rose on none of the 96. The loss on each step's own handful fell every single time. So the run improves on average, not at every step.
- **Mixed 16-bit** does not shrink what **Adam** needs, which is 16 bytes per weight in both precisions. In mixed precision the optimizer state is six times the weights, and in fp32 it is twice the weights.
- At 70 billion weights, the weights, gradients and optimizer state together come to 1.12 TB. That fills 35 devices of 32 GB before a single activation is stored. The optimizer state on its own is 840 GB of that. An activation is an in-between result. A large model works it out during the forward pass and keeps it for the backward pass.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Everything on screen is computed for real. That includes the pairs, the shuffle, the probabilities and the loss. It also includes every gradient, every update and every saved copy of the weights. The run saves nine copies at the default settings and ten at some others. What is missing is structure, because the model sees one character of context and has only 900 numbers. It has no hidden layers. Those are the extra stages of number-crunching that real models run between reading and guessing. It also has no tokenizer to split text into word pieces. Its training text is only 499 characters long. The **Learning rate** is stuck at 0.6, and there is no control to change it.

Keep in mind that the stages do not all describe the same moment in the run. The Forward and Loss stages, and the Backward strip, describe the **Checkpoint** you picked. The counts, the batch gradient norms and the step totals describe the whole finished run.

The loss is also measured on the training text itself. There is no held-out text, meaning text the model never trained on. So nothing in this lab can tell real learning apart from memorising these exact sentences. The next lab, **Building a dataset**, adds that check.

**Step one batch** is a replay, not a second run. It rebuilds the handful that the run's seeded shuffle dealt at that step. It uses the trainer's own arithmetic, so pressing it again always gives the same numbers. Like the run itself, its update leaves out three extras that big runs use. They are momentum, gradient clipping and weight decay. The "Toy versus real" list at the end of the lesson explains the last two. A press past the final checkpoint deals the next pass's handful. That is a step the saved run never took, and the card says so.

The Backward strip shows one pair's gradient at a saved checkpoint. That is not the same as the full handful's gradient that the optimizer applied at that step.

**Where the memory goes** is multiplication, since nothing is set aside or measured. Activations are left out on purpose. So each total is the least a run could need, not a forecast. The only real memory in this lab belongs to its own trainer. It holds 3,600 bytes of weights and 3,600 bytes of gradient, with no optimizer state under plain SGD. This lab describes how a run splits across devices but does not simulate it. So the card never shows how the bytes would be divided between chips.

More epochs is not the same as more learning. Epochs count passes through the text, and only steps change the weights. Twelve epochs at **Batch size** 64 make 96 updates. Twelve at 2 make 2988 and end nearly a whole nat lower. Small batches win here by updating more often per pass, but each update comes from fewer pairs. That is why, at **Batch size** 2, the whole-corpus loss rose on 1,174 of the 2,988 steps.

### Where real training runs break

Real runs differ in ways this one cannot show, starting with how they count. They count tokens seen rather than epochs. The biggest make only one pass through their text, so the epoch stops meaning much. The **dataloader** also becomes a real bottleneck. That means it can turn into the slow part that holds the whole run back.

A run that restarts must carry on exactly as before, and Adam's remembered numbers shape every step it takes. So a **checkpoint** that can restart a run must save the optimizer state as well as the weights. That optimizer state can be twice the size of the weights, or six times in mixed precision. The checkpoint also needs the dataloader's place in the text and the state of the random shuffles.

Memory is also more than the three parts of the card's bar. A research paper called ZeRO points this out, and the card's numbers follow that paper. It looks at a model with 1.5 billion weights, reading 32 examples at a time, each 1,000 tokens long. In that case the activations take about 60 GB. That is more than twice the 24 GB of weights, gradients and optimizer state. A trick works out the activations again during the backward pass instead of storing them. That cuts them to about 8 GB. The paper also names short-term scratch space and wasted gaps in memory as costs that appear at large scale.

Real loss curves also throw sudden spikes that a small, smooth toy run like this one never produces. The usual fix is to roll back to the last checkpoint and skip the handfuls that caused the spike. That only works if someone saved a checkpoint.

## Going deeper

### Adam and the optimizer step

Adam divides each weight's gradient by a running estimate of how large that weight's gradients usually are. That lets one learning rate work for weights whose gradients differ enormously in size. The cost is two extra numbers to remember for every weight.

### Building big batches

Big batches are usually built by adding up. The run does several forward and backward passes and adds their gradients together. Then it takes one **optimizer step**. This is called gradient accumulation. The loss curve responds to the number of steps. It does not respond to how many smaller handfuls were added up for each one.

### Saving checkpoints at scale

At large scale, saving happens in the background and is split across many machines. Stopping everything to write out a huge optimizer state would leave every machine in the run sitting idle. Deciding how many checkpoints to keep becomes a policy. A few are kept for restarting, and a thin history is kept for study. A record also shows which one a released model came from.

### Memory and parallelism

The 16 bytes per weight have to live somewhere, so big runs divide them up rather than shrink them. Real models are built from many layers stacked one after another. Each layer has its own grids of numbers. There are three main ways to split the work across chips:

- Data parallelism gives each chip a different handful of examples to work through.
- Tensor parallelism splits each layer's grids of numbers into pieces held by different chips.
- Pipeline parallelism gives each chip a different slice of the stack of layers, like stations on an assembly line.

A paper by Narayanan and colleagues shows how the three combine to train on thousands of GPUs. Data parallelism on its own still leaves every chip holding a full copy of the 16 bytes per weight.

### Sharding

**Sharding** is the fix for that wasted space, because it stops every chip from storing the same full copy. Picture a group project. Nobody prints the whole binder of notes. Each member keeps one chapter and borrows the others when needed. Sharding works the same way. Each chip keeps only a slice of the weights, gradients and optimizer state. It fetches the rest when it needs them. The comparison stops matching in one place. A group can pass paper around slowly, but chips must trade their slices again and again during every step.

A method called ZeRO splits all three across the chips. FSDP, a feature of the PyTorch training toolkit inspired by ZeRO, does the same kind of splitting. The card's 120 GB for 7.5 billion weights is the ZeRO paper's own example.

### Why this matters for LoRA

Fine-tuning means training an already trained model further on new examples. Only the weights that are being trained need a gradient and optimizer state. So you can train a small add-on, called an adapter, beside weights that stay frozen, meaning unchanged. That removes most of those two costs. The LoRA paper compares this with fine-tuning all of GPT-3, a model with 175 billion weights, using Adam. It reports 10,000 times fewer trained numbers and 3 times less GPU memory.

A related method, QLoRA, also stores the frozen weights in 4 bits each. Its paper reports fine-tuning a model with 65 billion weights on a single 48 GB GPU. The **LoRA & adapters** lab trains one of these adapters.

### Toy versus real

- **Updates:** This lab's default run takes 384 steps of 16 pairs each. That is tiny next to a published run. Llama 2 reports 2.0 trillion training tokens at 4 million tokens per batch. That works out to about 500,000 updates.
- **Step size:** This lab uses plain SGD at a learning rate of 0.6. Runs that use Adam pick far smaller rates. An Adam rate and an SGD rate work differently, so you cannot compare them directly. GPT-3's published rates run from 0.0006 for its smallest model down to 0.00006 for its largest. Llama 2 used 0.0003 for its two smaller models, and half that for its two largest.
- **Memory:** The lab's whole model is 3.6 KB, which is 900 weights at 4 bytes each. At 16 bytes per weight, 7.5 billion weights take the card's 120 GB, before any activations are counted.
- **What the loop adds:** The GPT-3 and Llama 2 papers both wrap these seven stages in three extras. This lab leaves them out. A warmup-and-decay schedule starts the learning rate small, raises it, and then lowers it again over the run. Gradient clipping shrinks any gradient whose norm is over 1.0 back down to a length of 1.0. Weight decay, set to 0.1, pulls every weight a little toward zero at each step.
