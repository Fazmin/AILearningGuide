## A word that needs another word

Read this sentence from the lab: “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired.” Who was
tired? You knew at once that `it` means the animal and not the street, and you worked that out by
linking one word to another word several places earlier.

A language model, the kind of program behind a chatbot, has to make links like this for every word
it reads. To guess what comes next, a word often needs information from somewhere else in the text,
such as the name that a pronoun stands for. It might instead need the opening bracket that a closing
bracket belongs to, or the pattern that a few earlier examples set up. The part of the model that
fetches that information is called **attention**.

Here is the plan: first you will build attention yourself, one piece at a time, where each new piece
fixes a problem with the piece before it. Then you will turn around and question the machine you
built, the way a careful scientist questions a result that looks too neat.

Hold on to one warning, because it comes back near the end. In the small model in this lab, the line
from `it` to `animal` is always thin. No part of the model makes it the strongest link, or gives
`animal` even 10 percent of the attention of `it`, even though you made that connection instantly.
Working out why that happens is part of the lesson, and it is a good reminder that these pictures
need careful reading.

### Why it is worth building

Most of today’s chat models are the same kind of model as the tiny one in this lab. They read text
from left to right, and they learned by guessing the next piece of text over and over. In a model
like this, attention is the only step that lets one word take information from another. Without it,
every word would be stuck with what it knew about itself and its place in the line, and its guesses
about what comes next would be far worse.

Attention also changed how models are trained, because it lets a model practise guessing at every
spot in a long text at the same time. You will see later why that is a big reason it became
practical to train very large models at all.

By the end you’ll be able to:

- tell apart the query, the key and the value that every word makes, and explain why the query and
  key decide where information comes from while the value carries it;
- read a picture of attention without reading too much into it, since a thick line shows where
  information could flow, not why the model answered as it did;
- tell a part of the model that follows the text’s content from one that always looks a fixed number
  of characters back, spaces included;
- explain what the one-way rule changes, since it stops a word from reading later words, and how
  models that read in both directions differ from this one;
- say why each match score gets divided by 8 before it becomes a share, why 8 is the right number
  here, and what goes wrong when that step is skipped.

### What the lab runs

The lab runs a real, very small model with two layers, which was trained on the plays of
Shakespeare. With the model’s own settings, the numbers you see are measured straight from that
model, rather than taken from a cartoon of how attention might work.

Three switches let you break the machinery on purpose and watch what changes. When you flip one, or
type in your own numbers, the lab works out new results from the model’s numbers. You are then

A few things are fixed or made up rather than measured, starting with the example sentences, which

purpose, from short strings repeated over and over. If the model fails to load, the lab shows
stand-in numbers that mean nothing, and it tells you so.

### Follow one word

The lab opens on the same sentence, with `it` already chosen as the **query**, the word whose search
you are following. Before any theory, look at the **Model attention routing diagram** card. Curved
lines called arcs fan out from `it` to the other words, and a thicker arc means `it` takes more from
that word.

**Pause and predict:** which word do you expect to get the thickest arc from `it`? Make your guess,
then read the **strongest route** in the readout strip under the arcs.

In Layer 2 head 2, where the lab starts, the strongest route goes to `because`, not to `animal`. A
few other things are worth seeing while you are here. Later words get only a faint, thin line,
because the model may not read ahead. The query has no arc to itself, so its weight on itself shows
only in the bar under its chip. The strip also names the **inspected key** and how much of the row
lands **on spaces**, a number you will learn to read with care.

The picture already disagrees with your instinct, so we need to know exactly what an arc is, and the
best way to learn that is to build one.

## Build it yourself, one fix at a time

Inside the model, every character of your sentence is stored as a long list of numbers that
describes it. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**, a bit like a player’s stats in a
sports game, where each number rates one skill. The difference is that nobody decided what the
model’s numbers stand for. The model settled on them itself during **training**, a long practice
period of guessing the next character over and over and nudging all its numbers a little after each
round.

This model reads characters, meaning letters, spaces and punctuation marks, so every character gets
its own vector. The lab’s **Words** view adds characters up into words, and it counts the space in
front of a word as part of that word. A word’s row is the row of its last character, so the row for
`it` is really the row for its `t`, the first spot to see the whole word. Every character that row
can see belongs to exactly one word, so a row’s word totals still add up to exactly 100 percent.

Now picture that `t` before attention runs, when its vector says only something like “I am a `t`,
and I sit at this seat in the line”. Nothing in it says that an animal appeared earlier, that
someone is tired, or even that the letter before it was an `i`. It is like opening a group chat
where the newest message says “she said yes!!” and nothing else, so you scroll back up to find out
who “she” is. Our job is to give every word a way to scroll back.

### First try: average everything

Let’s start with the simplest thing that could work. The word `it` needs information from earlier
words, so what if it took the average of all of them? Picture what `it` would receive: equal parts
`the`, `animal`, `did`, `not`, `cross`, `the`, `street` and `because`. The animal is in there, but
so is the street, at exactly the same volume.

Averaging can’t choose, because it gives every earlier word the same say whether or not that word
helps. The one useful word is drowned out by all the others. We need a way for `it` to say what it
is looking for and to pick out the words that offer it.

So here is our first fix: give every earlier word a score for how well it matches what `it` is
looking for.

### Score the matches

How do you score a match between two lists of numbers? The model uses a calculation called the **dot
product**, which takes two steps. First you multiply the two lists position by position, and then
you add up all of the answers. Try it with four numbers in each list, where the searching list is
(2, −1, 0, 3) and the other list is (1, 2, 5, 1):

- first position: 2 × 1 = 2
- second position: −1 × 2 = −2
- third position: 0 × 5 = 0
- fourth position: 3 × 1 = 3

Adding the four answers gives a score of 3, since the −2 from the second position cancels the 2 from
the first and leaves the 3 from the fourth. Whenever the two numbers in a position have the same
sign, they raise the score, and whenever their signs are opposite, they lower it. A high score
therefore means that the two lists agree across many positions, which is what a good match looks
like in numbers.

In the lab, the searching list is called the **query** and the list it is checked against is called
the **key**. For now, treat both as the word’s own vector, and a few steps from now you will see why
the model makes them different. The real lists are 64 numbers long, so the **Score** line in the
**Query key value inspector** card adds up 64 products rather than four. It shows the total next to
`q · k`, which is how the lab writes the dot product of a query and a key.

In one sentence: a dot product turns “how well do these two lists agree?” into a single number. But
a score on its own can be any size, even negative, and you can’t blend words by amounts like that,
so we need to turn the scores into shares.

### Turn scores into shares

We want shares that are all positive and add up to 100 percent. The tool that does this is called
**softmax**, and the lab writes its formula as `exp(s) ÷ Σ exp(s)`. In that formula, `s` is one
score, and `exp(s)` means a special number, about 2.718, raised to the power `s`. The Greek letter
`Σ`, called sigma, means “add this up for every character the query can see”. Put into words,
softmax turns every score into a positive number and then divides each one by the total of them all.

Try it with three scores, 2, 1 and 0, and turn each one into a positive number first:

- The score 2 becomes 2.718 × 2.718, which is about 7.39.
- The score 1 becomes 2.718 itself, which rounds to 2.72.
- The score 0 becomes exactly 1, because any number raised to the power 0 equals 1.

Those three numbers add up to about 11.11, and dividing by that total gives each score its share.
The top score gets 7.39 ÷ 11.11, which is about 66.5 percent of the row. The middle score gets about
24.5 percent and the bottom score about 9 percent, so together the three shares make 100 percent.
Hold on to these three shares, because they come back.

Negative scores work too, and real scores are often negative, so it helps to know that a power below
zero means dividing instead of multiplying. The score −1 becomes 1 ÷ 2.718, which is about 0.37, and
the score −2 becomes 1 ÷ 7.39, which is about 0.14. The more negative the score, the closer the
result gets to zero, but it never reaches zero. Scores with decimals land in between, so a score of
1.5 becomes about 4.48.

Two features of softmax matter for everything that follows. First, every share is positive, because
2.718 raised to any power is above zero, so even a poor match gets a sliver of the row. Second,
softmax cares a great deal about the gaps between scores, because each step of 1 multiplies rather
than adds. A gap of 1 makes one share about 2.7 times the other, and a gap of 2 makes it about 2.7 ×
2.7, or 7.4, times the other. A gap of 8 means multiplying by 2.7 eight times over, which comes to
about 3,000.

These shares are called **attention weights**, and each one tells you how much of one word ends up
in the query’s blend. You have now done by hand what the model does, so let’s check that the model
really does it the same way.

### Check the arithmetic for one pair

Click a cell in the grid of the **Attention matrix** card, where darker cells mean bigger attention
weights. Each cell’s row is a query word and its column is a key word, so one cell describes one
pair of words. Clicking sets both words at once and jumps the stages to `Score`, so the **Query key
value inspector** card shows the arithmetic for that pair. Now step to `Softmax`.

**Pause and predict:** the **Softmax** line redoes one pair’s share from all 64 numbers of the query
and keys, then prints the model’s own figure beside it. How close do you expect the two to be?

They agree to within about one part in a million. Next to the line, the lab shows the largest gap
between its sums and the model’s own figures anywhere in the row, written in a short form such as
2.4e-7. That form means 0.00000024, and it is how you know you are looking at the real arithmetic
rather than a drawing of it.

The strip in the inspector draws the whole row twice. The top band shows each score as a bar that
rises above its zero line when the score is positive and drops below it when the score is negative.
The bottom band shows each share as a bar, and shares are never negative. The band titles write the
head dimension as `d`, so `√d` means √64, which is 8, a number you will meet again soon.

Two readouts look alike but measure different things. **Selected score** is the score for one pair
of characters, the last character of the query word and the last character of the inspected word.
**Selected weight** is the share for the whole inspected word, all its characters plus the space in
front, which is the number the grid cell shows. A single character can get a tiny share while its
word’s total is large, because the weight is spread across the word’s characters. If you prefer
reading numbers to reading shades, open **View weights as text** under the grid.

### Shares compete

Every share in a row is worked out by dividing by the same total, which raises a question.
**Pause and predict:** if you push up the score for one word, what happens to the shares of all the
other words?

To try it, use the `Q` and `K` chips in the inspector, which are editable and show the first four
numbers of the query and of the inspected word’s key. Type a different number into one of the four
`K` boxes, then watch the arcs, the bars under the word chips, or the list under **View weights as
text**.

Every share in the row moves, not only the one for the word you edited. When one score changes, the
total changes, and every share is divided by that total. That is why a row always adds up to 100
percent and why routes compete: one connection can only grow stronger if others in its row grow
weaker.

The strip is a poor place to watch this, because it draws every share bar as a fraction of the
tallest one. The other bars there keep their heights unless the bar you changed is, or becomes, the
tallest.

Which way a score moves depends on the query too. Raising one number in `K` changes the score by the
matching number in `Q` times the amount you added. So where that `Q` number is positive, raising the
`K` number raises the score, and where it is negative, raising the `K` number lowers it. For
example, if the matching `Q` number is 0.5 and you add 2 to the `K` number, the score rises by 0.5 ×
2, which is 1. **Selected score** shows the change after a division by 8 that you will meet shortly,
so it rises by 0.125.

Only the first four of the 64 numbers can be edited, while the other 60 stay as the model made them.
Press **Match the model vectors** to switch back to the model’s own numbers. The lab remembers what
you typed, though, so if you type in a box again, your earlier changes come back with it.

So the shares are a fixed budget that the words compete for, and now we can spend that budget.

### Blend by the shares

With shares in hand, we can finally do what averaging did badly: blend the words, with each one
counting exactly as much as its share. A blend like this is called a **weighted average**, and you
have probably met one in a class grade. Say a test counts for 75 percent of your grade and a quiz
for the other 25 percent, and you score 80 on the test and 100 on the quiz. Three quarters of 80 is
60 and one quarter of 100 is 25, so your grade works out to 85.

Attention does the same calculation with whole lists of numbers instead of single marks. Suppose the
query gives 75 percent of its weight to a list holding (4, 0) and 25 percent to a list holding (0,
8). Three quarters of the first is (3, 0) and one quarter of the second is (0, 2), so adding them
position by position gives a blend of (3, 2). The lab shows the first four numbers of the real
blend, out of 64, in the **Mixed value** chip.

The **Mix length** readout turns the whole blend into one number that says how big its numbers are
overall. To get it, square every number in the blend, add up the squares, and take the square root.
For the blend (3, 2), that is √(9 + 4), which is about 3.6. The readout is 0 only when every number
in the blend is 0.

Score, share, blend: that is a working recipe, but look at what we blended. It was the same vector
that we scored the match against, and that turns out to be a problem.

### One vector can’t do two jobs

So far each word uses one vector for two very different jobs. It has to be found by the right
searches, and once it is found, it has to hand over something useful for the guess. What makes a
word a good match is not the same as what is worth handing over, so one list of numbers is stuck
trying to do both.

The fix is to split it. From each word’s starting vector, the model makes a **key**, shaped by
training for being matched, and a separate **value**, shaped for being useful once it arrives. There
is a third job as well. The word doing the searching needs to describe what it wants, which is not
the same as what it is, so it also makes a **query**. All three are vectors, made from the same
starting vector by three separate sets of learned numbers, like three separate recipes.

Picture a classroom where every word of the sentence sits at its own desk, in order from left to
right. Each desk holds three items:

- a **search slip** that describes what this word is looking for, which is its query;
- a **label** pinned to the front of the desk that advertises what this word can offer, which is its
  key;
- a **packet** in the drawer that holds the information that actually gets handed over, which is its
  value.

Every word compares its search slip with the label on every desk it is allowed to see, including its
own. Good matches score high, the scores become shares, and the word receives a blend of everyone’s
packets, each counting as much as its share. Here is the whole split in one line: **Q and K choose
who, V is what.**

This is where the desk picture stops matching. Real slips and labels have no words written on them,
because they are lists of learned numbers whose meaning nobody chose. A strong match between a slip
and a label tells you nothing about what is in the packet that gets handed over. Real desks would
also have seat numbers, while attention gets them only because the model adds them on purpose, a gap
we will come to in a moment.

### Zero the values

If the split is real, you should be able to empty every packet without moving a single route. Set
**Value vectors (V)** to *Zero all*, which replaces every value with a list of zeros.

**Pause and predict:** what will happen to the arcs and the strip, and what will happen to the
**Mixed value** chip?

Every attention weight stays exactly where it was, while the **Mixed value** chip drops to all zeros
and **Mix length** falls to 0. The query and the keys chose the routes and the values travelled
along them, so emptying the values changes what arrives but not where it comes from.

For a finer test, click a dark cell in the grid, so the inspected word has a large share. Then
switch the control between *Model* and *Zero word*, which empties only the inspected word’s values,
including the value for the space in front of it. The **Mixed value** numbers and **Mix length**
change while the shares stay put, because the blend loses exactly that word’s part and nothing else.
When you are finished, set the control back to *Model*.

That is the cleanest proof of the split: choosing the route and carrying the content are separate
jobs done by separate lists.

### What the recipe can’t see: order

Look back at the desk story and you will find a gap, because nothing on a label says which desk it
sits at. Shuffle the desks and every match keeps the same score, so attention on its own could not
tell “the dog bit the man” from “the man bit the dog”. The one-way rule you will meet soon leaks a
little information about order. A word near the start can see only a few words while a word near the
end can see many, and **The transformer block** lab measures how much.

Models close the gap by giving every desk its seat number in some form, so that position becomes
part of the calculation. Any way of doing this is called a **positional encoding**, and four kinds
are common:

- **Learned seat numbers** (used by GPT-2, an older text-writing model, and by this lab’s model):
  the model learns one list of numbers for each seat. It adds that list to the vector of whatever
  character sits there. There is no list for a seat past the last one, so the model has a fixed
  longest input, which is 64 characters here.
- **Wave-pattern seat numbers** (used by the Transformer, the 2017 design that first built a model
  around attention): each seat gets a fixed pattern made from waves, with nothing to learn. Models
  that use this kind tend to work poorly on texts longer than the ones they trained on.
- **A distance penalty** (ALiBi): instead of seat numbers, the match score between two words is
  lowered more the farther apart they are, so nearby words tend to win.
- **A twist** (RoPE, used by Llama models and many other recent ones): each slip and label is turned
  by an amount set by its seat, like turning a clock hand. The seat part of the match then depends
  only on how far apart the two words sit, not on where in the text they are. **Modern attention at
  scale** shows how it works.

You can see the first kind at work in this model’s heads. A head can prefer to look a fixed distance
back only if its slips and labels carry the seat number. Some Layer 1 heads do exactly that, looking
1, 2 or 3 characters back on every text this lesson tries, with 66 to 100 percent of their weight
there. If the learned seat numbers are wiped to zero, that share falls to under 6 percent.

So order is not built into attention: it has to be added, and this model adds it with learned seat
numbers.

### The five buttons are the five steps you just built

You have now built the whole recipe, and the lab names its steps on five buttons in the first card:
`Query`, `Keys`, `Score`, `Softmax` and `Mix`. Press them in order and the **Query key value
inspector** highlights each step in turn:

- `Query`: the word you clicked makes its search slip, and the `Q` chip shows its first four
  numbers.
- `Keys`: every word the query is allowed to see shows its label. The `K` chip shows the key of the
  inspected word, which you choose by clicking a cell in the **Attention matrix**.
- `Score`: the query is compared with every key by a dot product on the **Score** line. Then every
  score in the row is divided by 8 on the **Scale** line, written `score ÷ √64`, because each list
  is 64 numbers long and √64 is 8.
- `Softmax`: the scaled scores become shares that are all positive and add up to 100 percent.
- `Mix`: the values are blended by those shares into the **Mixed value**.

Wait, though: we never built that divide-by-8 step, and the recipe worked without it, so why is it
there? Hold that question, because it gets its own section after this one.

### Many heads, many questions

One complete set of the three recipes, with its own learned numbers, is called an **attention
head**. One head asks one kind of question of the sentence, but a word may need several things at
once. So this model runs four heads side by side, and each one makes its own slips, labels and
packets from the same text. Running several heads like this, so that one sentence can be routed in
different ways at once, is called **multi-head attention**. Each head’s lists are 64 numbers long,
and that length is called the **head dimension**.

The model also has two layers, one after the other, so attention happens twice on the way through.
Layer 1 works on the character vectors as they come in and passes updated vectors on to Layer 2,
whose four heads then do their own fetching. That makes eight heads in all, and you choose one with
the **Layer** buttons and the **Attention head** slider at the top of the **Query key value
inspector** card. The grid of weights for one head in one layer is called an **attention pattern**,
and the **Attention matrix** card draws it.

**Pause and predict:** if you keep `it` as the query and change heads, will every head send `it` to
the same place?

**Try this:** Keep `it` as the query and move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads
with **Layer** set to *Layer 1*, then do the same with *Layer 2*. The sentence never changes, so
watch only the arcs and the **strongest route**.

The routes change from head to head: in Layer 1, head 1 sends most of the weight of `it` to
`because`. Heads 2, 3 and 4 pile it onto `it` itself, for a reason you will uncover later. In Layer
2, the four heads’ strongest routes go to `it`, `because`, `cross` and `it`. Each head has its own
learned numbers, so each one routes the sentence its own way, though two heads can learn almost the
same habit. Layer 2 also looks different from Layer 1 because its heads read vectors that Layer 1
has already changed.

That is one sentence with eight different sets of routes. Now that you have built the machine, let’s
break it, starting with the step we skipped.

## A bug you’d hit: scores that are too loud

Remember the divide-by-8 step on the **Scale** line? The recipe you built worked without it, so
let’s find out what goes wrong if you leave it out.

Go back to the three scores from earlier, 2, 1 and 0, which gave shares of about 66.5, 24.5 and 9
percent. Now make them 8 times bigger, so they become 16, 8 and 0. **Pause and predict:** do the
shares stay spread out and become a little more lopsided, or does one score take nearly everything?

One score takes nearly everything: the top score now gets more than 99.9 percent of the row, which
leaves almost nothing for the other two. That is because softmax multiplies by about 2.7 for every
step of gap. When one character takes 99 percent or more of a row, the lab calls that row
**saturated**. A saturated row has stopped blending, because it hands over one value and ignores
almost everything else.

So scores that are too loud break the blend, but why would real scores be 8 times too loud in the
first place?

### Why divide by 8?

Picture a game where you flip a coin 64 times, adding 1 to your total for every heads and taking
away 1 for every tails. Most of the flips cancel each other out, so your total almost never ends up
anywhere near 64. In about three games out of four it lands within 8 of zero, on one side or the
other, and it rarely lands more than 16 away.

Now change the number of flips: with 16 flips the total usually lands within about 4 of zero, while
with 256 flips it usually lands within about 16. How far from zero the total usually lands is called
its spread, and four times as many flips only doubles the spread. That is the square-root pattern:
√16 = 4, √64 = 8 and √256 = 16.

A dot product behaves the same way when the numbers in both lists are about the size of 1 and their
signs look random. Each of the 64 positions adds a product of about size 1, sometimes positive and
sometimes negative, like one coin flip. So the score usually lands within about 8 of zero, while
lists of 256 numbers would give scores that usually land within about 16. Dividing by the square
root of the head dimension brings the typical score back to a size of about 1, however long the
lists are.

A dot product divided by the square root of the head dimension is called a **scaled dot product**.
That pattern holds for lists of random-looking numbers, but a trained head can still learn bigger
scores on purpose. Hold on to that, because you will see it in a moment.

The division also has a history, since it comes from the original Transformer paper, by Vaswani and
colleagues in 2017. The authors suspected that large scores push softmax into a corner where one
share is nearly everything. In that corner, nudging a score barely moves any share, so training gets
almost no signal about which way to adjust it, and learning would become very slow.

### Switch off the scaling

Now test the idea on the real model, on the pronoun sentence in Layer 2 head 2, where the lab
starts, using two readouts. **Largest weight** is the biggest share any single character gets in the
current row. **Saturated rows** counts the rows in this head’s whole grid where one character gets
99 percent or more.

**Pause and predict:** with the division on, how many rows do you expect to be saturated? Now guess
again for when you set **Score scaling** to *No scaling*, so the scores reaching softmax are 8 times
bigger.

With the division, no row is saturated, but 26 of 55 rows are saturated without it. The count is out
of 55 because the sentence has 56 characters and the first one is left out. That first character can
only see itself, so it always gives itself 100 percent and would tell you nothing.

Now set the control back to *÷ √64* and move to Layer 1 head 3 or head 4. **Saturated rows** still
counts most of the 55 rows, even with the division on. These are the trained heads from a moment
ago, which pile onto the previous character on purpose. That is how they put 99.9 percent of `it` on
the `i` before its `t`. So the division does not ban saturation, since all it does is stop scores
from growing because the lists are long. The trouble it prevents is saturation that nobody asked
for.

Be honest about what you have seen: the switch shows the corner but not the slow learning, which
would appear only in training, and the lab never trains the model. Leave the control on *÷ √64*, so
the next experiments use the model’s own setting.

In one sentence: without the division, long lists make the scores loud and rows saturate, and with
it, a row saturates only where a head has learned to.

## How a model could cheat, and the rule that stops it

So far, a word has only ever looked at itself and the words before it. Why not the words after it
too? To answer that, you need to know how this model practised. It learned by guessing each next
character in a 64-character piece of text, called a window, and it saw the whole window at once.

**Pause and predict:** if you were training this model to guess the next character, and every spot
could see the whole window, what is the laziest way to score perfectly?

It could read the answer, because the next character is sitting right there in the window, one seat
to the right. When the model reads “the cat”, the spot holding `c` has to guess that `a` comes next,
and without a rule the `c` could read that `a` and copy it. Picture a practice quiz where the answer
to each question is printed right underneath it. You could score 100 percent by copying the line
below each question, and you would learn nothing at all.

### The one-way rule

The fix is a rule: a word may read itself and the words before it, but it may never read the words
after it. This rule is called the **causal mask**. It is a mask because it covers up later words,
and causal because each word may only use what came before it, as a cause comes before its effect.

The mask works by changing the scores before softmax runs, setting the score for every later
character to minus infinity, written −∞, which is lower than any number. That makes it the one
exception to “every share is positive”, because 2.718 raised to −∞ shrinks all the way to zero, so a
later character’s share is exactly zero. The lab draws those blocked spots with diagonal stripes,
called hatching, and the **Scale** line says `masked` if you inspect one.

Because each row can only reach back, the grid in the **Attention matrix** card is a triangle rather
than a square. The diagonal runs from the top-left to the bottom-right corner, where each character
meets itself, and the corner above it, where later characters would sit, stays empty. The diagonal
also lets you read distances, which you will need later. In the Characters view, a stripe of dark
cells one cell to the left of the diagonal means each character looks 1 back. A stripe 7 cells to
the left of it means each character looks 7 back.

Here is the clever part. Without the mask, the only fair way to practise would be 64 separate runs
of the model, each showing only the text up to the spot it has to guess. With the mask, the model
takes in the whole window in one run, and every spot still has to guess its next character without
seeing it. One run then gives 64 fair guesses to learn from. Attention lets those 64 guesses be
worked out side by side, and the mask keeps them honest.

That was a big change from older language models, called recurrent models, read text strictly in
order and squeezed everything they had read into a single memory of fixed size. That is a bit like
remembering a whole book by rewriting one index card after every page. The details get crowded out,
and you cannot start page 50 until you have finished page 49. The powerful chips used to train
models are built to do huge numbers of small calculations side by side. Attention finally let
language models use those chips fully, and that is a large part of why training on huge amounts of
text became practical.

In one sentence: the mask hides every answer from the spot that has to guess it, and that is what
makes all-at-once practice fair.

### Lift the one-way rule

What would the heads do if they were allowed to look ahead? In the **Attention matrix** card, set
**Mask** to *Bidirectional*, so every character may read the whole sentence. The triangle fills into
a square, and the readout strip adds two new numbers: the share **on later characters** and the
share **on the next character**.

**Pause and predict:** with `it` as the query, will every head rush to the later characters, or only
some? And will any head go for the very next character, which is the answer?

It depends a great deal on the head: Layer 1 head 3 moves only 0.1 percent of the weight of `it` to
later characters, and head 4 only 0.2 percent. Layer 2 head 4, by contrast, moves 90.2 percent of
its weight there. Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put about a third of the weight of `it` on the very next
character, 33.8 and 30.6 percent. That next character is exactly the answer the model practises
guessing, so this is the lazy trick from the start of this section, caught in the act.

Look below the diagonal too, where every share shrinks by the same factor. The earlier characters’
scores have not changed, so softmax keeps their sizes compared with each other and divides them all
by a bigger total that now includes the later characters. For example, suppose 40 percent of a row
moves to later characters, leaving 60 percent for the earlier ones. Every earlier share is then
multiplied by 0.6, so a share that was 50 percent becomes 30 percent.

Set **Mask** back to *Causal* before you go on, so the grid shows the model as it was trained.

### Two families: encoders and decoder-only models

The one-way rule is a design choice, not a law of nature, and keeping it or dropping it divides
language models into families:

- A **decoder-only (causal) language model** keeps the rule and learns to guess the next piece of
  text at every position. This lab’s model is one, and so are most of today’s chat models, which
  write by guessing one piece, adding it to the end, and running again. The name comes from an older
  design with two halves, an encoder that read the input and a decoder that wrote the output; a
  decoder-only model keeps the writing half.
- An **encoder** drops the rule, so every word may look both backward and forward, and its grid is a
  full square. Reading in both directions like this is called bidirectional attention, and encoders
  use it to understand a whole text at once. That suits jobs such as sorting emails into spam and
  not spam, or finding the pages that best match a search.

An encoder cannot practise by guessing the next word, because every position could see its own
answer, so it needs a different practice task. A **masked language model** is an encoder that
practises by covering some words in a text with a blank, then guessing them from the words on both
sides. A well-known model called BERT works this way, and this kind of practice suits understanding
text better than writing it.

Be careful with that name, because “masked” here means words hidden behind a blank, not the causal
mask, and a masked language model has no causal mask at all.

One honest caveat about the switch you flipped: the lab’s *Bidirectional* setting is an experiment
on a decoder-only model, not an encoder. A real encoder is trained from the start with every
position visible, so it learns different queries and keys from any that this model ever made.

## Reading the evidence like a sceptic

You have built attention and broken it on purpose, so now it is time to settle the warning from the
start: no head in this model links `it` to `animal`. Is the model failing, or are we reading the
picture wrong? Here are three sceptic’s habits: check where the weight lands, move the content to
see if the route follows, and change the pattern to tell copying from habit.

### A route that is not what it looks like

Press the **Pronoun sentence** button in the Examples row of the first card. It reloads the example
and resets the mask, the scaling and the values to the model’s own settings. You saw earlier that
Layer 1 heads 2, 3 and 4 pile the weight of `it` onto the word `it` itself.

**Pause and predict:** why would a head give a word almost all of its attention to itself? What do
you think it is really looking at?

Set **Layer** to *Layer 1*, move the **Attention head** slider to head 3, and switch **Matrix view**
to *Characters*. You will find a dark stripe one step below the diagonal, which means that every
character looks at the character immediately before it. The lab reads `it` at its last letter, the
`t`, and the character right before that `t` is the `i` inside `it`. That is why heads 3 and 4 put
99.9 percent of `it` on the word `it`. They are not thinking about the word at all, only about the
letter one seat back.

So a thick arc, or a full bar under a chip, shows where information could come from. It is not a
sign that the model has worked out what `it` refers to.

Then why does no head, in either layer, reach for `animal`? One likely reason is that the lab reads
`it` at its `t`, and the only guess that spot has to make is the next character. Here that is a
space, and the spelling of `it` already makes a space a good guess without any help from `animal`.
No head in this model shows a sign of linking `it` to `animal`, and these pictures cannot tell you
how a large chatbot handles pronouns.

The same care applies to the **on spaces** readout. With `it` as the query, Layer 1 head 2 puts 99.1
percent on spaces, but only because that head always looks two characters back. Two characters back
from the `t` in `it` is the space in front of the word. For `because`, the same head puts 1.3
percent on spaces, so a big On spaces number does not mean a head is interested in spaces. It can be
a side effect of where a word ends and how far back the head looks.

That is the first habit: before you believe a word-level picture, check which character the weight
really sits on.

### Move the content, see if the route follows

Here is a route that looks much more like understanding. Press **Repeated name**, which loads “Mark
Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” with the second `Mark` as the query. Set **Layer** to *Layer 2*
and move the **Attention head** slider to head 3. That head sends 92.7 percent of the second
`Mark`’s weight to `Jones`, the word that came right after the first `Mark` in the sentence.

That is a sensible place to look, because at the second `Mark` the model has to guess what comes
next, and last time `Mark` appeared, `Jones` came right after it. Switch **Matrix view** to
*Characters* to see exactly where the weight lands. Almost all of it sits on one character, the
space right after the first `Mark`, which the Words view counts as part of `Jones`. That is also why
**On spaces** reads close to 100 percent.

Under the one-way rule, that space has seen only “Mark ” so far, so its packet cannot hold the name
`Jones` at all. The head points at the spot that came right after `Mark` last time, which is the
spot it would copy from to guess the next character this time.

A sceptic should still ask whether the head is following the name, or always looks a set distance
back with the name happening to sit there. The way to find out is to move the name and see whether
the route moves with it.

**Pause and predict:** press **Name moved earlier**, which loads “Anna Blake met Mark Jones and then
Mark”. The space after the earlier `Mark` is now 19 characters back instead of 34. Will the head
still find `Jones`?

It does, and the share on `Jones` stays high, at 97.2 percent. A head that always looked a set
distance back would have lost its target when the name moved. This route follows the content
instead, because wherever the earlier `Mark` sits, the head’s weight lands right after it.

To push the test further, press **Repeated name** again, then click at the end of the **Sentence**
box at the top of the first card. Type a space and then Smith, and click the new `Smith` chip to
make it the query, keeping Layer 2 head 3. There is no earlier `Smith` for the head to match, and no
word receives even 20 percent of its attention.

Other names can come out differently, because the head matches characters, not whole names. `Clark`
ends in the same `k` as `Mark`, so the head lands mostly on the space after the second `Mark`, which
the Words view counts as part of `Clark`. A name such as `Davis` makes the head park much of its
weight on the first character of the sentence, which the Words view credits to the first `Mark`.
That looks like what the last section calls an attention sink, a parking spot for spare weight, not
a link to `Mark`.

That is the second habit: move the content, and a route that moves with it is reading content, while
a route that stays put is reading distance. Even the `Jones` route is only a lead worth testing,
though, not proof that the model used it.

### Copying or habit? Change the period

Moving one name is a single test, and a sharper one changes a whole repeating pattern, as a game
shows. Picture someone reading out a long string of letters that repeats, while two friends race to
call out each next letter. The first friend looks back along the string for the last place the
current letter appeared and calls out the letter that came after it. The second friend has a habit
instead, and always calls out the letter from 7 places back.

If the string repeats every 8 letters, both friends are right every time once the pattern has gone
round once. Say the letters are ABCDEFGH repeated and you have heard the second C, so the first
friend finds the earlier C and calls out the D that followed it. Counting 7 back from the second C
goes B, A, H, G, F, E, D, so the habit friend also calls out D, which is exactly what comes next.

Now change the string so it repeats every 9 letters, as ABCDEFGHI. Counting 7 back from the second C
now lands on E, which is wrong, while the first friend still finds the earlier C and calls out D.
Only the first friend keeps winning, so changing the repeat length is how you tell the two apart.

The game stops matching the model in two places. A head does not call out anything itself, because
it only puts weight on a position, and the rest of the model turns that into a guess. Also, nobody
can call the letters the first time through the string, since there is no earlier copy to look back
at yet.

Press **Repeats every 8**, which loads eight random letters repeated four times and switches to the
Characters view. In Layer 2, heads 2 and 3 show stripes 7, 15 and 23 characters back, putting 94 and
88 percent of their weight on earlier copies of the next character. The next character itself is
still hidden by the one-way rule, so the heads find where that same letter appeared before. By the
last copy of the string, the pattern has already appeared three times, which is why there are three
stripes.

**Pause and predict:** when you press **Repeats every 9**, will these two heads act like the first
friend or the second? Will their stripes stay at 7, 15 and 23 characters back, or move?

The stripes move to 8, 17 and 26 characters back, and head 2 now puts 95 percent on earlier copies
of the next character while head 3 puts 89 percent. Less than 1 percent is left at the old
distances, so these heads are the first friend: they look for what followed last time, wherever it
is.

Now look at Layer 1, whose four heads keep the same stripes at both repeat lengths. Head 1 puts
about two thirds of its weight 3 characters back, head 2 looks 2 back, and heads 3 and 4 look 1
back. Those are fixed-distance habits, like the second friend’s, and only the learned seat numbers
make them possible. Apart from Layer 2 heads 2 and 3, none of the heads puts more than 11 percent on
earlier copies of the next character at either repeat length.

A head that copies like this is called an **induction head**. A head that looks at the character
right before the current one, like Layer 1 heads 3 and 4, is called a **previous-token head**. Here
a token means one piece of text, which in this model is a single character.

In the usual explanation of a two-layer model, the two kinds of head work as a pair. Take “Mark
Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” again, where a previous-token head in Layer 1 looks one
character back from every spot. So the space after the first `Mark` picks up a note that says,
roughly, “the character before me was `k`”. When the second `Mark` reaches its last letter, `k`, the
induction head’s slip asks for a spot whose previous character was `k`. The space after the first
`Mark` carries exactly that label, so the head reads it, and that space is where `Jones` begins.

That is the third habit: change the pattern and watch whether the stripes follow it. The pairing
story is neat, but this lab has not proven it, and the next section explains why.

## What an attention picture can’t tell you

So what does a thick arc actually prove? Less than it seems, because a thick arc means only that one
position’s value was given a large weight in one word’s blend, in one head of one layer. It does not
tell you what that value contained, whether the next step kept it, or whether a later layer
cancelled it. It also does not tell you whether the final answer depended on it at all. The `Jones`
route showed this already, since the space it points at could not even hold the name.

A route is a place where information could flow, not evidence that it mattered. Showing that a part
of the model matters takes an experiment that changes the model on purpose and then measures the
effect. One such experiment is switching a head off and checking whether the model’s predictions get
worse, and tests like that are the subject of **Interpretability II: circuits**.

### What this model demonstrates, and what it does not

The model was given a test built so that a fixed-distance habit would fail it. A random string is
repeated once, at a repeat length the model never practised on: 11, 13, 17 or 19 characters. The
test then measures how much weight each head puts on the character right after the earlier match.

To read the results, you need one more idea, which is how the model’s error gets measured. When the
model guesses, it does not pick one character but gives each of its 66 symbols a chance, such as 60
percent for `e` and 10 percent for `a`. The error is a score called **loss**, which is higher when
the model gave the character that actually comes next only a small chance.

Lower loss is better, and it is measured in a unit called a nat. To turn nats into something you can
picture, raise 2.718 to the power of the loss. The answer is roughly how many equally likely
characters the model is choosing among. A blind guess that gives all 66 symbols an equal chance
scores about 4.19 nats, because 2.718 raised to 4.19 is about 66.

Here is what the test shows:

- On random strings, Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 83 and 81 percent of their weight on the character
  after the earlier match. A head with a fixed 7-back habit would score near zero here, because none
  of these repeat lengths puts that character 7 back.
- Every other head stays under 6 percent on random strings, whatever the repeat length.
- The copying pays off in the guesses. On random strings, the loss on the first copy is about 4.3, a
  little worse than a blind guess. On the second copy it falls to between 0.13 and 0.25, so the
  model is nearly certain of each next character.
- Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 put 99 percent or more of their weight on the previous character, which a
  Layer 2 copying head would need from the layer below.

**How sure is this?** The 83 and 81 percent come from the worst of the four repeat lengths, so the
other lengths score at least as well. On pieces of held-out Shakespeare, each repeated once in the
same way and never seen in practice, the same two heads put 40 and 51 percent on that character.
There the loss falls from about 1.6 or 1.7 on the first copy to between 0.39 and 0.53 on the second.
The first-copy loss on random strings is most likely a little worse than a blind guess because the
model still bets on characters that are common in Shakespeare.

Here is what the test does not show, and these gaps matter as much as the results:

- It does not show **the wiring**, because nobody traced which Layer 1 head feeds which Layer 2
  head. Switching a head off is a blunt test, because it removes everything the head adds to the
  model rather than one connection. Switching off Layer 2 head 3 makes the loss on the second copy
  more than ten times higher. If the copying heads relied on a previous-token head, switching that
  head off should hurt clearly more than switching off a head that does something else. Instead,
  switching off Layer 1 head 2, which is not a previous-token head, raises the loss about as much as
  switching off Layer 1 head 4, which is one.
- It says nothing about **big models**, because this model has about 1.4 million learned numbers,
  called **parameters**, reads characters, and learned from one author’s plays plus the made-up
  repeats. It cannot tell you how big chatbots pick up a pattern from a few examples you type into
  your message, which researchers call in-context learning.
- It does not explain **the other heads**: Layer 1 head 1 and Layer 2 heads 1 and 4 are shown in the
  lab but not explained.
- It is not **a full explanation**, because an attention pattern on its own is evidence about
  routing and nothing more.

### Limits of this lab

The lab runs a real, very small trained model with two layers of four heads, and all of its
parameters were set during training. The badge at the top of the first card starts with `ONNX model`
once the model is running, since ONNX is the file format it is stored in. While the model loads, the
badge says `Loading teaching model…`, and if loading fails, it says
`Model unavailable · inspectable fallback`. In both cases the lab shows stand-in values from a fixed
wave pattern with no learned meaning, and a note in the **Attention matrix** card tells you so.

The model practised on Shakespeare for 8,000 rounds of training, called steps, and each step gave it
a batch of windows to guess its way through. Its practice text was the 1.0 million characters in the
first 90 percent of the plays, and over all those steps it went through that text about 23 times.
The last 10 percent was kept aside, so the model could be tested on lines it had never seen.

On that held-out last tenth, its loss is 1.54 nats per character, compared with 4.19 for a blind
guess. Since 2.718 raised to 1.54 is about 4.7, that is roughly as unsure as picking among 5 equally
likely characters instead of 66. That is far better than guessing, though still a long way from
certain.

About 30 percent of the practice windows were not ordinary Shakespeare text. In every batch of 64
windows, 19 were made-up repeats: a short string, sometimes random symbols and sometimes a snippet
of the plays, repeated over and over. They were put in on purpose, so the model would meet lots of
repetition and have a reason to learn copying. Each string repeated every 4 to 31 characters, and
repeats of 11, 13, 17 and 19 characters were left out so they could be used for the test above.

The three switches are experiments run on a model that has already finished training, which limits
what they can show:

- **Mask** lifts the rule for one head of a model that was trained with the rule on, so that head
  never practised scoring later characters. In Layer 2, its slips and labels are still built from a
  Layer 1 that kept the rule, and the rest of the model does not change.
- **Score scaling** turns off the division for one head of a model that learned with it on. So it
  shows that head’s rows saturating, not what training without the division would have produced.
- **Value vectors (V)** changes the lab’s blend for one head, but it does not change the model’s
  answer, because the emptied blend never runs through the rest of the model.

Input stops at 64 characters, which is the window length the model trained on. The Words view adds
up characters, while big chat models read pieces of words called tokens, so the lab’s word totals
are not real tokens. The editable chips show only the first four of 64 numbers, and once you edit
them, the shares come from your numbers rather than the model’s.

### In real systems

Full attention compares every position with every other position, so the work grows with the square
of the text’s length. Double the length of a text and there are about four times as many pairs to
score. Make it ten times longer and there are about a hundred times as many, which adds up fast.
That one fact drives most of the engineering behind models that read very long texts.

**A common mix-up:** Attention in a model is not the same as human focus or attention span. A thick
arc or dark cell is also not proof that one word caused the model’s answer. Even a link that follows
the content, like `Mark` to `Jones`, is one weight in a weighted average worked out with dot
products, not a reason or a decision.

## Where this goes next

You have built one head and questioned it closely, and the attention inside a large chat model is
the same recipe with a few more parts around it. Each of those parts connects to another lab in this
course.

### Many heads, one output

Real models run several attention heads side by side, which is what the **Attention head** slider
lets you step through here. Their blends are joined end to end and passed through one more learned
mixing step, called the **output projection**. That step decides how the fetched information gets
written back into the model. The result is added into a running record that each position carries
through the model, called the residual stream, which **The transformer block** follows.

Splitting the work into heads costs little: this model’s full vectors are 256 numbers long, split
into four heads of 64 numbers each. Four heads of 64 cost about the same arithmetic as one head of
256, and in return you get four separate ways to route information.

### Positions in bigger models

Most current models use the twist (RoPE) rather than this lab’s learned seat numbers. Stretching
such a model to handle longer inputs works by adjusting how far each seat turns the slips and
labels, and **Modern attention at scale** covers both ideas.

### Saving work while writing

When a model writes text one piece at a time, the keys and values of earlier pieces never change,
since under the one-way rule they never read the new ones. They can therefore be saved and reused,
instead of being recalculated for every new piece of text. That saved store is the subject of
**Inference & KV cache**, where K stands for keys and V stands for values.

**Grouped-query attention** makes that store smaller by letting several query heads share one set of
keys and values. It gives up a small amount of quality in return for a large saving in memory. Other
methods, such as FlashAttention, work out exactly the same softmax in small blocks, so the full grid
of scores never has to be stored at once.

### Induction heads in research

The copying heads you met are called induction heads, and Olsson and colleagues studied them in 2022.
Their paper is titled *In-context Learning and Induction Heads*, and it describes such a head by two
behaviours. First, the head looks back for the spot right after an earlier copy of the current
token. Second, it copies, which makes the token found there more likely to come next.

In a two-layer model, they found an induction head paired with a previous-token head, the same two
kinds you saw here. In larger models, they saw a sudden change early in training, when induction
heads formed while the models got better at in-context learning. They describe their evidence as
preliminary and indirect. It suggests that these heads drive most of that learning, but it is early
work and does not prove the link directly.

### Attention sinks

Some heads park most of their attention on the very first token whenever they have nothing useful to
fetch from anywhere else. A position that soaks up weight like this is called an **attention sink**,
and you may have seen something like it if you typed a name such as `Davis` earlier. This matters in
practice, because deleting that position from the saved store of keys and values makes the model’s
output worse.
