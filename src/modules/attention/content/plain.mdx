## The big picture

Read this sentence from the lab: “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired.” You
knew right away that `it` means the animal and not the street, because you linked one word to
another word several places earlier in the sentence.

A language model, the kind of program behind a chatbot, has to make links like this for every word
it reads. To guess what comes next, a word often needs information from somewhere else in the text,
such as the name that a pronoun stands for. It might instead need the opening bracket that a closing
bracket belongs to, or the pattern that a few earlier examples set up. The part of the model that
fetches that information is called **attention**, and this module shows you how it works, one small
step at a time.

In a single breath: every word sends out a search request. It compares that request with what each
earlier word has to offer, which gives each one a match score. The word then takes in a blend of
information from the words that match best. None of these links is wired in ahead of time, because the model
works them out fresh for every sentence it reads. That means the same word can draw on different
neighbours in different sentences.

### Where you have met it

Most of today’s chat models are the same kind of model as the tiny one in this lab. They read text
in one direction, from left to right, and they learned by guessing the next piece of text over and
over again. Suppose a chatbot keeps track of who “she” is in your message, or carries on a list in
the same format that you started. In both cases, attention is the part of the model that lets each
word reach back to earlier words for the information it needs.

### Why it matters

In a model like this one, attention is the only step that lets one word take information from
another. Without it, every word would be stuck with what it knew about itself and its place in the
line. The model’s guesses about the next word would then be far worse, because what helps a guess
usually sits somewhere else in the text.

Attention also changed the way models are trained, and that matters nearly as much. It lets a model
practise guessing what comes next at every spot in a long text at the same time. Older models had to
work through the text one spot after another. That is a big reason it became practical to train very
large models at all.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- the search slip, label and packet that every word makes, and why the slip and label decide where
  information comes from while the packet carries it;
- how to read a picture of attention carefully, since a thick line shows where information could
  flow, not why the model answered as it did;
- how to tell a part of the model that follows the text’s content from one that always looks a fixed
  number of characters back, spaces included;
- what the one-way rule changes, since it stops a word from reading later words, and how models that
  read in both directions differ from this one;
- why each match score gets divided by 8 before it becomes a share, why 8 is the right number here,
  and what goes wrong when that step is skipped.

## What it is

### Words as lists of numbers

Inside the model, every character of your sentence is stored as a long list of numbers that
describes it. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**, a bit like a player’s stats in a
sports game, where each number rates one skill. The model’s vectors differ in one important way:
nobody decided what each number stands for.

The model settled on its numbers itself during **training**, a long practice period in which it
guessed the next character over and over. After each round of guesses, it nudged all its numbers a
little, so that its next guesses would come out better.

This model reads characters, meaning letters, spaces and punctuation marks, rather than whole words,
so every character gets its own vector. For most of this lesson we will talk about words, since the
lab’s main views group characters that way, and return to characters when they matter.

### Three things on every desk

Picture a classroom where every word of the sentence sits at its own desk, in order from left to
right. Each desk holds three different items, and each item has an official name that the lab and
the glossary use:

- A **search slip** describes what this word is looking for in the rest of the sentence, and its
  official name is the **query**.
- A **label** pinned to the front of the desk advertises what this word can offer to others, and its
  official name is the **key**.
- A **packet** in the drawer holds the information that actually gets handed over, and its official
  name is the **value**.

Every word, every time, compares its search slip with the label on every desk it is allowed to see,
including its own desk. Labels that match the slip well receive a high score, while poor matches
receive a low one. Those scores are then turned into shares that add up to 100 percent. Finally, the
word receives a blend of everyone’s packets, in which each packet counts exactly as much as its
share.

All three items are vectors, and the model calculates all three from the same starting vector, using
three separate sets of learned numbers. Each set works like a separate recipe, so a word’s label can
differ from its packet. Training shaped the label for being matched and the packet for being useful
once it arrives.

Keep this split of jobs in mind for the rest of the lesson, because the lab keeps returning to it.
The query and the key decide **who gets read**, while the value decides **what gets read**. The lab
has a switch that tests this split directly, which you will try in How to play with it.

### The five stages

The lab walks through one word’s search in five stages, named on the buttons in the first card:
`Query`, `Keys`, `Score`, `Softmax` and `Mix`. Each stage below gives the idea first and then a
small example that you can check by hand.

In the `Query` stage, you choose the word whose search you want to follow by clicking its chip in
the **Model attention routing diagram** card. A chip is one of the small boxes that hold each word,
and the word you click becomes the query. The `Q` chip in the **Query key value inspector** card
then displays the first four numbers of that word’s query.

In the `Keys` stage, every word that the query is allowed to see presents its key, the label on the
front of its desk. The `K` chip displays the first four numbers of the key belonging to whichever
word you are inspecting, which the lab calls the inspected key. You choose which word to inspect by
clicking a cell in the **Attention matrix** grid, as How to play with it shows.

In the `Score` stage, the query is compared with every key using a calculation called the **dot
product**, which takes two steps. First you multiply the two lists position by position, and then
you add up all of the answers. Here is a short example with four numbers in each list, matching the
four numbers that each chip displays. Suppose the query is (2, −1, 0, 3) and the key is (1, 2, 5,
1):

- first position: 2 × 1 = 2
- second position: −1 × 2 = −2
- third position: 0 × 5 = 0
- fourth position: 3 × 1 = 3

Adding the four answers gives a score of 3, since the −2 from the second position cancels the 2 from
the first and leaves the 3 from the fourth. Whenever the two numbers in a position have the same
sign, they increase the score, and whenever their signs are opposite, they decrease it. A high score
therefore means that the two lists tend to agree across many positions, which is what a good match
looks like in numbers.

The inspector shows its working as a stack of labelled lines, like the lines of a worked answer in a
maths test. The real lists in this model are 64 numbers long, so the **Score** line adds up 64
products rather than four. It shows the total next to `q · k`, which is how the lab writes the dot
product of a query and a key.

The `Score` stage does one more thing, and it does it to the query’s whole row of scores, meaning
its score with every character it can see. Every score is divided by 8, because each list in this
model is 64 numbers long and 8 is the square root of 64. The **Scale** line shows this step as
`score ÷ √64`, and it gets its own section below, because it matters more than it looks.

In the `Softmax` stage, the scaled scores are turned into shares that are all positive and add up to
100 percent. The tool that does this is called **softmax**, and the lab writes its formula as
`exp(s) ÷ Σ exp(s)`. In that formula, `s` is one score, and `exp(s)` means a special number, about
2.718, raised to the power `s`. The Greek letter `Σ`, called sigma, means “add this up for every
character the query can see”. Put into words, softmax turns every score into a positive number and
then divides each one by the total of them all.

Try it with three scores, 2, 1 and 0, and turn each one into a positive number first:

- The score 2 becomes 2.718 × 2.718, which is about 7.39.
- The score 1 becomes 2.718 itself, which rounds to 2.72.
- The score 0 becomes exactly 1, because any number raised to the power 0 equals 1.

Those three numbers add up to about 11.11, and dividing by that total gives each score its share.
The top score gets 7.39 ÷ 11.11, which is about 66.5 percent of the row. The middle score gets about
24.5 percent and the bottom score about 9 percent, so together the three shares make 100 percent.

Negative scores work too, and real scores are often negative, so it helps to know that a power below
zero means dividing instead of multiplying. The score −1 becomes 1 ÷ 2.718, which is about 0.37, and
the score −2 becomes 1 ÷ 7.39, which is about 0.14. The more negative the score, the closer the
result gets to zero, but it never reaches zero. Scores with decimals land in between, so a score of
1.5 becomes about 4.48.

Two features of softmax matter for everything that follows in this lesson. Every share is positive,
because 2.718 raised to any power is above zero, so even a low-scoring character gets a sliver of
the row. Softmax also cares a great deal about the gaps between scores, because each step of 1
multiplies rather than adds. A gap of 1 makes one share about 2.7 times the other, and a gap of 2
makes it about 2.7 × 2.7, or 7.4, times the other. A gap of 8 means multiplying by 2.7 eight times
over, which comes to about 3,000.

These shares are called **attention weights**, and each one tells you how much of one word’s value
ends up in the query’s blend.

In the `Mix` stage, the query collects every value it is allowed to see and blends them together,
with each value counting exactly as much as its attention weight. A blend like this is called a
**weighted average**, and you have probably met one already in a class grade. Say a test counts for
75 percent of your grade and a quiz counts for the other 25 percent. You score 80 on the test and
100 on the quiz. Three quarters of 80 is 60 and one quarter of 100 is 25, so your grade for the
class works out to 85.

Attention does the same calculation with whole lists of numbers instead of single marks, multiplying
every number in a value by that value’s weight. Suppose the query gives 75 percent of its weight to
a value holding (4, 0) and 25 percent to a value holding (0, 8). Three quarters of the first value
is (3, 0) and one quarter of the second is (0, 2). Adding them position by position gives (3 + 0,
0 + 2), so the blend is (3, 2). The lab shows the first four numbers of the real blend, out of 64, in
the **Mixed value** chip.

The **Mix length** readout turns the whole blend into one number that says how big its numbers are
overall. To get it, square every number in the blend, add up the squares, and take the square root.
For the blend (3, 2), that is √(9 + 4), which is about 3.6. The readout is 0 only when every number
in the blend is 0, because any other number has a square above 0.

### Heads and layers

Everything so far describes one **attention head**: one complete set of the three recipes, with its
own learned numbers. This model has four heads working side by side, and each one makes its own
slips, labels and packets from the same text. Running several heads side by side like this, so that
one sentence can be routed in four different ways at once, is called **multi-head attention**.

Each head’s lists are 64 numbers long in this model, and that length is called the **head
dimension**.

The model also has two layers, one after the other, so attention happens twice on the way through.
Layer 1 works on the character vectors as they come in and passes updated vectors on to Layer 2,
whose four heads then do their own fetching. That makes eight heads in all, and you choose one with
the **Layer** buttons and the **Attention head** slider at the top of the **Query key value
inspector** card.

The grid of attention weights for one head in one layer, showing how every word shares out its
attention, is called an **attention pattern**. The **Attention matrix** card draws it, with one row
for each query word and one column for each key word.

### Why divide by 8?

The divide-by-8 step looks like tidy bookkeeping, but it decides how the shares come out. To see
why, picture a game where you flip a coin 64 times, adding 1 to your total for every heads and
taking away 1 for every tails. Most of the flips cancel each other out, so your total almost never
ends up anywhere near 64. In about three games out of four it lands within 8 of zero, on one side or
the other, and it rarely lands more than 16 away.

Now change the number of flips. With 16 flips the total usually lands within about 4 of zero, while
with 256 flips it usually lands within about 16. How far from zero the total usually lands is called
its spread, and four times as many flips only doubles the spread. That is the square-root pattern:
√16 = 4, √64 = 8 and √256 = 16.

A dot product behaves the same way when the numbers in both lists are about the size of 1 and their
signs look random. Each of the 64 positions then adds a product of about size 1, sometimes positive
and sometimes negative, like one coin flip. So the score usually lands within about 8 of zero, while
lists of 256 numbers would give scores that usually land within about 16. Dividing by the square
root of the head dimension brings the typical score back to a size of about 1, however long the
lists are.

That pattern holds for lists of random-looking numbers, but a trained head can still learn bigger
scores on purpose, as you will see in Layer 1.

The size of the scores matters because softmax turns gaps between scores into huge differences in
share. Go back to the three scores 2, 1 and 0, which gave shares of about 66.5, 24.5 and 9 percent.
Now make them 8 times bigger, so they become 16, 8 and 0. The top score now takes more than 99.9
percent of the row, which leaves almost nothing for the other two.

When one character takes 99 percent or more of a row, the lab calls that row **saturated**. A
saturated row has stopped blending, because it hands over one value and ignores almost everything
else.

A dot product divided by the square root of the head dimension is called a **scaled dot product**.
The lab’s **Score scaling** switch lets you turn the division off and watch rows saturate for
yourself.

### The one-way rule

In this model, a word may read itself and the words before it, but it may never read the words after
it, and this rule is called the **causal mask**. It is called a mask because it covers up later
words, and causal because each word may only use what came before it, as a cause comes before its
effect.

The mask works by changing the scores before softmax runs: the score for every later character is
set to minus infinity, written −∞, which is lower than any number. That makes it the one exception
to “every share is positive”, because 2.718 raised to −∞ shrinks all the way to zero. The later
character’s share therefore comes out as exactly zero. The lab draws those blocked spots with
diagonal stripes, called hatching, and the **Scale** line says `masked` if you inspect one.

Because each row can only reach back, the grid in the **Attention matrix** card is a triangle rather
than a square. The diagonal runs from the top-left to the bottom-right corner, where each word or
character meets itself, and the corner above it, where later words would sit, stays empty.

The diagonal also lets you read distances. In the Characters view, a stripe of dark cells just below
the diagonal, one cell to the left of it in every row, means each character looks 1 back. A stripe 7
cells to the left of the diagonal means each character looks 7 back.

To see why the rule exists, remember that this model learned by guessing each next character in a
64-character piece of text, called a window, seen all at once. Picture a practice quiz where the
answer to each question is printed right underneath it. You could score 100 percent by copying the
line below each question, and you would learn nothing at all.

Without the one-way rule, the model would be in the same position, because the spot before each
answer could read the answer directly. For example, when the model reads “the cat”, the spot holding
`c` has to guess that `a` comes next. Without the rule, the `c` could read the `a` one seat to its
right and copy it. The mask hides every answer from the position that has to guess it.

That is what makes all-at-once practice fair. Without the mask, the only fair way to practise would
be to run the model 64 separate times. Each time, it would see only the text up to the spot it has
to guess. With the mask, the model takes in the whole window in one run, and every spot still has to
guess its next character without seeing it. One run then gives 64 fair guesses to learn from.
Attention lets those 64 guesses be worked out side by side, and the mask keeps them honest.

The rule is a design choice, not a law of nature, and keeping it or dropping it divides language
models into families:

- An **encoder** drops the rule, so every word may look both backward and forward, and its grid is a
  full square. Reading in both directions like this is called bidirectional attention, and encoders
  use it to understand a whole text at once. That suits jobs such as sorting emails into spam and
  not spam, or finding the pages that best match a search.
- A **decoder-only (causal) language model** keeps the rule and learns to guess the next piece of
  text at every position. This lab’s model is one, and so are most of today’s chat models, which
  write by guessing one piece, adding it to the end, and running again. The name comes from an older
  design with two halves: an encoder that read the input and a decoder that wrote the output. A
  decoder-only model keeps only the writing half.

An encoder cannot practise by guessing the next word, because every position could see its own
answer, so it needs a different practice task. A **masked language model** is an encoder that
practises by covering some words in the middle of a text with a blank. It then guesses them from the
words on both sides. A well-known model called BERT works this way, and this kind of practice suits
understanding text better than writing it.

Be careful with that name, because “masked” here means words hidden behind a blank, which is a
different thing from the causal mask. A masked language model has no causal mask at all.

The lab’s **Mask** control can lift the one-way rule for one head so you can see what that head
would read. Doing so does not turn this model into an encoder, as Where it breaks explains.

### Where position comes from

The desk story has a gap: nothing on a label says which desk it sits at, so shuffling the desks
would leave every match with the same score. Attention on its own therefore could not tell “the dog
bit the man” from “the man bit the dog”. The one-way rule leaks a little information about order,
because a word near the start can see only a few words while a word near the end can see many. **The
transformer block** lab measures how much.

Models close the gap by giving every desk its seat number in some form, so that position becomes
part of the calculation. Any way of doing this is called a **positional encoding**, and four kinds
are common:

- **Learned seat numbers** (used by GPT-2, an older text-writing model, and by this lab’s model):
  the model learns one list of numbers for each seat. It adds that list to the vector of whatever
  character sits there. There is no list for a seat past the last one, so the model has a fixed
  longest input, which is 64 characters here.
- **Wave-pattern seat numbers** (used by the Transformer, the 2017 design that first built a model
  around attention): each seat gets a fixed pattern made from waves, with nothing to learn. Models
  that use this kind tend to work poorly on texts longer than the ones they trained on.
- **A distance penalty** (ALiBi): instead of seat numbers, the match score between two words is
  lowered more the farther apart they are, so nearby words tend to win.
- **A twist** (RoPE, used by Llama models and many other recent ones): each slip and label is turned
  by an amount set by its seat, like turning a clock hand. The match still depends on what the slip
  and label say. The seat part of the match, though, depends only on how far apart the two words
  sit, not on where in the text they are. **Modern attention at scale** shows how it works.

This lab’s model uses the first kind, and you can see the result in its heads. A head can prefer to
look a fixed distance back only if its slips and labels carry the seat number. Some heads in Layer 1
here do exactly that, looking 1, 2 or 3 characters back on every text this lesson tries. Those heads
put between 66 and 100 percent of their weight at that fixed distance. If the learned seat numbers
are wiped to zero, that share falls to under 6 percent.

### What is actually running

With the model’s own settings, none of this is a simulation: the lab runs a real, very small trained
model and reads every vector and route straight out of it. The three switches and the editable chips
work out new results from those same vectors. The model has two layers of four heads and about 1.4
million learned numbers, called **parameters**, which were all set during training.

The badge at the top of the first card starts with `ONNX model` once the model is running, since
ONNX is the file format it is stored in. While the model loads, the badge says
`Loading teaching model…`, and if loading fails, it says `Model unavailable · inspectable fallback`.
In both cases the lab shows stand-in values from a fixed wave pattern with no learned meaning, and a
note in the **Attention matrix** card tells you so.

The model practised on Shakespeare for 8,000 rounds of training, called steps, and each step gave it
a batch of windows to guess its way through. The first 90 percent of the text was used for practice,
and over all those steps the model went through it about 23 times. The last 10 percent was kept
aside, so the model could be tested on lines it had never seen.

About 30 percent of the practice windows were not ordinary Shakespeare text. In every batch of 64
windows, 19 were made-up repeats: a short string, sometimes random symbols and sometimes a snippet
of the play text, repeated over and over. They were put in on purpose, so the model would meet lots
of repetition and have a reason to learn copying. Each string repeated every 4 to 31 characters.
Repeats of 11, 13, 17 and 19 characters were left out on purpose, so they could be used later to
test the model on something new.

The model knows 66 different symbols, such as letters, punctuation marks and the space. The
**Words** view is one of two settings on the **Matrix view** control. It adds up the shares of each
word’s characters, and it counts the space in front of a word as part of that word. For example, the
Words cell for `animal` adds together the shares on the space before it and on a, n, i, m, a and l.

A word’s row is the row of its last character, so the row for `it` is really the row for its `t`.
Under the one-way rule, that `t` is the first spot that has seen the whole word. Every character
that row can see, spaces included, belongs to exactly one word, so a row’s word totals still add up
to exactly 100 percent.

The **Characters** view shows the grid the model really works with, with one row and one column for
every character.

## Why it’s here

Start with the information a word holds before attention runs, which is less than you might expect.
Take the `t` at the end of `it`. Before attention runs, its vector says only something like “I am a
`t`, and I sit at this seat in the line”. Nothing in it says that an animal appeared earlier, that
someone is tired, or even that the letter before it was an `i`. Guessing what comes next from that
alone would be like guessing the next word of a story from a single letter.

Picture opening a group chat where the newest message says “she said yes!!” and nothing else. To
make sense of it, you scroll back up through the conversation to find out who “she” is and what the
original question was. Each word in a model faces the same problem, and attention is how it looks
back through the earlier text for what it needs.

Some of the fetching is very simple: Layer 1 heads 3 and 4, which you will meet later, look exactly
one character back. So after Layer 1, the `t` can carry information about the `i` before it, and
later heads can build on that.

### Looking things up by content

The important feature is that this looking back is organised by content rather than by fixed wiring.
A word has no permanent connection to “three words back” or to “the first word of the sentence”.
Instead, its query is compared with every key it can see, and the resulting scores decide where its
information comes from. As a result, the same head can pull information from the previous word in
one sentence and from a word thirty places back in another. Some heads do learn a fixed-distance
habit, as you will see, but they learn it from the seat numbers, not from wiring.

After training, the model’s parameters never change again, yet the routes are calculated fresh for
every sentence, because they depend on which queries match which keys in that text. That is the
whole trick: a fixed set of learned numbers, used to build new connections every time.

### Doing it all at once

Attention also solved a second problem, which was about speed rather than understanding. Older
language models, called recurrent models, read text strictly in order and squeezed everything they
had read into a single memory of fixed size. That is a bit like remembering a whole book by
rewriting one index card after every page. On a single card the details get crowded out, and you
cannot start page 50 until you have finished page 49.

During training, attention works out the scores for every position at the same time, so none of that
work has to wait its turn. The one-way rule keeps that fair, since each spot still has to guess its
next character without seeing it. The powerful chips used to train models are built to do huge
numbers of small calculations side by side. Attention finally let language models use those chips
fully, and that is a large part of why training on huge amounts of text became practical.

## How to play with it

The lab opens on the pronoun sentence, “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired”.
It starts on Layer 2 head 2, with `it` as the query and `animal` as the inspected key. The
experiments below build on each other, so try them in order the first time you work through the lab.

### Follow one word

Click any word chip in the **Model attention routing diagram** card to make that word the query.
Curved lines called arcs fan out from it to the other words, and a thicker arc represents a bigger
attention weight. Later words get only a very faint, thin line, because the one-way rule gives them
no weight at all. The query has no arc to itself, so its weight on itself shows only in the bar
under its chip, while the bars under the other chips show their weights.

The readout strip underneath names the **query**, the **inspected key**, the **strongest route** and
its **weight**. It also shows how much of the row lands **on spaces**, a number that needs careful
reading, as What to notice explains. With `it` as the query in Layer 2 head 2, the strongest route
goes to `because` rather than to `animal`, which is worth remembering.

**Try this:** Keep `it` as the query and move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads
with **Layer** set to *Layer 1*. Then do the same with *Layer 2*, and notice that the sentence never
changes while the routes do. In Layer 1, head 1 sends most of the weight of `it` to `because`. Heads
2, 3 and 4 pile it onto `it` itself, for reasons you will see below. In Layer 2, the four heads’
strongest routes go to `it`, `because`, `cross` and `it`. Each head has its own learned numbers for
making queries and keys, so each one routes the sentence its own way, though two heads can learn
almost the same habit.

### Check the arithmetic for one pair

Click a cell in the grid of the **Attention matrix** card, where darker cells represent bigger
attention weights. Each cell’s row is the query word and its column is the key word, so a single
cell describes one pair of words. Clicking sets both words at once and jumps the stages to `Score`,
so the **Query key value inspector** card shows the arithmetic for that pair.

Now step from `Score` to `Softmax` and look at the strip in the inspector, which draws the whole row
twice. The top band shows each score as a bar that rises above its zero line when the score is
positive and drops below it when the score is negative. The bottom band shows each share as a bar,
and shares are never negative. The inspected word’s characters are outlined, and later characters
are hatched out by the mask, since the query cannot read them. The band titles write the head
dimension as `d`, so `√d` means √64, which is 8.

The **Softmax** line works out the share for one pair of characters itself. It uses the model’s full
query and keys, all 64 numbers of each, not only the four on screen. It then prints the model’s own
figure beside it. Next to that, it shows the largest gap between the lab’s sums and the model’s own
figures anywhere in the row, written in a short form such as 2.4e-7. That form means 0.00000024, so
every share in the row agrees to within about one part in a million. That shows you are looking at
the real arithmetic.

Two readouts look alike but measure different things, so it is worth telling them apart. **Selected
score** is the scaled score for one pair of characters: the last character of the query word and the
last character of the inspected word. **Selected weight** is the share for the whole word, all its
characters plus the space in front, which is the number the grid cell shows. A single character can
get a tiny share while its word’s total is large, because the weight is spread across the word’s
characters.

If you prefer reading numbers to reading shades, open **View weights as text** under the grid for a
list of the current row.

### Edit a key

The `Q` and `K` chips are editable, which lets you test how a change to a single key spreads through
the whole row. Type a different number into one of the four boxes in the `K` chip. Then watch the
arcs, the bars under the word chips, or the list under **View weights as text**. Every share in the
row moves, not only the one for the word you edited. That happens because every share is worked out
by dividing by the same total, and that total changes when one key’s score does.

The strip is a poor place to watch this, because it draws every share bar as a fraction of the
tallest one. The other bars there keep their heights unless the bar you changed is, or becomes, the
tallest.

Raising one number in `K` changes the score by the matching number in `Q` times the amount you
added. So where that `Q` number is positive, raising the `K` number raises the score. Where it is
negative, raising the `K` number lowers the score, whatever sign the `K` number started with. For
example, if the matching `Q` number is 0.5 and you add 2 to the `K` number, the score rises by 0.5 ×
2, which is 1. **Selected score** shows the change after the division by 8, so it rises by 0.125.

Only the first four of the 64 numbers can be edited, while the other 60 stay as the model made them.
Press **Match the model vectors** to switch back to the model’s own numbers. The lab remembers what
you typed, though, so if you type in a box again, your earlier changes come back with it.

### Switch off the scaling

Set **Score scaling** to *No scaling*, so the scores reaching softmax are 8 times bigger than
before. In many rows the largest score now takes nearly everything, and two readouts show it as you
switch back and forth. **Largest weight** is the biggest share any single character gets in the
current row. **Saturated rows** counts the rows in this head’s whole grid where one character gets
99 percent or more.

For Layer 2 head 2 on the pronoun sentence, no row is saturated with the division, but 26 of 55 rows
are saturated without it. The count is out of 55 because the sentence has 56 characters and the
first one is left out. That first character can only see itself, so it always gives itself 100
percent and would tell you nothing.

So in this head, the division by 8 is what keeps the shares spread across several characters,
instead of letting one big score drown out the rest. The division does not ban saturation, though,
since all it does is stop scores from growing just because the lists are long. A trained head can
still learn scores big enough to saturate when that is useful.

Set the control back to *÷ √64*, then move to Layer 1 head 3 or head 4, and **Saturated rows** still
counts most of the 55 rows. Those heads pile onto the previous character on purpose, which is how
they put 99.9 percent of `it` on the `i` before its `t`. The trouble the division prevents is
saturation that nobody asked for, caused only by long lists. Leave the control on *÷ √64*, so the
next experiments use the model’s own setting.

### Empty the packets

Set **Value vectors (V)** to *Zero all*, which replaces every value with a list of zeros. Look at
the strip and the arcs, and you will notice that every attention weight stays exactly where it was.
The **Mixed value** chip, though, drops to all zeros, and **Mix length** falls to 0.

This is the cleanest test of the split of jobs from earlier, between choosing a route and carrying
the content. The query and the keys chose the routes and the values travelled along them, so
emptying the values changes what arrives but not where it comes from.

Next, click a dark cell in the grid, so the inspected word has a large share. Then switch the
control between *Model* and *Zero word*, which empties only the inspected word’s values, including
the value for the space in front of it. The **Mixed value** numbers and **Mix length** change while
the shares stay put, because the blend loses exactly that word’s contribution and nothing else. When
you are finished, set the control back to *Model*, which restores the model’s own values.

### Lift the one-way rule

In the **Attention matrix** card, set **Mask** to *Bidirectional*, so every character may read the
whole sentence. Shares now appear above the diagonal, and the triangle fills into a square because
later characters can be read too. The readout strip adds two new numbers: the share **on later
characters** and the share **on the next character**.

Every share below the diagonal shrinks by the same factor. The earlier characters’ scores have not
changed, so softmax keeps their sizes compared with each other and divides them all by a bigger
total that now includes the later characters. For example, suppose 40 percent of a row moves to
later characters, leaving 60 percent for the earlier ones. Every earlier share is then multiplied by
0.6, so a share that was 50 percent becomes 30 percent.

How much moves depends a great deal on which head you are looking at. For `it` on the pronoun
sentence, Layer 1 head 3 moves only 0.1 percent of its weight to later characters, and head 4 moves
only 0.2 percent.
Layer 2 head 4, by contrast, moves 90.2 percent of its weight there.

Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put about a third of the weight of `it` on the very next character, 33.8 and
30.6 percent. That next character is exactly the answer the model practises guessing, so reading it
would turn guessing into copying. This is the practice-quiz problem from earlier, and it is why the
model trained with the mask on. Set **Mask** back to *Causal* before you go on, so the grid shows
the model as it was trained.

### A route that is not what it looks like

Press the **Pronoun sentence** button in the Examples row of the first card, which reloads the
example and resets the mask, scaling and values to the model’s own settings. With `it` as the query,
move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads with **Layer** on *Layer 1*, then again on
*Layer 2*. Look for a thick arc to `animal`, which you might expect to find, since that is what `it`
means in this sentence. There isn’t one: no head’s strongest route goes to `animal`, and none gives
it even 10 percent.

Look at Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 instead, which put 99.9 percent of `it` on the word `it` itself. That
self-route has no arc, so you see it as a full bar under the `it` chip. To see why it happens,
switch **Matrix view** to *Characters* and find the stripe just below the diagonal. A stripe in that
position means that every character looks at the character immediately before it.

The lab reads `it` at its last letter, the `t`, and the character immediately before that letter is
the `i` inside `it`. That is why the Words view credits the word `it` with nearly all the weight. A
thick arc or a full bar shows where information could come from, and it is not a sign that the model
has worked out what `it` refers to.

So why does no head reach for `animal`? One likely reason is that the lab reads `it` at its `t`, and
the only guess that spot has to make is the next character. Here that is a space, and the spelling
of `it` already makes a space a good guess without any help from `animal`. No head in this model
shows a sign of linking `it` to `animal`, and these pictures cannot tell you how a large chatbot
handles pronouns.

### A route that follows the content

Now press **Repeated name**, which loads “Mark Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” with the second
`Mark` as the query. Set **Layer** to *Layer 2* and move the **Attention head** slider to head 3.
That head sends 92.7 percent of the second `Mark`’s weight to `Jones`, the word that came right
after the first `Mark` in the sentence.

That is a sensible place to look, because at the second `Mark` the model has to guess what comes
next. The last time `Mark` appeared, a space and then `Jones` came right after it.

Switch **Matrix view** to *Characters* to see exactly where the weight lands. Almost all of it sits
on one character: the space right after the first `Mark`, which the Words view counts as part of
`Jones`. That is also why **On spaces** reads close to 100 percent. Under the one-way rule, that
space has seen only “Mark ” so far, so its packet cannot hold the name `Jones` at all. The head
points at the spot that came right after `Mark` last time, which is the spot it would copy from to
guess the next character this time.

Press **Name moved earlier**, which loads “Anna Blake met Mark Jones and then Mark”. The share on
`Jones` stays high, at 97.2 percent, although the space right after the earlier `Mark` is now 19
characters back instead of 34. A head that always looked a set distance back would have lost its
target when the name moved. This route follows the content instead: wherever the earlier `Mark`
sits, the head’s weight lands right after it.

For one more check, press **Repeated name** again, then click at the end of the **Sentence** box at
the top of the first card. Type a space and then Smith, and click the new `Smith` chip to make it
the query, keeping Layer 2 head 3. There is no earlier `Smith` for the head to match, and no word
receives even 20 percent of its attention.

Other names can come out differently, because the head matches characters, not whole names. `Clark`
ends in the same `k` as `Mark`, so the head lands mostly on the space after the second `Mark`, which
the Words view counts as part of `Clark`. A name such as `Davis` makes the head park much of its
weight on the first character of the sentence, which the Words view credits to the first `Mark`.
That looks like what Going deeper calls an attention sink, a parking spot for spare weight, not a
link to `Mark`.

Even the `Jones` route is only a lead worth testing, not proof that the model used it, and Where it
breaks explains why.

## What to notice

- **Every row adds up to 100 percent**, so routes compete: making one connection stronger has to
  weaken the others in that row, as you saw when you edited one key.
- **The grid is a triangle, not a square**, because each character can look at itself and at earlier
  characters but never ahead. Attention lets the model practise on every position in one run, and
  this rule is what keeps that practice fair. Setting **Mask** to *Bidirectional* fills the empty
  corner.
- **The On spaces readout needs careful reading.** With `it` as the query, Layer 1 head 2 puts 99.1
  percent on spaces. That happens only because the head always looks two characters back, and two
  characters back from the `t` in `it` is the space in front of the word. For `because`, the same
  head puts 1.3 percent on spaces, while Layer 2 head 4 puts 61.7 percent on spaces for `it`. So a
  big On spaces number does not mean a head is interested in spaces. It can be a side effect of
  where a word ends and how far back the head looks.
- **Layer 1’s heads look a fixed distance back**: head 2 looks 2 characters back, and heads 3 and 4
  look 1 character back. Head 1 puts about two thirds of its weight 3 characters back. Layer 2’s
  routes look different on the same text, because Layer 2’s heads have their own learned numbers and
  read vectors that Layer 1 has already changed.
- **The division by 8 is not a small detail**, even though, with the model’s own setting, the
  **Score** and **Scale** lines differ only by that same factor. Without it, many rows in a head
  such as Layer 2 head 2 pile almost everything onto one character. With it, a row piles up like
  that only where a head has learned big scores on purpose, as Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 have.
- **Emptying the packets changes nothing in the strip and everything in the blend**, because keys
  decide who is read and values decide what is read.

## Where it breaks

### Where the desk picture stops matching

The desk story explains routing well, but routing is all it explains. Real slips and labels have no
words written on them, because they are lists of learned numbers whose meaning nobody chose. A
strong match between a slip and a label tells you nothing about what is in the packet that gets
handed over. Real desks would also come with seat numbers, while attention gets them only because
the model adds them on purpose.

### A thick arc is not an explanation

The biggest caution in this lesson is about how you read these pictures. A thick arc means that one
position’s packet was given a large weight in one word’s blend, in one head of one layer. It does
not tell you what that packet contained, whether the following step kept it, or whether a later
layer cancelled it. It also does not tell you whether the final answer depended on it at all. The
`Jones` route showed this already, since the packet it points at could not even hold the name.

A route is a place where information could flow, not evidence that it mattered. Showing that a part
of the model matters takes an experiment that changes the model on purpose and then measures the
effect. One such experiment is switching a head off and checking whether the model’s predictions get
worse, and tests like that are the subject of **Interpretability II: circuits**.

### Copying, or a habit?

Picture a game where someone reads out a long string of letters that repeats, and two friends race
to call out each next letter. The first friend looks back along the string for the last place the
current letter appeared and calls out the letter that came after it. The second friend has a habit
instead, and always calls out the letter from 7 places back.

If the string repeats every 8 letters, both friends are right every time once the pattern has gone
round once. Say the letters are ABCDEFGH repeated and you have just heard the second C, so the first
friend finds the earlier C and calls out the D that followed it. Counting 7 back from the second C
goes B, A, H, G, F, E, D, so the habit friend also calls out D, which is exactly what comes next.

Now change the string so it repeats every 9 letters, as ABCDEFGHI. Counting 7 back from the second C
now lands on E, which is wrong, while the first friend still finds the earlier C and calls out D.
Only the first friend keeps winning.

Heads in the model can behave like either friend, and the lab lets you run the same test on them. A
head with a fixed-distance habit looks like a copier whenever the repeat length happens to match its
distance. The reliable way to tell them apart is to change the repeat length and then watch whether
the stripes follow.

The game stops matching the model in two places. A head does not call out anything itself, because
it only puts weight on a position, and the rest of the model turns that into a guess. Also, nobody
can call the letters the first time through the string, since there is no earlier copy to look back
at yet.

Press **Repeats every 8**, which loads eight random letters repeated four times and switches to the
Characters view. In Layer 2, heads 2 and 3 show stripes 7, 15 and 23 characters back. They put 94
and 88 percent of their weight on earlier copies of the character that comes next. The next
character itself is still hidden by the one-way rule, so the heads find where that same letter
appeared before. By the last copy of the string, the pattern has already appeared three times, which
is why there are three stripes.

Now press **Repeats every 9** and watch the same two heads. Their stripes move to 8, 17 and 26
characters back. Head 2 now puts 95 percent on earlier copies of the next character, and head 3 puts
89 percent. Less than 1 percent is left at the old distances, so these heads are the first friend: they look for what
followed last time, wherever it is.

The other six heads do not copy, and none of them puts more than 11 percent on earlier copies of the
next character at either repeat length. Layer 1’s four heads keep the same stripes both times: head 1
looks 3 characters back, head 2 looks 2 back, and heads 3 and 4 look 1 back. Those are fixed-distance habits, like the second friend’s, and only the
learned seat numbers make them possible.

A head that copies like this is called an **induction head**. A head that looks at the character
right before the current one, like Layer 1 heads 3 and 4, is called a **previous-token head**. Here
a token means one piece of text, which in this model is a single character.

In the usual explanation of a two-layer model, the two kinds of head work as a pair. Take “Mark
Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” again, where a previous-token head in Layer 1 looks one
character back from every spot. So the space after the first `Mark` picks up a note that says,
roughly, “the character before me was `k`”. When the second `Mark` reaches its last letter, `k`, the
induction head’s slip asks for a spot whose previous character was `k`. The space after the first
`Mark` carries exactly that label, so the head reads it, and that space is where `Jones` begins.

This lab has not proven that wiring, as the next part explains.

### What this model shows, and what it does not

The model was given a test built so that a fixed-distance habit would fail it. A random string is
repeated once, at a repeat length the model never practised on: 11, 13, 17 or 19 characters. The
test then measures how much weight each head puts on the character right after the earlier match.

To read the results, you need one more idea, which is how the model’s error gets measured. When the
model guesses, it does not pick one character. Instead, it gives each of its 66 symbols a chance,
such as 60 percent for `e` and 10 percent for `a`. The error is a score called **loss**, which is
higher when the model is more surprised by the character that actually comes next. Being surprised
here means the model had given the correct character only a small chance.

Lower loss is better, and it is measured in a unit called a nat. To turn nats into something you can
picture, raise 2.718 to the power of the loss. The answer is roughly how many equally likely
characters the model is choosing among. A blind guess that gives all 66 symbols an equal chance
scores about 4.19 nats, because 2.718 raised to 4.19 is about 66.

Here is what the test shows about the two copying heads and the rest of the model:

- On random strings, Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 83 and 81 percent of their weight on the character
  after the earlier match. Those figures come from the worst of the four repeat lengths, so the
  other lengths score at least as well. A head with a fixed 7-back habit would score near zero here,
  because none of these repeat lengths puts that character 7 back.
- On pieces of held-out Shakespeare, each repeated once in the same way and never seen in practice,
  the same two heads put 40 and 51 percent on that character.
- Every other head stays under 6 percent on random strings, whatever the repeat length.
- On random strings, the loss on the first copy is about 4.3, a little worse than a blind guess.
  That is most likely because the model still bets on characters that are common in Shakespeare,
  which a random string does not favour. On the second copy the loss falls to between 0.13 and 0.25,
  so the model is nearly certain of each next character.
- On Shakespeare text, the loss on the first copy is about 1.6 or 1.7. On the second copy it falls
  to between 0.39 and 0.53.
- Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 put 99 percent or more of their weight on the previous character. That is
  the information a copying head in Layer 2 would need from the layer below.

Here is what the test does not show, and these gaps matter as much as the results:

- It does not show **the wiring**: nobody traced which Layer 1 head feeds which Layer 2 head.
  Switching a head off is a blunt test, because it removes everything the head adds to the model
  rather than one connection. Switching off Layer 2 head 3 makes the loss on the second copy more
  than ten times higher. If the copying heads relied on a previous-token head, switching that head
  off should hurt clearly more than switching off a head that does something else. Instead,
  switching off Layer 1 head 2, which is not a previous-token head, raises the loss about as much as
  switching off Layer 1 head 4, which is one. So this test cannot tell which head feeds which.
- It says nothing about **big models**: this model has about 1.4 million parameters, reads
  characters, and learned from one author’s plays plus the made-up repeats. So it cannot tell you
  how big chatbots pick up a pattern from a few examples you type into your message, which
  researchers call in-context learning.
- It does not explain **the other heads**: Layer 1 head 1 and Layer 2 heads 1 and 4 are shown in the
  lab but not explained.
- It is not **a full explanation**, because an attention pattern on its own is evidence about
  routing and nothing more.

### Other limits of this lab

The model trained for 8,000 steps, which added up to about 23 passes over its practice text. That
practice text was the 1.0 million characters in the first 90 percent of the plays. On the held-out
last tenth of Shakespeare, its loss is 1.54 nats per character, compared with 4.19 for a blind
guess. Since 2.718 raised to 1.54 is about 4.7, that is roughly as unsure as picking among 5 equally
likely characters instead of 66. That is far better than guessing, though still a long way from
certain. About 30 percent of its practice windows were made-up repeated strings, so this model was
shown repetition on purpose.

The three switches are experiments run on a model that has already finished training, which limits
what they can show:

- **Mask** lifts the rule for one head of a model that was trained with the rule on, so that head
  never practised scoring later characters. In Layer 2, its slips and labels are still built from a
  Layer 1 that kept the rule, and the rest of the model does not change.
- **Score scaling** turns off the division for one head of a model that learned with it on. So it
  shows that head’s rows piling onto one character, which the lab calls saturation, not what
  training without the division would have produced.
- **Value vectors (V)** changes the lab’s blend for one head, but it does not change the model’s
  answer, because the emptied blend never runs through the rest of the model.

Input stops at 64 characters, which is the window length the model trained on. The Words view adds
up characters, while big chat models read pieces of words called tokens, so the lab’s word totals
are not real tokens. The editable chips show only the first four numbers of lists that are 64
numbers long. Once you edit them, the shares come from your numbers rather than the model’s.

### In real systems

Full attention compares every position with every other position, so the work grows with the square
of the text’s length. Double the length of a text and there are about four times as many pairs to
score. Make it ten times longer and there are about a hundred times as many, which adds up fast.
That one fact drives most of the engineering behind models that read very long texts.

**A common mix-up:** Attention in a model is not the same as human focus or attention span. A thick
arc or dark cell is also not proof that one word caused the model’s answer. Even a link that follows
the content, like `Mark` to `Jones`, is one weight in a weighted average worked out with dot
products, not a reason or a decision.

## Going deeper

### Many heads, one output

Real models run several attention heads side by side, which is what the **Attention head** slider
lets you step through here. Their blends are joined end to end and passed through one more learned
mixing step, called the **output projection**. That step decides how the fetched information gets
written back into the model. The result is added into a running record that each position carries
through the model, called the residual stream, which **The transformer block** follows.

Splitting the work into heads costs little: this model’s full vectors are 256 numbers long, split
into four heads of 64 numbers each. Four heads of 64 cost about the same arithmetic as one head of
256, and in return you get four separate ways to route information.

### Positions in bigger models

Most current models use the twist (RoPE) rather than this lab’s learned seat numbers. Stretching
such a model to handle longer inputs works by adjusting how far each seat turns the slips and
labels, and **Modern attention at scale** covers both ideas.

### Saving work while writing

When a model writes text one piece at a time, the keys and values of earlier pieces never change,
since under the one-way rule they never read the new ones. They can therefore be saved and reused,
instead of being recalculated for every new piece of text. That saved store is the subject of
**Inference & KV cache**, where K stands for keys and V stands for values.

**Grouped-query attention** makes that store smaller by letting several query heads share one set of
keys and values. It gives up a small amount of quality in return for a large saving in memory. Other
methods, such as FlashAttention, work out exactly the same softmax in small blocks, so the full grid
of scores never has to be stored at once.

### Induction heads in research

The copying heads you met are called induction heads, and Olsson and colleagues studied them
in 2022. Their paper is titled *In-context Learning and Induction Heads*, and it describes such a head
by two behaviours. First, the head looks back for the spot right after an earlier copy of the
current token. Second, it copies, which makes the token found there more likely to come next.

In a two-layer model, they found an induction head paired with a previous-token head, the same two
kinds you saw here. In larger models, they saw a sudden change early in training, when induction
heads formed while the models got better at in-context learning. They describe their evidence as
preliminary and indirect. It suggests that these heads drive most of that learning, but it is early
work and does not prove the link directly.

### Attention sinks

Some heads park most of their attention on the very first token whenever they have nothing useful to
fetch from anywhere else. A position that soaks up weight like this is called an **attention sink**.
You may have seen something like it if you typed a name such as `Davis` earlier. This matters in
practice, because deleting that position from the saved store of keys and values makes the model’s
output worse.

### Where the division came from

Dividing by the square root of the head dimension comes from the original Transformer paper, by
Vaswani and colleagues in 2017. The authors suspected that large scores push softmax into a corner
where one share is nearly everything. In that corner, small changes to the scores barely move the
shares, so training gets almost no signal about which way to adjust them.

For example, if the top share is already 99.9 percent, nudging its score up or down a little barely
changes any share. Training can then hardly tell whether a nudge helped or hurt, so learning would
become very slow, which is the problem the division was meant to prevent.

The lab’s **Score scaling** switch shows the corner, but not the slow learning, which would only
appear during training, and the lab never trains the model.

### Bidirectional here, and a real encoder

The **Bidirectional** setting in this lab is an experiment on a decoder-only model, not an encoder.
A real encoder, such as a masked language model, is trained from the start with every position
visible. As a result, it learns different queries and keys from any that this model ever made.
