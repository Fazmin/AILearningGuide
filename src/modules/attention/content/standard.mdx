## A word that needs another word

Read this: *The animal did not cross the street because it was tired.* You linked `it` to `animal`
before the full stop. Now consider what a model holds at the position of `it` before anything
else runs: a vector that says, roughly, "this token, at this place". The animal is several words
back. Something has to fetch it.

That is the routing problem, and text is full of it: a pronoun needs its referent, a closing
bracket its opening one, a list item the format set three examples back. Which position matters
changes with every input, so no fixed wiring can answer it. **Next-token prediction** treated the
model as a map from a context to a next-token distribution; this lesson opens the step inside it
that does the routing, self-attention.

Here is the promise. You will build attention yourself, each piece fixing the failure of the one
before. Then you will interrogate it, reading its evidence the way a sceptical researcher would.

And here is the catch, so you can watch for it. Ask which word `it` reads most and you will
probably say `animal`. In this lab, no head gives `animal` even 10 percent of `it`’s weight. Hold
on to that; it comes back once you have the tools to explain it.

By the end you will be able to:

- tell queries, keys and values apart: queries and keys decide which positions are read, values
  decide what is read;
- read an attention pattern as evidence about routing, not proof that the output used the route;
- tell a head that follows content from one that reads a fixed distance, by moving the content;
- say what the causal mask changes, and how encoder and decoder-only models differ;
- say why scores are divided by the square root of the head dimension.

### What the lab runs

The lab runs a real two-layer, four-head character transformer with 1.37 million parameters,
trained on Tiny Shakespeare plus synthetic repeated strings. With the model’s own settings, every
attention weight and every Q, K and V vector comes from it. The **runtime status** badge says
when deterministic stand-in values show instead.

Three things are not plain measurement, and the lesson flags each as it arrives. The **Examples**
buttons load fixed inputs. **Mask** set to *Bidirectional*, **Score scaling** set to *No
scaling*, and **Value vectors (V)** are inference-time experiments on one head, not trained
conditions. And after you edit a Q or K chip, the arcs, that query’s word row, the `Softmax` row
and the mix come from your numbers until you press **Match the model vectors**.

### Follow one word

Click a word chip in the arc diagram to make it the query; thicker arcs carry more weight, and
the readout strip names the strongest route. With `it` in Layer 2 head 2, it goes to `because`.

One convention matters later. The model reads characters, so the **Words** view sums character
weights over each word, where a word owns the space in front of it. A word’s row is read at its
last character, so `it`’s row is really its `t`’s, and word rows sum to exactly 100 percent with
nothing discarded. The **Characters** view shows the model’s own grid.

## Build it yourself, one fix at a time

Here is the plan. We design the routing step from scratch. Each attempt fails in a specific way,
and the fix is the next piece of attention.

### First try: average everything

The simplest thing that could work: give `it` the average of every earlier vector. What arrives
is equal parts `animal`, `street`, `because` and the rest. The animal is in there, at exactly the
volume of the street. Every earlier position counts the same, whatever the query is looking for.
Uniform mixing cannot select.

It does get one thing right. Every position’s average can be computed at once. Recurrent models
pushed all history through one fixed-size state, computed strictly in order. Comparing all
positions in parallel during training is what let sequence models use modern accelerators fully,
and that is much of why attention made scale practical. Keep it.

So: averaging is parallel but blind. We need a way to choose.

### Score the matches

To choose, score every earlier position for how well it fits what `it` needs. That takes two
vectors: a **query** `q` at `it` for what it looks for, and a **key** `k` at each position for
what it offers. They are separate because what a pronoun looks for is not what a pronoun is. Both
are learned projections of the position’s vector.

Score a pair with the dot product, `q · k`. Components with the same sign raise it, opposite signs
lower it, so a high score means the two point in similar directions in a space this head learned.
The **Score** readout shows `q · k` for the selected pair; the `Q` and `K` chips show the first 4
of 64 dimensions.

But scores are any real numbers. We cannot average with weights of −3 and 41.

### Turn scores into shares

We want weights that are positive and sum to one, so the mix stays an average, only an uneven
one. Softmax does it: `exp(sⱼ) / Σ exp(s)` over every position the query may read. Scores of 2, 1
and 0 become shares of about 66.5, 24.5 and 9 percent.

Two properties matter from here on. Every share is positive, so even a poor match gets a sliver.
And gaps multiply: a gap of 1 makes one share about 2.7 times another. These shares are the
**attention weights**, and each query’s row sums to 1.

Step the inspector from `Score` to `Softmax`. The strip draws the row twice: scores above a zero
line, weights below, masked positions hatched. Score, then normalise. Is the lab really computing
that?

### Check the arithmetic for one pair

Select a cell in the **Attention matrix**; that sets the query and the inspected key. Step to
`Softmax`. The **Softmax** row recomputes that character pair’s weight in closed form from the
displayed Q and K, and prints the model’s own value beside it.

**Pause and predict:** a model exported to ONNX against a formula recomputed in your browser. Two
decimal places, or closer?

Far closer: they agree to within about 1e-6. This is the model’s arithmetic, not a sketch of it.

While you are here, separate two look-alike readouts. **Selected score** is one character pair;
**Selected weight** sums a whole word. A character can get a tiny share while its word’s total is
large.

### Shares compete

**Pause and predict:** edit one number in the `K` chip. Will only that word’s weight change?

Every weight in the row moves. Softmax divides by a total over the whole row, so changing one score
changes every share. Read the arcs or percentages rather than the strip, which scales bars to the
tallest one, then press **Match the model vectors**.

Recap: a row is a competition. Strengthening one route weakens the others.

### One vector can’t do two jobs

Now look at what we mix. We have been averaging the positions’ own vectors, so the vector that won
the match is also the one delivered. A position’s advertisement and its payload are forced to be
the same, and training cannot shape one for matching and the other for being useful on arrival.

The fix is a third projection, the **value** `v`, and the output becomes `Σⱼ wⱼ vⱼ`. One
independent set of query, key and value projections is an **attention head**. Query and key choose
*who* is read; the value is *what* is read.

Notice what is stored and what is not. The projection matrices are fixed after training. The
routes are computed fresh for every input from its content and positions, which is why the same
learned weights serve any input. A split this clean deserves a test.

### Zero the values

**Pause and predict:** set **Value vectors (V)** to *Zero all*. What happens to the strip, the arcs
and the **Mixed value**?

Every weight stays exactly where it was, while the **Mixed value** falls to zero. The weights are
computed from Q and K before any value is read. Q and K chose the route; V is what travels along
it. *Zero word* removes only the inspected word’s values, and the mix loses exactly that word’s
share; pick a cell with a large weight to see it. Then set it back to *Model*.

Recap: we can choose and deliver. But the recipe has a blind spot.

### What the recipe can’t see: order

`q · k` sees only vectors, not which slot they came from. Reorder the words and the outputs only
reorder, so attention cannot tell *the dog bit the man* from *the man bit the dog*. (The causal
mask, coming up, leaks a little order; **The transformer block** measures it.) Order has to be
injected:

- **Learned absolute** (GPT-2, and this lab’s model): a trained vector per slot is added at the
  input. A slot beyond the table has none, so context is capped at 64 here.
- **Sinusoidal absolute** (the original Transformer): a fixed sine and cosine pattern is added.
  Nothing is trained, but such models extrapolate poorly past their trained length.
- **Relative, ALiBi**: no position vector. A penalty proportional to query–key distance is
  subtracted from each score, with a fixed slope per head.
- **Rotary, RoPE** (Llama and many recent models): queries and keys are rotated by an angle
  proportional to position, so the score depends only on the distance between the two. **Modern attention at
  scale** has a card for it.

A head can prefer a fixed distance back only if its queries and keys carry the slot. Here, Layer 1
has heads that read 1, 2 or 3 characters back on every text this lesson tries.

**Pause and predict:** zero the 64 learned position vectors. Do those stripes survive?

They collapse, from between 66 and 100 percent of the weight to under 6 percent. The lab has no
control for this, so take the figure from the lesson. The order test itself runs in **The
transformer block**.

### The five buttons are the five steps you just built

The stage buttons under the arc diagram read `Query`, `Keys`, `Score`, `Softmax`, `Mix`. You built
each: the two projections that ask and answer, the dot product, the competing shares, the weighted
sum of values. Compactly, for a causal head:

`softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k + M) V`

Here `d_k` is the head dimension, 64, and `M` is a mask. Those two pieces are still unexplained,
and each gets a section, because each fixes a failure you only meet by running without it. The
**Mixed value** chip shows the first dimensions of the 64-dimensional result, which the output
projection then writes back into the residual stream.

### Many heads, many questions

One head asks one question per position. The **Layer** buttons and **Attention head** slider pick
one of eight heads, two layers of four.

**Pause and predict:** keep `it` as the query and sweep all four heads at each **Layer**. How many
different strongest routes?

Several: `because`, `it`, `it`, `it` in Layer 1; `it`, `because`, `cross`, `it` in Layer 2. Each
head learned its own projections. Layer 2 also differs because it attends to vectors that layer 1
already modified.

That is **multi-head attention**: outputs concatenated, then one learned **output projection**.
Splitting a width of 256 into 4 heads of 64 costs about the same arithmetic as one 256-wide head,
for four independent routing channels.

## A bug you’d hit: scores that are too loud

We skipped a step. The **Scale** readout divides every score by 8 before softmax. Building
attention for the first time, you would probably leave that out.

**Pause and predict:** without the division, softmax sees scores 8 times larger. Do rows stay
graded, or does one character take nearly everything?

One character takes nearly everything. Scale 2, 1 and 0 by 8 and you get 16, 8 and 0: the top
score now takes more than 99.9 percent. Softmax turns gaps into ratios, and a gap of 8 is a ratio
of about 3,000.

### Why divide by 8?

Dot products grow with width. If the components of `q` and `k` are independent with mean 0 and
variance 1, `q · k` sums `d_k` products and has variance `d_k`, so its typical size is `√d_k`: 8
for 64 dimensions, 16 for 256. Dividing by `√d_k` brings scores back to about 1 at any width. That
is the **scaled dot product**.

The reason comes from the original Transformer paper (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017, section
3.2.1), whose authors suspected that large dot products push softmax into regions with extremely
small gradients. The division does not forbid sharp rows, though; a trained head can still learn
large scores on purpose.

### Switch off the scaling

On **Pronoun sentence**, Layer 2 head 2, watch two readouts. **Largest weight** is the biggest
share in the current row. **Saturated rows** counts the rows of this head’s grid that put 99
percent or more on one character.

**Pause and predict:** with the division, none of its 55 rows saturate. Set **Score scaling** to
*No scaling*. How many now?

26 of 55. In this head, the division is what keeps rows graded instead of one-hot. Set it back to
*÷ √64* and visit Layer 1 head 3: it saturates anyway, piling onto the previous character on
purpose. The failure the division prevents is saturation caused only by width.

Be honest about the switch. It multiplies a trained head’s scores by 8 at inference, which shows
saturation, not training without the division. The gradients belong to training, which the lab
cannot show.

## How a model could cheat, and the rule that stops it

We built attention so every position reads the others in parallel. Now train it to guess the next
character at every position of a 64-character window at once.

**Pause and predict:** if every position may read every other, what is the laziest way to drive
the loss down?

Read the next character and copy it: the position before each answer can see the answer. The loss
would fall and the model would learn nothing about predicting.

### The one-way rule

The fix is `M`. The **causal mask** adds 0 to scores for earlier or equal positions and −∞ for
later ones, so every later weight is exactly zero. The matrix becomes a triangle, hatched above the
diagonal in the Characters view. Now one pass over a window gives 64 fair guesses: every position
trains in parallel on next-token prediction without seeing its answer.

The mask is a design choice, not a law, and it splits model families. A **decoder-only (causal)
language model**, like this lab’s, keeps it and predicts the next token. An **encoder** drops it:
attention is bidirectional, the matrix is a full square, and every position reads earlier and later
ones. An encoder cannot train on next-token prediction, since each position would see its answer.

A **masked language model** such as BERT is an encoder trained instead to fill hidden tokens from
both sides, which suits understanding better than generating.

**A common mix-up:** the "masked" there means tokens hidden behind a blank. A masked language model
has no causal mask at all.

### Lift the one-way rule

In the **Attention matrix** card, set **Mask** to *Bidirectional*. Weight appears above the
diagonal, and the readout strip adds the share on later characters and on the very next one.

**Pause and predict:** with `it` as the query, will every head move much weight forward?

It depends on the head. Layer 2 head 4 moves 90.2 percent onto later characters; Layer 1 heads 3
and 4 move almost none. Every weight below the diagonal shrinks by the same factor, because the
row still sums to 100 percent.

Now the cheat, live. Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 33.8 and 30.6 percent of `it` on the very next
character. In next-token training that character is the answer, and reading it would solve the task
by copying, which is what the mask prevents.

Keep straight what you did. *Bidirectional* is an experiment on a decoder-only model, not an
encoder: this model trained with the mask on, so those later-position scores were never trained.
Set **Mask** back to *Causal*.

**How sure is this?** Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 move 0.1 and 0.2 percent. Layer 1 is exact for the
lifted head; Layer 2’s queries and keys still come from a causal Layer 1.

## Reading the evidence like a sceptic

You have built the machine and stress-tested two parts. Switch roles. A picture of attention
invites a story; a sceptic asks what else would draw the same picture. Start with the opening
prediction.

### A route that is not what it looks like

**Pause and predict:** press **Pronoun sentence** and sweep the **Attention head** slider through
both layers with `it` as the query. Which head sends the most to `animal`?

None. No head’s strongest route is `animal`, and none gives it even 10 percent. Layer 1 heads 3 and
4 instead send 99.9 percent to `it` itself. Switch to **Characters**: a stripe one place below the
diagonal. They read the previous character, and the row for `it` is read at its `t`, so the
previous character is the `i` inside `it`. The route comes from position.

Why no `animal`? One likely reason: the `t` only has to guess the next character, a space, and
the spelling of `it` already makes that a good guess. No head here shows a sign of linking `it`
to `animal`, and these pictures cannot tell you how a large model handles pronouns.

The **On spaces** readout sets the same trap. For `it`, Layer 1 head 2 puts 99.1 percent on
spaces, but only because it reads two characters back and `it` has two letters. It is a habit
meeting a word boundary, not an interest in spaces.

**How sure is this?** For `because` the same head puts 1.3 percent on spaces. Layer 2 head 4 puts
61.7 percent there for `it`. Layer 1 heads 2, 3 and 4 read 2, 1 and 1 characters back, and head 1
puts about two thirds of its weight 3 back.

### Move the content, see if the route follows

A route that comes from position should not care what the text says; one that comes from content
should move when the content moves. That is a test.

Press **Repeated name** and set **Layer 2**, head 3. The second `Mark` sends 92.7 percent to
`Jones`, the word after the earlier `Mark`, which is where to look when guessing what follows this
one.

**Pause and predict:** press **Name moved earlier**. The earlier name is now 19 characters back
instead of 34. Does the route break, or follow?

It follows, at 97.2 percent on `Jones`. In **Characters** it sits on the space after the earlier
`Mark`. Under the mask that space has seen only "Mark ", so its value cannot hold `Jones` yet: the
head points at the slot it would copy from.

Then run a control. Type Smith at the end and select it: with no earlier match, no word gets even
20 percent. With Davis, about two thirds goes to the first character, which the Words view counts
as the first `Mark`, a parking spot rather than a link.

Recap: a route that follows the content is worth testing. It is still not proof that the output
used it.

### Copying or habit? Change the period

A fixed-distance head looks like copying whenever the repeat length matches its distance. So
change the repeat length and see whether the stripes follow.

Press **Repeats every 8** and view **Characters**. Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 have stripes 7, 15 and 23
back, where the next character of the pattern sits.

**Pause and predict:** press **Repeats every 9**. Which heads’ stripes move?

Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 move to 8, 17 and 26, leaving less than 1 percent at 7, 15 and 23. That is
copying. Layer 1 heads 1 to 4 keep their strongest stripe 3, 2, 1 and 1 back at both periods:
fixed-distance habits, which depend on the learned position vectors.

**How sure is this?** The two copying heads put 94 and 88 percent on the true next character at
period 8, and 95 and 89 percent at period 9. The six other heads stay at or under 11 percent at
either period.

## What an attention picture can’t tell you

You caught one route that came from position and confirmed two that follow content. How far does
that reach? Less far than it feels.

**An attention weight is not an explanation.** It says how much of one value vector was included
at one head in one layer. It says nothing about what that value *contains*, whether the output
projection kept it, whether a later layer cancelled it, or whether the answer depended on it.
That takes intervention, the subject of **Interpretability II: circuits**.

### What this model demonstrates, and what it does not

The model trained for 8,000 steps on 64-character windows. In each batch of 64, 45 came from the
first 90 percent of the text and 19 were synthetic strings repeated at a period from 4 to 31
characters, except 11, 13, 17 and 19. Those four were held back for a check a position habit
should fail.

The check repeats a random string once at a held-out period and measures each head’s attention
from the second copy to the character after the earlier match, an offset one less than the
period. A fixed 7-back look-up would score near zero.

Demonstrated:

- Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 0.83 and 0.81 of their weight there on random strings, the worst case
  over the four periods. Every other head stays under 0.06 at each period.
- The loss on the repeated half falls from about 4.3 to between 0.13 and 0.25 nats on random
  strings.
- Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 put 0.99 or more on the previous character, the information a layer-2
  induction head needs from the layer below.

**How sure is this?** On held-out Shakespeare snippets the two heads score 0.40 and 0.51, and the
repeated-half loss falls from 1.6–1.7 to 0.39–0.53 nats.

Not demonstrated:

- The wiring. No path patching was run, and switching a head off removes everything it writes.
  Switching off Layer 2 head 3 raises the repeated-half loss more than tenfold, but Layer 1 head 2,
  not a previous-token head, costs about as much as Layer 1 head 4. Ablation alone does not
  isolate the circuit.
- Anything beyond this model, which has 1.4 million parameters, reads characters and learned from
  one author’s plays; it says nothing about in-context learning in a large language model.
- What Layer 1 head 1 and Layer 2 heads 1 and 4 do. They are displayed, not explained.

### Limits of this lab

The training ran about 23 passes over the 1.0 million characters of its split. Held-out loss on
the last 10 percent is 1.54 nats per character (2.23 bits), against 4.19 nats for a uniform guess
over 66 symbols. About 30 percent of each batch was synthetic repeats, by design, so this model
saw repetition on purpose.

The three switches are inference-time experiments on one head. **Mask** lifts the mask on a head
trained with it; **Score scaling** multiplies its scores by 8; **Value vectors (V)** changes the
lab’s mix, not the model’s output.

Input caps at 64 characters, the trained block size. Word totals are sums over characters, not
tokenizer tokens. The chips expose 4 of 64 dimensions, and after an edit the weights are yours.

### In real systems

Full self-attention scores every pair of positions, so time and memory grow with the square of
sequence length; that single fact drives most long-context engineering. Heads rarely specialise
into cleanly describable roles, and patterns can sometimes be altered without changing output. A
single head’s map is one term in a much larger computation.

## Where this goes next

Production attention keeps the core you built and changes much around it.

**Multi-head attention** concatenates its heads’ outputs, passes them through the **output
projection**, and adds the result back to the residual stream, which **The transformer block**
follows.

Most current models use RoPE rather than learned absolute vectors, and context extension methods
work by rescaling its angles; both are in **Modern attention at scale**.

Past keys and values do not change during generation, so they can be cached, the subject of
**Inference & KV cache**. **Grouped-query attention** shrinks that cache by sharing one key/value
head across several query heads. Kernels such as FlashAttention compute exactly this softmax in
on-chip tiles without storing the full score matrix.

**Induction heads** are studied in *In-context Learning and Induction Heads* (Olsson and
colleagues, 2022). They define the head by two behaviours: prefix matching, which attends back to
tokens that were followed by the current token, and copying, which raises the logit of the
attended-to token.

In a two-layer model the circuit pairs a **previous-token head** with an induction head, the
circuit the **Repeats every 8** and **Repeats every 9** tests probe. In larger models the authors
report a phase change early in training in which induction heads form while in-context learning
improves, and they describe their evidence that these heads drive most in-context learning as
preliminary and indirect.

**Attention sinks** are positions, often the first token, that absorb large weight when a head has
nothing useful to retrieve; evicting them from a cache degrades output.
