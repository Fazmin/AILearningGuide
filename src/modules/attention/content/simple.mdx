## The big picture

Read this sentence from the lab: “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired.” You knew right away that `it` means the animal and not the street. You made that link by connecting one word to another word several places earlier.

A language model is the kind of program behind a chatbot. It has to make links like this for every word it reads. To guess what comes next, a word often needs information from somewhere else in the text. It might need the name that a pronoun stands for. It might need the opening bracket that a closing bracket belongs to. It might even need the pattern that a few earlier examples set up. The part of the model that fetches this information is called **attention**. This module shows you how it works, one small step at a time.

In a single breath: every word sends out a search request. It compares the request with what each earlier word offers, and each one gets a match score. The word then takes in a blend of information from the best matches. None of these links is wired in ahead of time. The model works them out fresh for every sentence. So the same word can draw on different neighbours in different sentences.

### Where you have met it

Most of today’s chat models are the same kind of model as the tiny one in this lab. They read text in one direction, from left to right. They learned by guessing the next piece of text over and over. Suppose a chatbot keeps track of who “she” is in your message. Or suppose it carries on a list in the same format that you started. In both cases, attention lets each word reach back to earlier words for the information it needs.

### Why it matters

In a model like this one, attention is the only step that lets one word take information from another. Without it, every word would be stuck with what it knew about itself and its place in the line. The model’s guesses would be far worse, because what helps a guess usually sits somewhere else in the text.

Attention also changed the way models are trained, and that matters nearly as much. It lets a model practise guessing what comes next at every spot in a long text at once. Older models had to work through the text one spot after another. That is a big reason it became practical to train very large models at all.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- The search slip, label and packet that every word makes. The slip and label decide where information comes from, and the packet carries it.
- How to read a picture of attention with care. A thick line shows where information could flow, not why the model answered as it did.
- How to tell a part of the model that follows content from one that looks a fixed distance back.
- What the one-way rule changes, since it stops words from reading later words. Also how models that read both ways differ.
- Why each match score gets divided by 8 before it becomes a share. Also why 8 is the right number here, and what goes wrong without it.

## What it is

### Words as lists of numbers

Inside the model, every character of your sentence is stored as a long list of numbers that describes it. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**. It works a bit like a player’s stats in a sports game, where each number rates one skill. The model’s vectors differ in one important way, because nobody decided what each number stands for.

The model settled on its numbers itself during **training**. Training is a long practice period in which the model guesses the next character over and over. After each round of guesses, it nudges all its numbers a little. That way its next guesses come out better.

This model reads characters, meaning letters, spaces and punctuation marks, not whole words. So every character gets its own vector. Most of this lesson talks about words, because the main views of the lab group characters that way. We return to characters when they matter.

### Three things on every desk

Picture a classroom where every word of the sentence sits at its own desk, in order. Each desk holds three different items, and each item has an official name that the lab and glossary use:

- A **search slip** describes what this word wants from the sentence, and its official name is the **query**.
- A **label** on the desk shows what this word can offer, and its official name is the **key**.
- A **packet** in the drawer holds the information that gets handed over, and its official name is the **value**.

Every word compares its search slip with the label on every desk it may see, including its own. Labels that match the slip well get a high score, and poor matches get a low one. The lab then turns those scores into shares that add up to 100 percent. Finally, the word receives a blend of everyone’s packets. Each packet counts exactly as much as its share.

All three items are vectors. The model makes them from one starting vector, using three separate sets of learned numbers. Each set works like its own recipe, so a word’s label can differ from its packet. Training shaped the label for being matched and the packet for being useful once it arrives.

Keep this split of jobs in mind, because the lab keeps returning to it. The query and the key decide **who gets read**, and the value decides **what gets read**. The lab has a switch that tests this split directly. You will try it in How to play with it.

### The five stages

The lab walks through the search of one word in five stages. They are named on the buttons in the first card: `Query`, `Keys`, `Score`, `Softmax` and `Mix`. Each stage below gives the idea first and then a small example that you can check by hand.

In the `Query` stage, you choose the word whose search you want to follow. You click its chip in the **Model attention routing diagram** card. A chip is one of the small boxes that hold each word. The word you click becomes the query. The `Q` chip in the **Query key value inspector** card then shows the first four numbers of its query.

In the `Keys` stage, every word that the query may see presents its key, the label on its desk. The `K` chip shows the first four numbers of the key for the word you are inspecting. The lab calls that word the inspected key. You choose it by clicking a cell in the **Attention matrix** grid, as How to play with it shows.

In the `Score` stage, the lab compares the query with every key using a calculation called the **dot product**. It takes two steps. First you multiply the two lists position by position, then you add up the answers. Here is a short example with four numbers in each list, matching the four numbers that each chip shows. Suppose the query is (2, −1, 0, 3) and the key is (1, 2, 5, 1):

- In the first position, 2 × 1 gives 2.
- In the second position, −1 × 2 gives −2.
- In the third position, 0 × 5 gives 0.
- In the fourth position, 3 × 1 gives 3.

Adding the four answers gives a score of 3. The −2 cancels the 2, and that leaves the 3 from the last position. When the two numbers in a position have the same sign, they raise the score. When their signs are opposite, they lower it. A high score means the two lists tend to agree across many positions. That is what a good match looks like in numbers.

The inspector shows its working as a stack of labelled lines, like a worked answer on a maths test. The real lists in this model are 64 numbers long. So the **Score** line adds up 64 products instead of four. It shows the total next to `q · k`. That is how the lab writes the dot product of a query and a key.

The `Score` stage does one more thing to the whole row of scores for the query. That row holds the score for every character the query can see. Every score is divided by 8. Each list has 64 numbers, and 8 is the square root of 64. The **Scale** line shows this step as `score ÷ √64`. It gets its own section below, because it matters more than it looks.

In the `Softmax` stage, the scaled scores become shares that are all positive and add up to 100 percent. The tool that does this is called **softmax**. The lab writes its formula as `exp(s) ÷ Σ exp(s)`, where `s` is one score. The part `exp(s)` means a special number, about 2.718, raised to the power `s`. The Greek letter `Σ`, called sigma, means “add this up for every character the query can see”. In words, softmax turns every score into a positive number. Then it divides each one by the total of them all.

Try it with three scores, 2, 1 and 0. First turn each one into a positive number:

- The score 2 becomes 2.718 × 2.718, which is about 7.39.
- The score 1 becomes 2.718 itself, which rounds to 2.72.
- The score 0 becomes exactly 1, because any number raised to the power 0 equals 1.

Those three numbers add up to about 11.11. Dividing by that total gives each score its share. The top score gets 7.39 ÷ 11.11, which is about 66.5 percent of the row. The middle score gets about 24.5 percent, and the bottom score gets about 9 percent. Together, the three shares make 100 percent.

Negative scores work too, and real scores are often negative. A power below zero means dividing instead of multiplying. The score −1 becomes 1 ÷ 2.718, which is about 0.37. The score −2 becomes 1 ÷ 7.39, which is about 0.14. The more negative the score, the closer the result gets to zero, but it never reaches zero. Scores with decimals land in between, so a score of 1.5 becomes about 4.48.

Two features of softmax matter for everything that follows in this lesson. First, every share is positive, because 2.718 raised to any power is above zero. Even a low-scoring character gets a sliver of the row. Second, softmax cares a great deal about the gaps between scores. Each step of 1 multiplies instead of adding. A gap of 1 makes one share about 2.7 times the other. A gap of 2 makes it about 2.7 × 2.7, or 7.4, times the other. A gap of 8 means multiplying by 2.7 eight times over, which comes to about 3,000.

These shares are called **attention weights**. Each one tells you how much of one word’s value ends up in the query’s blend.

In the `Mix` stage, the query collects every value it may see and blends them together. Each value counts exactly as much as its attention weight. A blend like this is called a **weighted average**. You have probably met one already in a class grade. Say a test counts for 75 percent of your grade and a quiz counts for the other 25 percent. You score 80 on the test and 100 on the quiz. Three quarters of 80 is 60, and one quarter of 100 is 25. So your grade for the class works out to 85.

Attention does the same calculation with whole lists of numbers instead of single marks. It multiplies every number in a value by the weight of that value. Suppose the query gives 75 percent of its weight to a value holding (4, 0). It gives the other 25 percent to a value holding (0, 8). Three quarters of the first value is (3, 0), and one quarter of the second is (0, 2). Adding them position by position gives (3 + 0, 0 + 2), so the blend is (3, 2). The lab shows the first four numbers of the real blend, out of 64, in the **Mixed value** chip.

The **Mix length** readout turns the whole blend into one number that says how big its numbers are overall. To get it, square every number in the blend, add up the squares, and take the square root. For the blend (3, 2), that is √(9 + 4), which is about 3.6. The readout is 0 only when every number in the blend is 0. Any other number has a square above 0.

### Heads and layers

Everything so far describes one **attention head**. A head is one complete set of the three recipes, with its own learned numbers. This model has four heads working side by side. Each one makes its own slips, labels and packets from the same text. Running several heads side by side is called **multi-head attention**. It routes one sentence in four different ways at once.

Each head’s lists are 64 numbers long in this model, and that length is called the **head dimension**.

The model also has two layers, one after the other, so attention happens twice on the way through. Layer 1 works on the character vectors as they come in. It passes updated vectors on to Layer 2, whose four heads then do their own fetching. That makes eight heads in all, and you pick one with the **Layer** buttons and the **Attention head** slider. Both sit at the top of the **Query key value inspector** card.

The grid of attention weights for one head in one layer shows how every word shares out its attention. It is called an **attention pattern**. The **Attention matrix** card draws it, with one row per query word and one column per key word.

### Why divide by 8?

The divide-by-8 step looks like tidy bookkeeping, but it decides how the shares come out. To see why, picture a game where you flip a coin 64 times. You add 1 to your total for every heads and take away 1 for every tails. Most of the flips cancel each other out, so your total almost never ends up anywhere near 64. In about three games out of four, it lands within 8 of zero, on one side or the other. It rarely lands more than 16 away.

Now change the number of flips. With 16 flips, the total usually lands within about 4 of zero. With 256 flips, it usually lands within about 16. How far from zero the total usually lands is called its spread. Four times as many flips only doubles the spread. That is the square-root pattern: √16 = 4, √64 = 8 and √256 = 16.

A dot product behaves the same way when both lists hold numbers of about size 1 with random-looking signs. Each of the 64 positions adds a product of about size 1. It is sometimes positive and sometimes negative, like one coin flip. So the score usually lands within about 8 of zero. Lists of 256 numbers would give scores that usually land within about 16. Dividing by the square root of the head dimension brings the typical score back to about 1. That holds however long the lists are.

That pattern holds for lists of random-looking numbers. A trained head can still learn bigger scores on purpose, as you will see in Layer 1.

The size of the scores matters because softmax turns gaps between scores into huge differences in share. Go back to the three scores 2, 1 and 0. They gave shares of about 66.5, 24.5 and 9 percent. Now make them 8 times bigger, so they become 16, 8 and 0. The top score now takes more than 99.9 percent of the row. That leaves almost nothing for the other two.

When one character takes 99 percent or more of a row, the lab calls that row **saturated**. A saturated row has stopped blending, because it hands over one value and ignores almost everything else.

A dot product divided by the square root of the head dimension is called a **scaled dot product**. The lab’s **Score scaling** switch lets you turn the division off and watch rows saturate for yourself.

### The one-way rule

In this model, a word may read itself and the words before it. It may never read the words after it, and this rule is called the **causal mask**. It is a mask because it covers up later words. It is causal because each word may only use what came before it, like a cause before its effect.

The mask works by changing the scores before softmax runs. The score for every later character is set to minus infinity, written −∞, which is lower than any number. That makes it the one exception to “every share is positive”. The number 2.718 raised to −∞ shrinks all the way to zero. The share of a later character therefore comes out as exactly zero. The lab draws those blocked spots with diagonal stripes, called hatching. The **Scale** line says `masked` if you inspect one.

Each row can only reach back. So the grid in the **Attention matrix** card is a triangle, not a square. The diagonal runs from the top-left to the bottom-right corner, where each word or character meets itself. The corner above it, where later words would sit, stays empty.

The diagonal also lets you read distances. In the Characters view, look for a stripe of dark cells right below the diagonal. If it sits one cell left of the diagonal in every row, each character looks 1 back. A stripe 7 cells to the left of the diagonal means each character looks 7 back.

To see why the rule exists, remember how this model learned. It guessed each next character in a 64-character piece of text, called a window. It saw the whole window at once. Picture a practice quiz where the answer to each question is printed right underneath it. You could score 100 percent by copying the line below each question, and you would learn nothing at all.

Without the one-way rule, the model would be in the same position. The spot before each answer could read the answer directly. For example, when the model reads “the cat”, the spot holding `c` has to guess that `a` comes next. Without the rule, the `c` could read the `a` one seat to its right and copy it. The mask hides every answer from the position that has to guess it.

That is what makes all-at-once practice fair. Without the mask, the only fair way to practise would be to run the model 64 separate times. Each time, it would see only the text up to the spot it has to guess. With the mask, the model takes in the whole window in one run. Every spot still has to guess its next character without seeing it. One run then gives 64 fair guesses to learn from. Attention lets those 64 guesses be worked out side by side, and the mask keeps them honest.

The rule is a design choice, not a law of nature. Keeping it or dropping it divides language models into two families:

- An **encoder** drops the rule, so every word may look both backward and forward. Its grid is a full square. Reading in both directions is called bidirectional attention. Encoders use it to understand a whole text at once. That suits sorting emails into spam and not spam, or finding the pages that best match a search.
- A **decoder-only (causal) language model** keeps the rule. It learns to guess the next piece of text at every position. This lab’s model is one, and so are most of today’s chat models. They write by guessing one piece, adding it to the end, and running again. The name comes from an older design with two halves. An encoder read the input, and a decoder wrote the output. A decoder-only model keeps only the writing half.

An encoder cannot practise by guessing the next word, because every position could see its own answer. It needs a different practice task. A **masked language model** is an encoder that practises by hiding some words in a text behind a blank. It then guesses them from the words on both sides. A well-known model called BERT works this way. This kind of practice suits understanding text better than writing it.

Be careful with that name. Here “masked” means words hidden behind a blank, which is different from the causal mask. A masked language model has no causal mask at all.

The **Mask** control in the lab can lift the one-way rule for one head. You can then see what that head would read. Doing so does not turn this model into an encoder, as Where it breaks explains.

### Where position comes from

The desk story has a gap, because nothing on a label says which desk it sits at. Shuffling the desks would leave every match with the same score. Attention on its own could not tell “the dog bit the man” from “the man bit the dog”. The one-way rule leaks a little information about order. A word near the start sees only a few words, while a word near the end sees many. **The transformer block** lab measures how much.

Models close the gap by giving every desk a seat number in some form. That makes position part of the calculation. Any way of doing this is called a **positional encoding**, and four kinds are common:

- **Learned seat numbers** are used by GPT-2, an older text-writing model, and by this lab’s model. The model learns one list of numbers for each seat. It adds that list to the vector of whatever character sits there. No list exists for a seat past the last one. So the model has a fixed longest input, which is 64 characters here.
- **Wave-pattern seat numbers** are used by the Transformer, the 2017 design that first built a model around attention. Each seat gets a fixed pattern made from waves, with nothing to learn. Models that use this kind tend to work poorly on texts longer than the ones they trained on.
- **A distance penalty** is called ALiBi. It skips seat numbers and lowers the match score the farther apart two words sit. So nearby words tend to win.
- **A twist** is called RoPE, and Llama models and many other recent ones use it. Each slip and label is turned by an amount set by its seat, like turning a clock hand. The match still depends on what the slip and label say. The seat part of the match depends only on how far apart the two words sit. It does not depend on where in the text they are. **Modern attention at scale** shows how it works.

This lab’s model uses the first kind, and you can see the result in its heads. A head can look a fixed distance back only if its slips and labels carry the seat number. Some heads in Layer 1 do exactly that. They look 1, 2 or 3 characters back on every text this lesson tries. Those heads put between 66 and 100 percent of their weight at that fixed distance. If the learned seat numbers are wiped to zero, that share falls to under 6 percent.

### What is actually running

With the model’s own settings, none of this is a simulation. The lab runs a real, very small trained model and reads every vector and route straight out of it. The three switches and the editable chips work out new results from those same vectors. The model has two layers of four heads. It also has about 1.4 million learned numbers, called **parameters**, and training set all of them.

The badge at the top of the first card starts with `ONNX model` once the model is running. ONNX is the file format the model is stored in. While the model loads, the badge says `Loading teaching model…`. If loading fails, it says `Model unavailable · inspectable fallback`. In both cases the lab shows stand-in values from a fixed wave pattern with no learned meaning. A note in the **Attention matrix** card tells you so.

The model practised on Shakespeare for 8,000 rounds of training, called steps. Each step gave it a batch of windows to guess its way through. The first 90 percent of the text was used for practice. Over all those steps, the model went through it about 23 times. The last 10 percent was kept aside, so the model could be tested on lines it had never seen.

About 30 percent of the practice windows were not ordinary Shakespeare text. In every batch of 64 windows, 19 were made-up repeats. Each one was a short string repeated over and over. The string was sometimes random symbols and sometimes a snippet of the play text. They were put in on purpose, so the model meets lots of repetition and learns to copy. Each string repeated every 4 to 31 characters. Repeats of 11, 13, 17 and 19 characters were left out on purpose. That kept them free to test the model on something new.

The model knows 66 different symbols, such as letters, punctuation marks and the space. The **Words** view is one of two settings on the **Matrix view** control. It adds up the shares of each word’s characters. It counts the space in front of a word as part of that word. For example, the Words cell for `animal` adds the shares on its six letters and the space before it.

The row for a word is the row of its last character. So the row for `it` is really the row for its `t`. Under the one-way rule, that `t` is the first spot that has seen the whole word. Every character that row can see, spaces included, belongs to exactly one word. So the word totals in a row still add up to exactly 100 percent.

The **Characters** view shows the grid the model really works with. It has one row and one column for every character.

## Why it’s here

Start with the information a word holds before attention runs, which is less than you might expect. Take the `t` at the end of `it`. Before attention runs, its vector says only something like “I am a `t`, at this seat in the line”. Nothing in it says that an animal appeared earlier, or that someone is tired. It does not even say that the letter before it was an `i`. Guessing what comes next from that alone is like guessing the next word of a story from one letter.

Picture opening a group chat where the newest message says “she said yes!!” and nothing else. To make sense of it, you scroll back up through the conversation. You need to find out who “she” is and what the original question was. Each word in a model faces the same problem. Attention is how it looks back through the earlier text for what it needs.

Some of the fetching is very simple. Layer 1 heads 3 and 4, which you will meet later, look exactly one character back. So after Layer 1, the `t` can carry information about the `i` before it. Later heads can build on that.

### Looking things up by content

The important feature is that this looking back is organised by content instead of by fixed wiring. A word has no permanent connection to “three words back” or to “the first word of the sentence”. Instead, its query is compared with every key it can see. The resulting scores decide where its information comes from. So the same head can pull information from the previous word in one sentence. In another sentence, it can pull from a word thirty places back. Some heads do learn a fixed-distance habit, as you will see. They learn it from the seat numbers, not from wiring.

After training, the parameters of the model never change again, yet the routes are calculated fresh for every sentence. They depend on which queries match which keys in that text. That is the whole trick: a fixed set of learned numbers, used to build new connections every time.

### Doing it all at once

Attention also solved a second problem, which was about speed instead of understanding. Older language models, called recurrent models, read text strictly in order. They squeezed everything they had read into a single memory of fixed size. That is a bit like remembering a whole book by rewriting one index card after every page. On a single card the details get crowded out. You also cannot start page 50 until you finish page 49.

During training, attention works out the scores for every position at the same time. None of that work has to wait its turn. The one-way rule keeps that fair, since each spot still has to guess its next character without seeing it. The powerful chips used to train models are built to do huge numbers of small calculations side by side. Attention finally let language models use those chips fully. That is a large part of why training on huge amounts of text became practical.

## How to play with it

The lab opens on the pronoun sentence, “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired”. It starts on Layer 2 head 2, with `it` as the query and `animal` as the inspected key. The experiments below build on each other, so try them in order the first time through.

### Follow one word

Click any word chip in the **Model attention routing diagram** card to make that word the query. Curved lines called arcs fan out from it to the other words. A thicker arc stands for a bigger attention weight. Later words get only a very faint, thin line, because the one-way rule gives them no weight at all. The query has no arc to itself. Its weight on itself shows only in the bar under its chip. The bars under the other chips show their weights.

The readout strip underneath names the **query**, the **inspected key**, the **strongest route** and its **weight**. It also shows how much of the row lands **on spaces**. That number needs careful reading, as What to notice explains. With `it` as the query in Layer 2 head 2, the strongest route goes to `because`, not `animal`. That matters later.

**Try this:** Keep `it` as the query. Move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads with **Layer** on *Layer 1*, then on *Layer 2*. The sentence never changes, but the routes do. In Layer 1, head 1 sends most of the weight of `it` to `because`. Heads 2, 3 and 4 pile it onto `it` itself, for reasons you will see below. In Layer 2, the strongest routes of the four heads go to `it`, `because`, `cross` and `it`. Each head has its own learned numbers for making queries and keys. So each one routes the sentence its own way. Two heads can still learn almost the same habit.

### Check the arithmetic for one pair

Click a cell in the grid of the **Attention matrix** card. Darker cells stand for bigger attention weights. The row of a cell is the query word, and its column is the key word. So one cell describes one pair of words. Clicking sets both words at once and jumps the stages to `Score`. The **Query key value inspector** card then shows the arithmetic for that pair.

Now step from `Score` to `Softmax` and look at the strip in the inspector. It draws the whole row twice. The top band shows each score as a bar. The bar rises above its zero line for a positive score. It drops below the line for a negative score. The bottom band shows each share as a bar, and shares are never negative. The characters of the inspected word are outlined. Later characters are hatched out by the mask, since the query cannot read them. The band titles write the head dimension as `d`, so `√d` means √64, which is 8.

The **Softmax** line works out the share for one pair of characters itself. It uses the model’s full query and keys, all 64 numbers of each, not only the four on screen. It then prints the figure from the model itself beside it. Next to that, it shows the largest gap in the row between the lab’s sums and the model’s figures. The gap is written in a short form such as 2.4e-7, which means 0.00000024. So every share in the row agrees to within about one part in a million. That shows you are looking at the real arithmetic.

Two readouts look alike but measure different things. **Selected score** is the scaled score for one pair of characters. Those are the last character of the query word and the last character of the inspected word. **Selected weight** is the share for the whole word, with all its characters plus the space in front. That is the number the grid cell shows. A single character can get a tiny share while its word has a large total. The word’s weight is spread across its characters.

If you prefer numbers to shades, open **View weights as text** under the grid, which lists the current row.

### Edit a key

The `Q` and `K` chips are editable. That lets you test how a change to one key spreads through the whole row. Type a different number into one of the four boxes in the `K` chip. Then watch the arcs, the bars under the word chips, or the list under **View weights as text**. Every share in the row moves, not only the one for the word you edited. Every share is worked out by dividing by the same total. That total changes when one key’s score does.

The strip is a poor place to watch this. It draws every share bar as a fraction of the tallest one. The other bars there keep their heights unless the bar you changed is, or becomes, the tallest.

Raising one number in `K` changes the score by the matching number in `Q` times the amount you added. Where that `Q` number is positive, raising the `K` number raises the score. Where it is negative, raising the `K` number lowers the score, whatever sign the `K` number started with. For example, say the matching `Q` number is 0.5 and you add 2 to the `K` number. The score rises by 0.5 × 2, which is 1. **Selected score** shows the change after the division by 8, so it rises by 0.125.

Only the first four of the 64 numbers can be edited. The other 60 stay as the model made them. Press **Match the model vectors** to switch back to the numbers of the model. The lab remembers what you typed, though. If you type in a box again, your earlier changes come back with it.

### Switch off the scaling

Set **Score scaling** to *No scaling*, so the scores reaching softmax are 8 times bigger than before. In many rows the largest score now takes nearly everything. Two readouts show it as you switch back and forth. **Largest weight** is the biggest share any single character gets in the current row. **Saturated rows** counts the rows in the whole grid of this head. A row counts if one character gets 99 percent or more.

For Layer 2 head 2 on the pronoun sentence, no row is saturated with the division. Without it, 26 of 55 rows are saturated. The count is out of 55 because the sentence has 56 characters and the first one is left out. That first character can only see itself, so it always gives itself 100 percent and would tell you nothing.

So in this head, the division by 8 keeps the shares spread across several characters. Without it, one big score drowns out the rest. The division does not ban saturation, though. All it does is stop scores from growing only because the lists are long. A trained head can still learn scores big enough to saturate when that is useful.

Set the control back to *÷ √64*, then move to Layer 1 head 3 or head 4. **Saturated rows** still counts most of the 55 rows. Those heads pile onto the previous character on purpose. That is how they put 99.9 percent of `it` on the `i` before its `t`. The trouble the division prevents is saturation that nobody asked for, caused only by long lists. Leave the control on *÷ √64*, so the next experiments use the setting of the model itself.

### Empty the packets

Set **Value vectors (V)** to *Zero all*, which replaces every value with a list of zeros. Look at the strip and the arcs. Every attention weight stays exactly where it was. The **Mixed value** chip, though, drops to all zeros, and **Mix length** falls to 0.

This is the cleanest test of the split of jobs from earlier. One job is choosing a route, and the other is carrying the content. The query and the keys chose the routes, and the values travelled along them. So emptying the values changes what arrives but not where it comes from.

Next, click a dark cell in the grid, so the inspected word has a large share. Then switch the control between *Model* and *Zero word*. *Zero word* empties only the values of the inspected word. That includes the value for the space in front of it. The **Mixed value** numbers and **Mix length** change while the shares stay put. The blend loses exactly the part that word gave it and nothing else. When you are finished, set the control back to *Model*, which restores the values of the model.

### Lift the one-way rule

In the **Attention matrix** card, set **Mask** to *Bidirectional*, so every character may read the whole sentence. Shares now appear above the diagonal. The triangle fills into a square because later characters can be read too. The readout strip adds two new numbers: the share **on later characters** and the share **on the next character**.

Every share below the diagonal shrinks by the same factor. The scores of the earlier characters have not changed, so softmax keeps their sizes compared with each other. It divides them all by a bigger total that now includes the later characters. For example, suppose 40 percent of a row moves to later characters. That leaves 60 percent for the earlier ones. Every earlier share is then multiplied by 0.6, so a share that was 50 percent becomes 30 percent.

How much moves depends a great deal on the head you are looking at. For `it` on the pronoun sentence, Layer 1 head 3 moves only 0.1 percent of its weight later. Head 4 moves only 0.2 percent. Layer 2 head 4, by contrast, moves 90.2 percent of its weight there.

Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put about a third of the weight of `it` on the next character. The figures are 33.8 and 30.6 percent. That next character is exactly the answer the model practises guessing, so reading it would turn guessing into copying. This is the practice-quiz problem from earlier, and it is why the model trained with the mask on. Set **Mask** back to *Causal* before you go on, so the grid shows the model as it was trained.

### A route that is not what it looks like

Press the **Pronoun sentence** button in the Examples row of the first card. It reloads the example and resets the mask, scaling and values to the settings of the model. With `it` as the query, move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads. Do it on *Layer 1*, then on *Layer 2*. Look for a thick arc to `animal`. You might expect one, since that is what `it` means in this sentence. There isn’t one. No head has its strongest route go to `animal`, and none gives it even 10 percent.

Look at Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 instead. They put 99.9 percent of `it` on the word `it` itself. That self-route has no arc, so you see it as a full bar under the `it` chip. To see why it happens, switch **Matrix view** to *Characters* and find the stripe right below the diagonal. A stripe in that position means that every character looks at the character right before it.

The lab reads `it` at its last letter, the `t`. The character right before that letter is the `i` inside `it`. That is why the Words view gives the word `it` nearly all the weight. A thick arc or a full bar shows where information could come from. It is not a sign that the model has worked out what `it` refers to.

So why does no head reach for `animal`? One likely reason is that the lab reads `it` at its `t`. The only guess that spot has to make is the next character. Here that is a space, and the spelling of `it` already makes that a good guess without `animal`. No head in this model shows a sign of linking `it` to `animal`. These pictures also cannot tell you how a large chatbot handles pronouns.

### A route that follows the content

Now press **Repeated name**. It loads “Mark Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” with the second `Mark` as the query. Set **Layer** to *Layer 2* and move the **Attention head** slider to head 3. That head sends 92.7 percent of the weight of the second `Mark` to `Jones`. That word came right after the first `Mark`.

That is a sensible place to look. At the second `Mark`, the model has to guess what comes next. The last time `Mark` appeared, a space and then `Jones` came right after it.

Switch **Matrix view** to *Characters* to see exactly where the weight lands. Almost all of it sits on one character. That is the space right after the first `Mark`, which the Words view counts as part of `Jones`. That is also why **On spaces** reads close to 100 percent. Under the one-way rule, that space has seen only “Mark ” so far. So its packet cannot hold the name `Jones` at all. The head points at the spot that came right after `Mark` last time. That is the spot it would copy from to guess the next character this time.

Press **Name moved earlier**, which loads “Anna Blake met Mark Jones and then Mark”. The share on `Jones` stays high, at 97.2 percent. The space right after the earlier `Mark` is now 19 characters back instead of 34. A head that always looked a set distance back would have lost its target when the name moved. This route follows the content instead. Wherever the earlier `Mark` sits, the weight of the head lands right after it.

For one more check, press **Repeated name** again. Then click at the end of the **Sentence** box at the top of the first card. Type a space and then Smith. Click the new `Smith` chip to make it the query, keeping Layer 2 head 3. There is no earlier `Smith` for the head to match. No word receives even 20 percent of its attention.

Other names can come out differently, because the head matches characters and not whole names. `Clark` ends in the same `k` as `Mark`. So the head lands mostly on the space after the second `Mark`. The Words view counts that space as part of `Clark`. A name such as `Davis` makes the head park much of its weight on the sentence’s first character. The Words view gives that character to the first `Mark`. That looks like what Going deeper calls an attention sink, a parking spot for spare weight. It is not a link to `Mark`.

Even the `Jones` route is only a lead worth testing. It is not proof that the model used it, and Where it breaks explains why.

## What to notice

- **Every row adds up to 100 percent**, so routes compete. Making one connection stronger weakens the others in that row, as you saw when you edited one key.
- **The grid is a triangle, not a square.** Each character can look at itself and earlier characters, but never ahead. Attention lets the model practise on every position in one run, and this rule keeps that practice fair. Setting **Mask** to *Bidirectional* fills the empty corner.
- **The On spaces readout needs careful reading.** With `it` as the query, Layer 1 head 2 puts 99.1 percent on spaces. That happens only because the head always looks two characters back. Two characters back from the `t` in `it` is the space in front of the word. For `because`, the same head puts 1.3 percent on spaces. Layer 2 head 4 puts 61.7 percent on spaces for `it`. So a big On spaces number does not mean a head is interested in spaces. It can be a side effect of where a word ends and how far back the head looks.
- **Layer 1’s heads look a fixed distance back.** Head 2 looks 2 characters back, and heads 3 and 4 look 1 character back. Head 1 puts about two thirds of its weight 3 characters back. Layer 2 looks different on the same text. Its heads have their own learned numbers, and they read vectors that Layer 1 has already changed.
- **The division by 8 is not a small detail.** With the model’s own setting, the **Score** and **Scale** lines differ only by that same factor. Without it, many rows in a head such as Layer 2 head 2 pile almost everything onto one character. With the division, a row piles up like that only where a head has learned big scores on purpose. Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 have.
- **Emptying the packets changes nothing in the strip and everything in the blend.** Keys decide who is read, and values decide what is read.

## Where it breaks

### Where the desk picture stops matching

The desk story explains routing well, but routing is all it explains. Real slips and labels have no words written on them. They are lists of learned numbers whose meaning nobody chose. A strong match between a slip and a label says nothing about what is in the packet handed over. Real desks would also come with seat numbers. Attention gets them only because the model adds them on purpose.

### A thick arc is not an explanation

The biggest caution in this lesson is about how you read these pictures. A thick arc means one position’s packet got a large weight in one word’s blend. That holds for one head in one layer. It does not tell you what that packet contained. It does not tell you whether the next step kept it, or whether a later layer cancelled it. It also does not tell you whether the final answer depended on it at all. The `Jones` route showed this already, since the packet it points at could not even hold the name.

A route is a place where information could flow. It is not evidence that the information mattered. To show that a part of the model matters, you need an experiment. It changes the model on purpose and then measures the effect. One such experiment is switching a head off and checking whether the predictions of the model get worse. Tests like that are the subject of **Interpretability II: circuits**.

### Copying, or a habit?

Picture a game where someone reads out a long string of letters that repeats. Two friends race to call out each next letter. The first friend looks back along the string for the last place the current letter appeared. That friend calls out the letter that came after it. The second friend has a habit instead, and always calls out the letter from 7 places back.

Suppose the string repeats every 8 letters. Both friends are right every time once the pattern has gone round once. Say the letters are ABCDEFGH repeated, and you have now heard the second C. The first friend finds the earlier C and calls out the D that followed it. Counting 7 back from the second C goes B, A, H, G, F, E, D. So the habit friend also calls out D, which is exactly what comes next.

Now change the string so it repeats every 9 letters, as ABCDEFGHI. Counting 7 back from the second C now lands on E, which is wrong. The first friend still finds the earlier C and calls out D, so only the first friend keeps winning.

Heads in the model can behave like either friend. The lab lets you run the same test on them. A head with a fixed-distance habit looks like a copier whenever the repeat length happens to match its distance. The reliable way to tell them apart is to change the repeat length and watch whether the stripes follow.

The game stops matching the model in two places. A head does not call out anything itself. It only puts weight on a position, and the rest of the model turns that into a guess. Also, nobody can call the letters on the first pass through the string, because no earlier copy exists yet.

Press **Repeats every 8**, which loads eight random letters repeated four times and switches to the Characters view. In Layer 2, heads 2 and 3 show stripes 7, 15 and 23 characters back. They put 94 and 88 percent of their weight on earlier copies of the character that comes next. The one-way rule still hides that next character. So the heads find where the same letter appeared before. By the last copy of the string, the pattern has already appeared three times. That is why there are three stripes.

Now press **Repeats every 9** and watch the same two heads. Their stripes move to 8, 17 and 26 characters back. Head 2 now puts 95 percent on earlier copies of the next character, and head 3 puts 89 percent. Less than 1 percent is left at the old distances. These heads act like the first friend, because they look for what followed last time, wherever it is.

The other six heads do not copy. None of them puts more than 11 percent on earlier copies of the next character at either repeat length. The four heads of Layer 1 keep the same stripes both times. Head 1 looks 3 characters back, head 2 looks 2 back, and heads 3 and 4 look 1 back. Those are fixed-distance habits, like the second friend’s, and only the learned seat numbers make them possible.

A head that copies like this is called an **induction head**. A head that looks at the character right before the current one is called a **previous-token head**. Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 are previous-token heads. Here a token means one piece of text, which in this model is a single character.

In the usual explanation of a two-layer model, the two kinds of head work as a pair. Take “Mark Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” again. A previous-token head in Layer 1 looks one character back from every spot. So the space after the first `Mark` picks up a note. Roughly, it says “the character before me was `k`”. The second `Mark` reaches its last letter, `k`. The slip of the induction head then asks for a spot whose previous character was `k`. The space after the first `Mark` carries exactly that label. So the head reads it, and that space is where `Jones` begins.

This lab has not proven that wiring, as the next part explains.

### What this model shows, and what it does not

The model was given a test built so that a fixed-distance habit would fail it. It repeats a random string once. The length is one the model never practised on: 11, 13, 17 or 19 characters. The test then measures how much weight each head puts on the character right after the earlier match.

To read the results, you need one more idea, which is how the error of the model gets measured. When the model guesses, it does not pick one character. Instead, it gives each of its 66 symbols a chance. It might give 60 percent to `e` and 10 percent to `a`. The error is a score called **loss**. It is higher when the model is more surprised by the character that actually comes next. Being surprised here means the model had given the correct character only a small chance.

Lower loss is better, and it is measured in a unit called a nat. To turn nats into something you can picture, raise 2.718 to the power of the loss. The answer is roughly how many equally likely characters the model is choosing among. A blind guess gives all 66 symbols an equal chance. It scores about 4.19 nats, because 2.718 raised to 4.19 is about 66.

Here is what the test shows about the two copying heads and the rest of the model:

- On random strings, Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 83 and 81 percent on the right character. The right character here is the one after the earlier match. These figures come from the worst of the four repeat lengths. The other lengths score at least as well. A head with a fixed 7-back habit would score near zero. None of these repeat lengths puts that character 7 back.
- Now take pieces of held-out Shakespeare, each repeated once in the same way and never seen in practice. There the same two heads put 40 and 51 percent on that character.
- Every other head stays under 6 percent on random strings, whatever the repeat length.
- On random strings, the loss on the first copy is about 4.3, a little worse than a blind guess. The likely cause is that the model still bets on characters common in Shakespeare. A random string does not favour them. On the second copy the loss falls to between 0.13 and 0.25. The model is then nearly certain of each next character.
- On Shakespeare text, the loss on the first copy is about 1.6 or 1.7. On the second copy it falls to between 0.39 and 0.53.
- Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 put 99 percent or more of their weight on the previous character. That is the information a copying head in Layer 2 would need from the layer below.

Here is what the test does not show, and these gaps matter as much as the results:

- It does not show **the wiring**, because nobody traced which Layer 1 head feeds which Layer 2 head. Switching a head off is a blunt test. It removes everything the head adds to the model, not one connection. Switching off Layer 2 head 3 makes the loss on the second copy more than ten times higher. Suppose the copying heads relied on a previous-token head. Then switching that head off should hurt clearly more than switching off a head that does something else. Instead, switching off Layer 1 head 2 raises the loss about as much as switching off head 4. Head 2 is not a previous-token head, and head 4 is one. So this test cannot tell which head feeds which.
- It says nothing about **big models**. This model has about 1.4 million parameters and reads characters. It learned from the plays of one author plus the made-up repeats. So it cannot tell you how big chatbots pick up a pattern from a few examples in your message. Researchers call that in-context learning.
- It does not explain **the other heads**. Layer 1 head 1 and Layer 2 heads 1 and 4 are shown in the lab but not explained.
- It is not **a full explanation**. An attention pattern on its own is evidence about routing and nothing more.

### Other limits of this lab

The model trained for 8,000 steps, which added up to about 23 passes over its practice text. That practice text was the 1.0 million characters in the first 90 percent of the plays. On the held-out last tenth of Shakespeare, its loss is 1.54 nats per character. A blind guess scores 4.19. Since 2.718 raised to 1.54 is about 4.7, that is like picking among 5 equally likely characters. That is far better than guessing, though still a long way from certain. About 30 percent of its practice windows were made-up repeated strings, so the model was shown repetition on purpose.

The three switches are experiments on a model that has already finished training, which limits what they can show:

- **Mask** lifts the rule for one head of a model that was trained with the rule on. That head never practised scoring later characters. In Layer 2, its slips and labels are still built from a Layer 1 that kept the rule. The rest of the model does not change.
- **Score scaling** turns off the division for one head of a model that learned with it on. So it shows that head’s rows piling onto one character, which the lab calls saturation. It does not show what training without the division would have produced.
- **Value vectors (V)** changes the lab’s blend for one head. It does not change the model’s answer, because the emptied blend never runs through the rest of the model.

Input stops at 64 characters, which is the window length the model trained on. The Words view adds up characters, while big chat models read pieces of words called tokens. So the word totals of the lab are not real tokens. The editable chips show only the first four numbers of lists that are 64 numbers long. Once you edit them, the shares come from your numbers and not the numbers of the model.

### In real systems

Full attention compares every position with every other position. So the work grows with the square of the text’s length. Double the length of a text and there are about four times as many pairs to score. Make it ten times longer and there are about a hundred times as many, which adds up fast. That one fact drives most of the engineering behind models that read very long texts.

**A common mix-up:** Attention in a model is not the same as human focus or attention span. A thick arc or dark cell is also not proof that one word caused the answer of the model. Even a link that follows the content, like `Mark` to `Jones`, is one weight in a weighted average. It is not a reason or a decision.

## Going deeper

### Many heads, one output

Real models run several attention heads side by side. The **Attention head** slider lets you step through them here. Their blends are joined end to end and passed through one more learned mixing step, called the **output projection**. That step decides how the fetched information gets written back into the model. The result is added into a running record that each position carries through the model. It is called the residual stream, and **The transformer block** follows it.

Splitting the work into heads costs little. The full vectors of this model are 256 numbers long, split into four heads of 64 numbers each. Four heads of 64 cost about the same arithmetic as one head of 256. In return, you get four separate ways to route information.

### Positions in bigger models

Most current models use the twist (RoPE) and not the learned seat numbers of this lab. To stretch such a model to longer inputs, you adjust how far each seat turns the slips and labels. **Modern attention at scale** covers both ideas.

### Saving work while writing

A model writes text one piece at a time. The keys and values of earlier pieces never change. Under the one-way rule, they never read the new pieces. So the model can save them and reuse them, instead of recalculating them for every new piece of text. That saved store is the subject of **Inference & KV cache**. There K stands for keys and V stands for values.

**Grouped-query attention** makes that store smaller by letting several query heads share one set of keys and values. It gives up a small amount of quality in return for a large saving in memory. Other methods, such as FlashAttention, work out exactly the same softmax in small blocks. The full grid of scores then never has to be stored at once.

### Induction heads in research

The copying heads you met are called induction heads, and Olsson and colleagues studied them in 2022. Their paper is titled *In-context Learning and Induction Heads*. It describes such a head by two behaviours. First, the head looks back for the spot right after an earlier copy of the current token. Second, it copies, which makes the token found there more likely to come next.

In a two-layer model, they found an induction head paired with a previous-token head. These are the same two kinds you saw here. In larger models, they saw a sudden change early in training. Induction heads formed then, while the models got better at in-context learning. They describe their evidence as preliminary and indirect. It suggests that these heads drive most of that learning. But it is early work and does not prove the link directly.

### Attention sinks

Some heads park most of their attention on the very first token. They do this when they have nothing useful to fetch elsewhere. A position that soaks up weight like this is called an **attention sink**. You may have seen something like it if you typed a name such as `Davis` earlier. This matters in practice. Deleting that position from the saved store of keys and values makes the model’s output worse.

### Where the division came from

Dividing by the square root of the head dimension comes from the original Transformer paper. Vaswani and colleagues wrote it in 2017. The authors suspected that large scores push softmax into a corner where one share is nearly everything. In that corner, small changes to the scores barely move the shares. So training gets almost no signal about which way to adjust them.

For example, say the top share is already 99.9 percent. Nudging its score up or down a little barely changes any share. Training can then hardly tell whether a nudge helped or hurt, so learning would become very slow. That is the problem the division was meant to prevent.

The lab’s **Score scaling** switch shows the corner, but not the slow learning, which would only appear during training. The lab never trains the model.

### Bidirectional here, and a real encoder

The **Bidirectional** setting in this lab is an experiment on a decoder-only model, not an encoder. A real encoder, such as a masked language model, is trained from the start with every position visible. As a result, it learns different queries and keys from any that this model ever made.
