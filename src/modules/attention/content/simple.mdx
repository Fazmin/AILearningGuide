## A word that needs another word

Read this sentence from the lab: “The animal did not cross the street because it was tired.” Who was tired? You knew at once that `it` means the animal and not the street. You did that by linking one word to another word several places earlier.

A language model is the kind of program behind a chatbot. It has to make links like this for every word it reads. To guess what comes next, a word often needs information from somewhere else in the text. It might need the name that a pronoun stands for. It might need the opening bracket that a closing bracket belongs to. It might need a pattern that a few earlier examples set up. The part of the model that fetches this information is called **attention**.

Here is the plan: first you will build attention yourself, one piece at a time. Each new piece fixes a problem with the piece before it. Then you will turn around and question the machine you built. You will test it the way a careful scientist tests a result that looks too neat.

Hold on to one warning, because it comes back near the end. In the small model in this lab, the line from `it` to `animal` is always thin. No part of the model makes it the strongest link. No part even gives `animal` 10 percent of the attention of `it`. Yet you made that link instantly! Working out why is part of the lesson, and it shows that these pictures need careful reading.

### Why it is worth building

Most of today’s chat models are the same kind of model as the tiny one in this lab. They read text from left to right. They learned by guessing the next piece of text over and over. In a model like this, attention is the only step that lets one word take information from another. Without it, every word would be stuck with what it knew about itself and its place in the line. Its guesses about what comes next would be far worse.

Attention also changed how models are trained. It lets a model practise guessing at every spot in a long text at the same time. You will see later why that helped make very large models practical to train.

By the end you’ll be able to:

- Tell apart the query, the key and the value that every word makes. Explain why the query and key decide where information comes from, while the value carries it.
- Read a picture of attention without reading too much into it. A thick line shows where information could flow, not why the model answered as it did.
- Tell a part of the model that follows content from one that always looks a fixed distance back.
- Explain what the one-way rule changes, since it stops a word from reading later words. Also explain how models that read in both directions differ from this one.
- Say why each match score gets divided by 8 before it becomes a share. Also say why 8 is the right number here, and what goes wrong without that step.

The lab runs a real, very small model with two layers. It was trained on the plays of Shakespeare. With the model’s own settings, the numbers you see are measured straight from that model. They do not come from a cartoon of how attention might work.

Each was a short string repeated over and over. If the model fails to load, the lab shows stand-in numbers that mean nothing, and it tells you so.

### Follow one word

The lab opens on the same sentence. The word `it` is already chosen as the **query**, which means the word whose search you are following. Before any theory, look at the **Model attention routing diagram** card. Curved lines called arcs fan out from `it` to the other words. A thicker arc means `it` takes more from that word.

**Pause and predict:** which word do you expect to get the thickest arc from `it`? Make your guess, then read the **strongest route** in the readout strip under the arcs.

The lab starts on Layer 2 head 2, and there the strongest route goes to `because`, not to `animal`. A few other things are worth seeing while you are here. Later words get only a faint, thin line, because the model may not read ahead. The query has no arc to itself, so its own weight shows only in the bar under its chip. The strip also names the **inspected key**. It also shows how much of the row lands **on spaces**, a number to read with care.

The picture already disagrees with your instinct. So we need to know exactly what an arc is. The best way to learn that is to build one.

## Build it yourself, one fix at a time

Inside the model, every character of your sentence is stored as a long list of numbers. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**. It is a bit like a player’s stats in a sports game, where each number rates one skill. But nobody decided what the model’s numbers stand for. The model chose them itself during **training**. Training is a long practice period where the model guesses the next character over and over. After each round, it nudges all its numbers a little.

This model reads characters, meaning letters, spaces and punctuation marks. So every character gets its own vector. The lab’s **Words** view adds characters up into words. It counts the space in front of a word as part of that word. A word’s row is the row of its last character. So the row for `it` is really its `t` row, the first spot to see the whole word. Every character that row can see belongs to exactly one word. So a row’s word totals still add up to exactly 100 percent.

Now picture that `t` before attention runs. Its vector says only something like “I am a `t`, and I sit at this seat in the line”. Nothing in it says that an animal appeared earlier. Nothing says that someone is tired, or even that the letter before it was an `i`. It is like a group chat where the newest message says “she said yes!!” and nothing else. You have to scroll back up to find out who “she” is. Our job is to give every word a way to scroll back.

### First try: average everything

Let’s start with the simplest thing that could work. The word `it` needs information from earlier words. So what if it took the average of all of them? Picture what `it` would receive: equal parts `the`, `animal`, `did`, `not`, `cross`, `the`, `street` and `because`. The animal is in there, but so is the street, at exactly the same volume.

Averaging can’t choose, because it gives every earlier word the same say, whether or not that word helps. The one useful word is drowned out by all the others. We need a way for `it` to say what it is looking for. Then it can pick out the words that offer it.

So here is our first fix: score every earlier word on how well it matches what `it` wants.

### Score the matches

How do you score a match between two lists of numbers? The model uses a calculation called the **dot product**, and it has two steps. First you multiply the two lists position by position. Then you add up all the answers. Try it with four numbers in each list. The searching list is (2, −1, 0, 3) and the other list is (1, 2, 5, 1):

- first position: 2 × 1 = 2
- second position: −1 × 2 = −2
- third position: 0 × 5 = 0
- fourth position: 3 × 1 = 3

Adding the four answers gives a score of 3. The −2 cancels the 2 and leaves the 3. When the two numbers in a position have the same sign, they raise the score. When their signs are opposite, they lower it. So a high score means the two lists agree in many positions. That is what a good match looks like in numbers.

In the lab, the searching list is called the **query**. The list it is checked against is called the **key**. For now, treat both as the word’s own vector. A few steps from now you will see why the model makes them different. The real lists are 64 numbers long. So the **Score** line in the **Query key value inspector** card adds up 64 products, not four. It shows the total next to `q · k`, which is how the lab writes this dot product.

In one sentence: a dot product turns “how well do these two lists agree?” into one number. But a score can be any size, even negative. You can’t blend words by amounts like that. We need to turn the scores into shares.

### Turn scores into shares

We want shares that are all positive and add up to 100 percent. The tool that does this is called **softmax**. The lab writes its formula as `exp(s) ÷ Σ exp(s)`. In that formula, `s` is one score. The part `exp(s)` means a special number, about 2.718, raised to the power `s`. The Greek letter `Σ`, sigma, means “add this up for every character the query can see”. In words, softmax turns every score into a positive number. Then it divides each one by the total of them all.

Try it with three scores, 2, 1 and 0. First turn each one into a positive number:

- The score 2 becomes 2.718 × 2.718, which is about 7.39.
- The score 1 becomes 2.718 itself, which rounds to 2.72.
- The score 0 becomes exactly 1, because any number raised to the power 0 equals 1.

Those three numbers add up to about 11.11. Dividing by that total gives each score its share. The top score gets 7.39 ÷ 11.11, which is about 66.5 percent of the row. The middle score gets about 24.5 percent and the bottom score about 9 percent. Together the three shares make 100 percent. Hold on to these three shares, because they come back.

Negative scores work too, and real scores are often negative. A power below zero means dividing instead of multiplying. The score −1 becomes 1 ÷ 2.718, which is about 0.37. The score −2 becomes 1 ÷ 7.39, which is about 0.14. The more negative the score, the closer the result gets to zero, but it never reaches zero. Scores with decimals land in between, so a score of 1.5 becomes about 4.48.

Two features of softmax matter for everything that follows. First, every share is positive, because 2.718 raised to any power is above zero. So even a poor match gets a sliver of the row. Second, softmax cares a lot about the gaps between scores. Each step of 1 multiplies instead of adding. A gap of 1 makes one share about 2.7 times the other. A gap of 2 makes it about 2.7 × 2.7, or 7.4, times the other. A gap of 8 means multiplying by 2.7 eight times over, which comes to about 3,000.

These shares are called **attention weights**. Each one tells you how much of one word ends up in the query’s blend. You have now done by hand what the model does. So let’s check that the model really does it the same way.

### Check the arithmetic for one pair

Click a cell in the **Attention matrix** grid, where darker cells mean bigger weights. Each cell’s row is a query word and its column is a key word. So one cell describes one pair of words. Clicking sets both words at once and jumps the stages to `Score`. The **Query key value inspector** card then shows that pair’s arithmetic, so now step to `Softmax`.

**Pause and predict:** the **Softmax** line redoes one pair’s share from all 64 numbers of the query and keys. Then it prints the model’s own figure beside it. How close do you expect the two to be?

They agree to within about one part in a million. Next to the line, the lab shows the largest gap anywhere in the row. It is written in a short form such as 2.4e-7, which means 0.00000024. It is how you know you are looking at the real arithmetic, not a drawing of it.

The strip in the inspector draws the whole row twice. The top band shows each score as a bar. The bar rises above its zero line for a positive score and drops below it for a negative one. The bottom band shows each share as a bar, and shares are never negative. The band titles write the head dimension as `d`. So `√d` means √64, which is 8, a number you will meet again soon.

Two readouts look alike but measure different things. **Selected score** is the score for one pair of characters. Those are the last character of the query word and the last character of the inspected word. **Selected weight** is the share for the whole inspected word, all its characters plus the space in front. That is the number the grid cell shows. A single character can get a tiny share while its word’s total is large. That happens because the weight is spread across the word’s characters. If you prefer numbers to shades, open **View weights as text** under the grid.

### Shares compete

Every share in a row is found by dividing by the same total, and that raises a question. **Pause and predict:** if you push up one word’s score, what happens to the other words’ shares?

To try it, use the `Q` and `K` chips in the inspector. You can edit them, and they show the first four numbers of the query and the inspected key. Type a different number into one of the four `K` boxes. Then watch the arcs, the bars under the word chips, or the list under **View weights as text**.

Every share in the row moves, not only the one for the word you edited. When one score changes, the total changes. And every share is divided by that total. That is why a row always adds up to 100 percent, and why routes compete. One link can only grow stronger if others in its row grow weaker.

The strip is a poor place to watch this. It draws every share bar as a fraction of the tallest one. So the other bars keep their heights unless the bar you changed is, or becomes, the tallest.

Which way a score moves depends on the query too. Raising one number in `K` changes the score by the matching number in `Q` times the amount you added. Where that `Q` number is positive, raising the `K` number raises the score. Where it is negative, raising the `K` number lowers it. Say the matching `Q` number is 0.5 and you add 2 to the `K` number. The score rises by 0.5 × 2, which is 1. **Selected score** shows the change after a division by 8 you will meet soon, so it rises by 0.125.

Only the first four of the 64 numbers can be edited. The other 60 stay as the model made them. Press **Match the model vectors** to switch back to the model’s own numbers. The lab remembers what you typed, though. If you type in a box again, your earlier changes come back.

So the shares are a fixed budget that the words compete for, and now we can spend it.

### Blend by the shares

With shares in hand, we can finally do what averaging did badly. We can blend the words, with each one counting exactly as much as its share. A blend like this is called a **weighted average**. You have probably met one in a class grade. Say a test counts for 75 percent of your grade and a quiz for the other 25 percent. You score 80 on the test and 100 on the quiz. Three quarters of 80 is 60, and one quarter of 100 is 25. So your grade works out to 85.

Attention does the same thing with whole lists of numbers instead of single marks. Suppose the query gives 75 percent of its weight to a list holding (4, 0). It gives 25 percent to a list holding (0, 8). Three quarters of the first is (3, 0), and one quarter of the second is (0, 2). Adding them position by position gives a blend of (3, 2). The lab shows the first four numbers of the real blend, out of 64, in the **Mixed value** chip.

The **Mix length** readout turns the whole blend into one number. It says how big the blend’s numbers are overall. To get it, square every number in the blend, add up the squares, and take the square root. For the blend (3, 2), that is √(9 + 4), which is about 3.6. The readout is 0 only when every number in the blend is 0.

Score, share, blend: that is a working recipe, but look at what we blended. It was the same vector that we scored the match against. That turns out to be a problem.

### One vector can’t do two jobs

So far each word uses one vector for two very different jobs. It has to be found by the right searches. Once found, it has to hand over something useful for the guess. What makes a word a good match is not the same as what is worth handing over. So one list of numbers is stuck trying to do both.

The fix is to split it, so the model makes a **key** from each word’s starting vector. Training shapes the key for being matched. The model also makes a separate **value**, shaped for being useful once it arrives. There is a third job as well. The searching word must describe what it wants, and that differs from what it is. So it also makes a **query**. All three are vectors made from the same starting vector. Three separate sets of learned numbers make them, like three separate recipes.

Picture a classroom where every word sits at its own desk, in order from left to right. Each desk holds three items:

- A **search slip** says what this word is looking for, and that is its query.
- A **label** on the front of the desk shows what this word can offer, and that is its key.
- A **packet** in the drawer holds the information that gets handed over, and that is its value.

Every word compares its search slip with the label on every desk it may see, including its own. Good matches score high, and the scores become shares. Then the word receives a blend of everyone’s packets. Each packet counts as much as its share. Here is the whole split in one line: **Q and K choose who, V is what.**

This is where the desk picture stops matching. Real slips and labels have no words written on them. They are lists of learned numbers, and nobody chose what they mean. A strong match between a slip and a label tells you nothing about what is in the packet. Real desks would also have seat numbers. Attention gets them only because the model adds them on purpose. We will come to that gap in a moment.

### Zero the values

If the split is real, you should be able to empty every packet without moving a single route. Set **Value vectors (V)** to *Zero all*. That replaces every value with a list of zeros.

**Pause and predict:** what will happen to the arcs and the strip? What will happen to the **Mixed value** chip?

Every attention weight stays exactly where it was. But the **Mixed value** chip drops to all zeros, and **Mix length** falls to 0. The query and the keys chose the routes, and the values travelled along them. So emptying the values changes what arrives, but not where it comes from.

For a finer test, click a dark cell in the grid, so the inspected word has a large share. Then switch the control between *Model* and *Zero word*. *Zero word* empties only the inspected word’s values, including the value for the space in front of it. The **Mixed value** numbers and **Mix length** change, but the shares stay put. The blend loses exactly that word’s part and nothing else. When you are finished, set the control back to *Model*.

That is the cleanest proof of the split. Choosing the route and carrying the content are separate jobs, done by separate lists.

### What the recipe can’t see: order

Look back at the desk story and you will find a gap. Nothing on a label says which desk it sits at. Shuffle the desks and every match keeps the same score. So attention on its own could not tell “the dog bit the man” from “the man bit the dog”. The one-way rule you will meet soon leaks a little information about order. A word near the start sees only a few words, but a word near the end sees many. **The transformer block** lab measures how much.

Models close the gap by giving every desk its seat number in some form. Then position becomes part of the calculation. Any way of doing this is called a **positional encoding**, and four kinds are common:

- **Learned seat numbers** (used by GPT-2, an older text-writing model, and by this lab’s model). The model learns one list of numbers for each seat. It adds that list to the vector of whatever character sits there. There is no list past the last seat, so the longest input is fixed, at 64 characters here.
- **Wave-pattern seat numbers** (used by the Transformer, the 2017 design that first built a model around attention). Each seat gets a fixed pattern made from waves, with nothing to learn. Models with this kind tend to work poorly on texts longer than the ones they trained on.
- **A distance penalty** (ALiBi) uses no seat numbers. Instead, the match score drops more the farther apart two words are, so nearby words tend to win.
- **A twist** (RoPE, used by Llama models and many other recent ones). Each slip and label is turned by an amount set by its seat, like turning a clock hand. Then the seat part of the match depends only on how far apart the two words sit. **Modern attention at scale** shows how it works.

You can see the first kind at work in this model’s heads. A head can prefer a fixed distance back only if its slips and labels carry the seat number. Some Layer 1 heads do exactly that. They look 1, 2 or 3 characters back on every text this lesson tries. They put 66 to 100 percent of their weight at that distance. If the learned seat numbers are wiped to zero, that share falls to under 6 percent.

So order is not built into attention. It has to be added, and this model adds it with learned seat numbers.

### The five buttons are the five steps you just built

You have now built the whole recipe. The lab names its steps on five buttons in the first card: `Query`, `Keys`, `Score`, `Softmax` and `Mix`. Press them in order, and the **Query key value inspector** highlights each step in turn:

- `Query`: the word you clicked makes its search slip. The `Q` chip shows its first four numbers.
- `Keys`: every word the query may see shows its label. The `K` chip shows the key of the inspected word. You choose that word by clicking a cell in the **Attention matrix**.
- `Score`: the query is compared with every key by a dot product on the **Score** line. Then every score is divided by 8 on the **Scale** line, written `score ÷ √64`. Each list is 64 numbers long, and √64 is 8.
- `Softmax`: the scaled scores become shares that are all positive and add up to 100 percent.
- `Mix`: the values are blended by those shares into the **Mixed value**.

Wait, though: we never built that divide-by-8 step, and the recipe worked without it. So why is it there? Hold that question, because it gets its own section after this one.

### Many heads, many questions

One complete set of the three recipes, with its own learned numbers, is called an **attention head**. One head asks one kind of question of the sentence. But a word may need several things at once. So this model runs four heads side by side. Each head makes its own slips, labels and packets from the same text. Running several heads like this is called **multi-head attention**. It lets one sentence be routed in different ways at once. Each head’s lists are 64 numbers long, and that length is called the **head dimension**.

The model also has two layers, one after the other. So attention happens twice on the way through. Layer 1 works on the character vectors as they come in. It passes updated vectors on to Layer 2, whose four heads then do their own fetching. That makes eight heads in all, and you choose one with the **Layer** buttons and the **Attention head** slider. They sit at the top of the **Query key value inspector** card. The grid of weights for one head in one layer is called an **attention pattern**. The **Attention matrix** card draws it.

**Pause and predict:** as you change heads, will every head send `it` to the same place?

**Try this:** Keep `it` as the query. Move the **Attention head** slider through all four heads with **Layer** set to *Layer 1*. Then do the same with *Layer 2*. The sentence never changes, so watch only the arcs and the **strongest route**.

The routes change from head to head. In Layer 1, head 1 sends most of the weight of `it` to `because`. Heads 2, 3 and 4 pile it onto `it` itself, for a reason you will uncover later. In Layer 2, the four heads’ strongest routes go to `it`, `because`, `cross` and `it`. Each head has its own learned numbers, so each one routes the sentence its own way. Still, two heads can learn almost the same habit. Layer 2 also looks different from Layer 1. Its heads read vectors that Layer 1 has already changed.

That is one sentence with eight different sets of routes. Now that you have built the machine, let’s break it. We will start with the step we skipped.

## A bug you’d hit: scores that are too loud

Remember the divide-by-8 step on the **Scale** line? The recipe you built worked without it. So let’s find out what goes wrong if you leave it out.

Go back to the three scores from earlier, 2, 1 and 0. They gave shares of about 66.5, 24.5 and 9 percent. Now make them 8 times bigger, so they become 16, 8 and 0. **Pause and predict:** do the shares stay spread out and get a little more lopsided? Or does one score take nearly everything?

One score takes nearly everything: the top score now gets more than 99.9 percent of the row. That leaves almost nothing for the other two. The reason is that softmax multiplies by about 2.7 for every step of gap. When one character takes 99 percent or more of a row, the lab calls that row **saturated**. A saturated row has stopped blending, because it hands over one value and ignores almost everything else.

So scores that are too loud break the blend. But why would real scores be 8 times too loud in the first place?

### Why divide by 8?

Picture a game where you flip a coin 64 times. You add 1 to your total for every heads and take away 1 for every tails. Most of the flips cancel each other out. So your total almost never ends up anywhere near 64. In about three games out of four, it lands within 8 of zero, on one side or the other. It rarely lands more than 16 away.

Now change the number of flips: with 16 flips, the total usually lands within about 4 of zero. With 256 flips, it usually lands within about 16. How far from zero the total usually lands is called its spread. Four times as many flips only doubles the spread. That is the square-root pattern: √16 = 4, √64 = 8 and √256 = 16.

A dot product acts the same way if both lists hold numbers about size 1 with random-looking signs. Each of the 64 positions adds a product of about size 1. Sometimes it is positive and sometimes negative, like one coin flip. So the score usually lands within about 8 of zero. Lists of 256 numbers would give scores that usually land within about 16. Dividing by the square root of the head dimension brings the typical score back to about 1. That works however long the lists are.

A dot product divided by the square root of the head dimension is called a **scaled dot product**. That pattern holds for lists of random-looking numbers. But a trained head can still learn bigger scores on purpose. Hold on to that, because you will see it in a moment.

The division also has a history, from the original Transformer paper by Vaswani and colleagues in 2017. The authors suspected that large scores push softmax into a corner where one share is nearly everything. In that corner, nudging a score barely moves any share. So training gets almost no signal about which way to adjust it, and learning would slow down.

### Switch off the scaling

Now test the idea on the real model. Use the pronoun sentence in Layer 2 head 2, where the lab starts, and watch two readouts. **Largest weight** is the biggest share any single character gets in the current row. **Saturated rows** counts the rows in this head’s whole grid where one character gets 99 percent or more.

**Pause and predict:** with the division on, how many rows do you expect to be saturated? Now guess again for when you set **Score scaling** to *No scaling*. Then the scores reaching softmax are 8 times bigger.

With the division, no row is saturated. Without it, 26 of 55 rows are saturated. The count is out of 55 because the sentence has 56 characters and the first one is left out. That first character can only see itself. So it always gives itself 100 percent and would tell you nothing.

Now set the control back to *÷ √64* and move to Layer 1 head 3 or head 4. **Saturated rows** still counts most of the 55 rows, even with the division on. These are the trained heads from a moment ago. They pile onto the previous character on purpose. That is how they put 99.9 percent of `it` on the `i` before its `t`. So the division does not ban saturation. All it does is stop scores from growing because the lists are long. The trouble it prevents is saturation that nobody asked for.

Be honest about what you have seen. The switch shows the corner, but not the slow learning. Slow learning would appear only during training, and the lab never trains the model. Leave the control on *÷ √64*, so the next experiments use the model’s own setting.

In one sentence: without the division, long lists make scores loud and rows saturate. With it, a row saturates only where a head has learned to.

## How a model could cheat, and the rule that stops it

So far, a word has only looked at itself and the words before it. Why not the words after it too? To answer that, you need to know how this model practised. It learned by guessing each next character in a 64-character piece of text, called a window. It saw the whole window at once.

**Pause and predict:** say you were training this model to guess the next character. Every spot can see the whole window. What is the laziest way to score perfectly?

It could read the answer, which is sitting right there in the window, one seat to the right. Say the model reads “the cat”, so the spot holding `c` has to guess that `a` comes next. Without a rule, the `c` could read that `a` and copy it. Picture a practice quiz where the answer to each question is printed right underneath it. You could score 100 percent by copying, and you would learn nothing at all.

### The one-way rule

The fix is a rule. A word may read itself and the words before it, but never the words after it. This rule is called the **causal mask**. It is a mask because it covers up later words. It is causal because each word may only use what came earlier, as a cause comes before its effect.

The mask changes the scores before softmax runs. It sets the score for every later character to minus infinity, written −∞, lower than any number. It makes the one exception to “every share is positive”. The number 2.718 raised to −∞ shrinks all the way to zero. So a later character’s share is exactly zero. The lab draws those blocked spots with diagonal stripes, called hatching. The **Scale** line says `masked` if you inspect one.

Each row can only reach back, so the **Attention matrix** grid is a triangle, not a square. The diagonal runs from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner. That is where each character meets itself. The corner above it, where later characters would sit, stays empty. The diagonal also lets you read distances, which you will need later. In the Characters view, dark cells one cell left of the diagonal mean each character looks 1 back. A stripe 7 cells to the left means each character looks 7 back.

Here is the clever part. Without the mask, the only fair way to practise would be 64 separate runs. Each run would show only the text up to the spot it has to guess. With the mask, the model takes in the whole window in one run. Every spot still has to guess its next character without seeing it. So one run gives 64 fair guesses to learn from. Attention works out those 64 guesses side by side, and the mask keeps them honest.

That was a big change from older language models, called recurrent models. They read text strictly in order and squeezed everything they had read into one memory of fixed size. That is a bit like remembering a whole book by rewriting one index card after every page. The details get crowded out, and you cannot start page 50 until you have finished page 49. The powerful chips used to train models are built to do huge numbers of small sums side by side. Attention finally let language models use those chips fully. That is a large part of why training on huge amounts of text became practical.

In one sentence: the mask hides every answer from the spot that has to guess it. That is what makes all-at-once practice fair.

### Lift the one-way rule

What would the heads do if they could look ahead? In the **Attention matrix** card, set **Mask** to *Bidirectional*. Now every character may read the whole sentence. The triangle fills into a square. The readout strip adds two new numbers: the share **on later characters** and the share **on the next character**.

**Pause and predict:** with `it` as the query, will every head rush to the later characters, or only some? Will any head go for the very next character, which is the answer?

It depends a lot on the head. Layer 1 head 3 moves only 0.1 percent of the weight of `it` to later characters. Head 4 moves only 0.2 percent. But Layer 2 head 4 moves 90.2 percent of its weight there. Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put about a third of the weight of `it` on the next character. Their shares are 33.8 and 30.6 percent. That next character is exactly the answer the model practises guessing. So this is the lazy trick from the start of this section, caught in the act.

Look below the diagonal too, where every share shrinks by the same factor. The earlier characters’ scores have not changed. So softmax keeps their sizes compared with each other and divides them all by a bigger total. That total now includes the later characters. Say 40 percent of a row moves to later characters, leaving 60 percent for the earlier ones. Every earlier share is then multiplied by 0.6. So a share that was 50 percent becomes 30 percent.

Set **Mask** back to *Causal* before you go on. Then the grid shows the model as it was trained.

### Two families: encoders and decoder-only models

The one-way rule is a design choice, not a law of nature. Keeping it or dropping it splits language models into families:

- A **decoder-only (causal) language model** keeps the rule. It learns to guess the next piece of text at every position. This lab’s model is one, and so are most of today’s chat models. They write by guessing one piece, adding it to the end, and running again. The name comes from an older design with two halves. An encoder read the input, and a decoder wrote the output. A decoder-only model keeps the writing half.
- An **encoder** drops the rule, so every word may look both ways and its grid is a full square. Reading in both directions like this is called bidirectional attention. Encoders use it to understand a whole text at once. That suits jobs like sorting emails into spam and not spam. It also suits finding the pages that best match a search.

An encoder cannot practise by guessing the next word, because every position could see its own answer. So it needs a different practice task. A **masked language model** is an encoder that practises by covering some words in a text with a blank. Then it guesses them from the words on both sides. A well-known model called BERT works this way. This kind of practice suits understanding text better than writing it.

Be careful with that name, because here “masked” means words hidden behind a blank, not the causal mask. A masked language model has no causal mask at all.

Here is one honest caveat about the switch you flipped. The lab’s *Bidirectional* setting is an experiment on a decoder-only model, not an encoder. A real encoder is trained from the start with every position visible. So it learns different queries and keys from any that this model ever made.

## Reading the evidence like a sceptic

You have built attention and broken it on purpose. Now it is time to settle the warning from the start. No head in this model links `it` to `animal`. Is the model failing, or are we reading the picture wrong? Here are three habits of a sceptic. First, check where the weight lands. Second, move the content and see if the route follows. Third, change the pattern to tell copying from habit.

### A route that is not what it looks like

Press the **Pronoun sentence** button in the Examples row of the first card. It reloads the example and resets the mask, the scaling and the values to the model’s own settings. Earlier, Layer 1 heads 2, 3 and 4 piled the weight of `it` onto `it` itself.

**Pause and predict:** why would a head give a word almost all its attention to itself? What do you think it is really looking at?

Set **Layer** to *Layer 1*, the **Attention head** slider to head 3, and **Matrix view** to *Characters*. You will find a dark stripe one step below the diagonal. It means every character looks at the character right before it. The lab reads `it` at its last letter, the `t`. The character right before that `t` is the `i` inside `it`. That is why heads 3 and 4 put 99.9 percent of `it` on the word `it`. They are not thinking about the word at all. They only look at the letter one seat back.

So a thick arc, or a full bar under a chip, shows where information could come from. It is not a sign that the model has worked out what `it` refers to.

Then why does no head, in either layer, reach for `animal`? One likely reason is that the lab reads `it` at its `t`. The only guess that spot has to make is the next character. Here that is a space, and the spelling of `it` already makes a space a good guess without `animal`. No head in this model shows a sign of linking `it` to `animal`. These pictures also cannot tell you how a large chatbot handles pronouns.

The same care applies to the **on spaces** readout. With `it` as the query, Layer 1 head 2 puts 99.1 percent on spaces. But that is only because this head always looks two characters back. Two characters back from the `t` in `it` is the space in front of the word. For `because`, the same head puts 1.3 percent on spaces. So a big On spaces number does not mean a head is interested in spaces. It can be a side effect of where a word ends and how far back the head looks.

That is the first habit: before you believe a word-level picture, check which character the weight really sits on.

### Move the content, see if the route follows

Here is a route that looks much more like understanding, so press **Repeated name**. It loads “Mark Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark”, with the second `Mark` as the query. Set **Layer** to *Layer 2* and move the **Attention head** slider to head 3. That head sends 92.7 percent of the second `Mark`’s weight to `Jones`. That is the word that came right after the first `Mark`.

That is a sensible place to look. At the second `Mark`, the model has to guess what comes next. Last time `Mark` appeared, `Jones` came right after it. Switch **Matrix view** to *Characters* to see exactly where the weight lands. Almost all of it sits on one character, the space right after the first `Mark`. The Words view counts that space as part of `Jones`. That is also why **On spaces** reads close to 100 percent.

Under the one-way rule, that space has seen only “Mark ” so far. So its packet cannot hold the name `Jones` at all. The head points at the spot that came right after `Mark` last time. That is the spot it would copy from to guess the next character this time.

A sceptic should still ask: is the head following the name? Or does it always look a set distance back, and the name happens to sit there? To find out, move the name and see whether the route moves with it.

**Pause and predict:** press **Name moved earlier**. It loads “Anna Blake met Mark Jones and then Mark”. The space after the earlier `Mark` is now 19 characters back instead of 34. Will the head still find `Jones`?

It does, and the share on `Jones` stays high, at 97.2 percent. A head that always looked a set distance back would have lost its target when the name moved. This route follows the content instead, because wherever the earlier `Mark` sits, the head’s weight lands right after it.

To push the test further, press **Repeated name** again. Then click at the end of the **Sentence** box at the top of the first card. Type a space and then Smith. Click the new `Smith` chip to make it the query, and keep Layer 2 head 3. There is no earlier `Smith` for the head to match. No word receives even 20 percent of its attention.

Other names can come out differently, because the head matches characters, not whole names. `Clark` ends in the same `k` as `Mark`. So the head lands mostly on the space after the second `Mark`. The Words view counts that space as part of `Clark`. A name such as `Davis` makes the head park much of its weight on the sentence’s first character. The Words view credits that to the first `Mark`. It looks like what the last section calls an attention sink. That is a parking spot for spare weight, not a link to `Mark`.

That is the second habit: move the content. A route that moves with it is reading content, and a route that stays put is reading distance. Even the `Jones` route is only a lead worth testing, though. It is not proof that the model used it.

### Copying or habit? Change the period

Moving one name is a single test. A sharper test changes a whole repeating pattern, and a game shows why. Picture someone reading out a long string of letters that repeats. Two friends race to call out each next letter. The first friend looks back for the last place the current letter appeared. Then this friend calls out the letter that came after it. The second friend has a habit instead. This friend always calls out the letter from 7 places back.

Say the string repeats every 8 letters. Once the pattern has gone round once, both friends are right every time. Say the letters are ABCDEFGH repeated, and you have heard the second C. The first friend finds the earlier C and calls out the D that followed it. Counting 7 back from the second C goes B, A, H, G, F, E, D. So the habit friend also calls out D, which is exactly what comes next.

Now change the string so it repeats every 9 letters, as ABCDEFGHI. Counting 7 back from the second C now lands on E, which is wrong. The first friend still finds the earlier C and calls out D. Only the first friend keeps winning, so changing the repeat length is how you tell them apart.

The game stops matching the model in two places. A head does not call out anything itself. It only puts weight on a position, and the rest of the model turns that into a guess. Also, nobody can call the letters the first time through the string. There is no earlier copy to look back at yet.

Press **Repeats every 8**, which loads eight random letters repeated four times and switches to the Characters view. In Layer 2, heads 2 and 3 show stripes 7, 15 and 23 characters back. They put 94 and 88 percent of their weight on earlier copies of the next character. The next character itself is still hidden by the one-way rule. So the heads find where that same letter appeared before. By the last copy, the pattern has already appeared three times. That is why there are three stripes.

**Pause and predict:** with **Repeats every 9**, will these heads act like the first friend or the second? Will their stripes stay at 7, 15 and 23 characters back, or move?

The stripes move to 8, 17 and 26 characters back. Head 2 now puts 95 percent on earlier copies of the next character, and head 3 puts 89 percent. Less than 1 percent is left at the old distances. So these heads are the first friend. They look for what followed last time, wherever it is.

Now look at Layer 1, whose four heads keep the same stripes at both repeat lengths. Head 1 puts about two thirds of its weight 3 characters back. Head 2 looks 2 back, and heads 3 and 4 look 1 back. Those are fixed-distance habits, like the second friend’s. Only the learned seat numbers make them possible. At either length, only the two copying heads put over 11 percent on earlier copies of the next character.

A head that copies like this is called an **induction head**. A head that looks at the character right before the current one is called a **previous-token head**. Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 are like that. Here a token means one piece of text, which in this model is a single character.

In the usual explanation of a two-layer model, the two kinds of head work as a pair. Take “Mark Jones met Anna Blake and then Mark” again. A previous-token head in Layer 1 looks one character back from every spot. So the space after the first `Mark` picks up a note. It says, roughly, “the character before me was `k`”. Then the second `Mark` reaches its last letter, `k`. The induction head’s slip asks for a spot whose previous character was `k`. The space after the first `Mark` carries exactly that label. So the head reads it, and that space is where `Jones` begins.

That is the third habit: change the pattern and watch whether the stripes follow it. The pairing story is neat, but this lab has not proven it, as the next section explains.

## What an attention picture can’t tell you

So what does a thick arc actually prove? Less than it seems. A thick arc means only that one position’s value got a large weight in one word’s blend. That is in one head of one layer. It does not tell you what that value contained. It does not tell you whether the next step kept it, or whether a later layer cancelled it. It also does not tell you whether the final answer depended on it at all. The `Jones` route showed this already, since the space it points at could not even hold the name.

A route is a place where information could flow, not evidence that it mattered. To show that a part of the model matters, you need an experiment. It changes the model on purpose and then measures the effect. One such test is switching a head off and checking whether the predictions get worse. Tests like that are the subject of **Interpretability II: circuits**.

### What this model demonstrates, and what it does not

The model was given a test built so that a fixed-distance habit would fail it. A random string is repeated once, at a repeat length the model never practised on. Those lengths are 11, 13, 17 or 19 characters. The test measures how much weight each head puts on the character right after the earlier match.

To read the results, you need one more idea. That is how the model’s error gets measured. When the model guesses, it does not pick one character. It gives each of its 66 symbols a chance: maybe 60 percent for `e` and 10 percent for `a`. The error is a score called **loss**. Loss is higher when the model gave the true next character only a small chance.

Lower loss is better, and it is measured in a unit called a nat. To picture nats, raise 2.718 to the power of the loss. The answer is roughly how many equally likely characters the model is choosing among. A blind guess gives all 66 symbols an equal chance. It scores about 4.19 nats, because 2.718 raised to 4.19 is about 66.

Here is what the test shows:

- Start with random strings. Layer 2 heads 2 and 3 put 83 and 81 percent on the character after the match. A head with a fixed 7-back habit would score near zero here. None of these repeat lengths puts that character 7 back.
- Every other head stays under 6 percent on random strings, whatever the repeat length.
- The copying pays off in the guesses. On random strings, the loss on the first copy is about 4.3. That is a little worse than a blind guess. On the second copy it falls to between 0.13 and 0.25. So the model is nearly certain of each next character.
- Layer 1 heads 3 and 4 put 99 percent or more of their weight on the previous character. A copying head in Layer 2 would need that from the layer below.

**How sure is this?** The 83 and 81 percent come from the worst of the four repeat lengths. So the other lengths score at least as well. The test also used pieces of held-out Shakespeare, each repeated once in the same way. There the same two heads put 40 and 51 percent on that character. Loss fell from about 1.6 or 1.7 on copy one to between 0.39 and 0.53 on copy two. On random strings, the first copy is likely a little worse than a blind guess for one reason. The model still bets on characters that are common in Shakespeare.

Here is what the test does not show. These gaps matter as much as the results:

- It does not show **the wiring**, because nobody traced which Layer 1 head feeds which Layer 2 head. Switching a head off is a blunt test. It removes everything the head adds, not one connection. Switching off Layer 2 head 3 makes the loss on the second copy more than ten times higher. Now say the copying heads relied on a previous-token head. Then switching that head off should hurt clearly more than switching off another head. But switching off Layer 1 head 2, which is not a previous-token head, hurts about as much. It raises the loss about as much as switching off Layer 1 head 4, which is one.
- It says nothing about **big models**, because this model has about 1.4 million learned numbers, called **parameters**. It reads characters, and it learned from one author’s plays plus the made-up repeats. It cannot tell you how big chatbots pick up a pattern from a few examples in your message. Researchers call that in-context learning.
- It does not explain **the other heads**. Layer 1 head 1 and Layer 2 heads 1 and 4 are shown in the lab but not explained.
- It is not **a full explanation**, because an attention pattern alone is evidence about routing and nothing more.

### Limits of this lab

The lab runs a real, very small trained model with two layers of four heads. All of its parameters were set during training. The badge at the top of the first card starts with `ONNX model` once the model is running. ONNX is the file format it is stored in. While the model loads, the badge says `Loading teaching model…`. If loading fails, it says `Model unavailable · inspectable fallback`. In both cases, the lab shows stand-in values from a fixed wave pattern with no learned meaning. A note in the **Attention matrix** card tells you so.

The model practised on Shakespeare for 8,000 rounds of training, called steps. Each step gave it a batch of windows to guess its way through. Its practice text was the 1.0 million characters in the first 90 percent of the plays. Over all those steps, it went through that text about 23 times. The last 10 percent was kept aside to test the model on lines it had never seen.

On that held-out last tenth, its loss is 1.54 nats per character, against 4.19 for a blind guess. Since 2.718 raised to 1.54 is about 4.7, the model is roughly as unsure as picking among 5 characters. That is far better than picking among 66, but still a long way from certain.

About 30 percent of the practice windows were not ordinary Shakespeare text. In every batch of 64 windows, 19 were made-up repeats. Each was a short string repeated over and over. Some strings were random symbols, and some were snippets of the plays. They were put in on purpose, so the model would meet lots of repetition. That gave it a reason to learn copying. Each string repeated every 4 to 31 characters. Repeats of 11, 13, 17 and 19 characters were left out for the test above.

The three switches are experiments on a model that has finished training, which limits what they show:

- **Mask** lifts the rule for one head of a model trained with the rule on. So that head never practised scoring later characters. In Layer 2, its slips and labels still come from a Layer 1 that kept the rule. The rest of the model does not change.
- **Score scaling** turns off the division for one head of a model that learned with it on. So it shows that head’s rows saturating. It does not show what training without the division would have made.
- **Value vectors (V)** changes the lab’s blend for one head. But it does not change the model’s answer, since the emptied blend never reaches the rest of the model.

Input stops at 64 characters, which is the window length the model trained on. The Words view adds up characters, but big chat models read pieces of words called tokens. So the lab’s word totals are not real tokens. The editable chips show only the first four of 64 numbers. Once you edit them, the shares come from your numbers, not the model’s.

### In real systems

Full attention compares every position with every other position. So the work grows with the square of the text’s length. Double the length of a text, and there are about four times as many pairs to score. Make it ten times longer, and there are about a hundred times as many, which adds up fast. That one fact drives most of the engineering behind models that read very long texts.

**A common mix-up:** Attention in a model is not the same as human focus or attention span. A thick arc or dark cell is also not proof that one word caused the model’s answer. Even a link that follows the content, like `Mark` to `Jones`, is only one weight. It is one weight in a weighted average worked out with dot products, not a reason or a decision.

## Where this goes next

You have built one head and questioned it closely. The attention inside a large chat model is the same recipe with a few more parts around it. Each of those parts connects to another lab in this course.

### Many heads, one output

Real models run several attention heads side by side. That is what the **Attention head** slider lets you step through here. Their blends are joined end to end. Then they pass through one more learned mixing step, called the **output projection**. That step decides how the fetched information gets written back into the model. The result is added into a running record that each position carries through the model. It is called the residual stream, and **The transformer block** follows it.

Splitting the work into heads costs little. This model’s full vectors are 256 numbers long, split into four heads of 64 numbers each. Four heads of 64 cost about the same arithmetic as one head of 256. In return, you get four separate ways to route information.

### Positions in bigger models

Most current models use the twist (RoPE) instead of this lab’s learned seat numbers. To stretch such a model to longer inputs, you adjust how far each seat turns the slips and labels. **Modern attention at scale** covers both ideas.

### Saving work while writing

A model writes text one piece at a time. Earlier pieces’ keys and values never change, since under the one-way rule they never read new ones. So they can be saved and reused instead of being worked out again for every new piece. That saved store is the subject of **Inference & KV cache**. There, K stands for keys and V stands for values.

**Grouped-query attention** makes that store smaller by letting several query heads share one set of keys and values. It gives up a small amount of quality for a large saving in memory. Other methods, such as FlashAttention, work out exactly the same softmax in small blocks. Then the full grid of scores never has to be stored at once.

### Induction heads in research

The copying heads you met are called induction heads. Olsson and colleagues studied them in 2022. Their paper is titled *In-context Learning and Induction Heads*. It describes such a head by two behaviours. First, the head looks back for the spot right after an earlier copy of the current token. Second, it copies, which makes the token found there more likely to come next.

In a two-layer model, they found an induction head paired with a previous-token head. Those are the same two kinds you saw here. In larger models, they saw a sudden change early in training. Induction heads formed while the models got better at in-context learning. They call their evidence preliminary and indirect. It suggests that these heads drive most of that learning. But it is early work, and it does not prove the link directly.

### Attention sinks

Some heads park most of their attention on the very first token when they have nothing useful to fetch. A position that soaks up weight like this is called an **attention sink**. You may have seen something like it if you typed a name such as `Davis` earlier. This matters in practice, because deleting that position from the saved keys and values makes the output worse.
