## The big picture

A language model, the kind of program behind a chatbot, is built from numbers that it learned during
training. Every one of those numbers has to be stored somewhere, and many of them are used in a
calculation each time the model guesses the next piece of text. So once you have a large model that
works well, a practical question follows: can you get a much smaller model that works nearly as well?

This module shows two ways to do that, and both of them start from the same trained model. The first way
is **distillation**, which trains a small model to copy the guesses of a big one, the way a student
learns from a teacher. The second way is **pruning**, which keeps the big model but deletes the numbers
that seem to matter least. At the end, you chain both of them with the rounding trick from the
Quantization lab and measure what each step costs.

### Where you have met it

The model this lab shrinks may already be familiar, because it is the tiny language model from the
earlier labs that reads one character and guesses the next one. Here it plays the part of the
**teacher**, and every smaller model in the lab is measured against it. If you did the interpretability
labs, you have also met ablation, which means switching a part off to see what it was doing. Here it
becomes one of the ways to choose which numbers to delete.

Outside this lab, real distillation recipes train a small model on a bigger model’s guesses, usually
mixed with ordinary training on the right answers. Pruning even has hardware built around it. Some
newer graphics chips, which can do huge numbers of multiplications at once, have parts made to skip
zeros laid out in one particular pattern.

### Why it matters

A model’s size decides how much memory it needs and how much multiplying each guess takes. A model that
is too big may not fit on the machine you have, or it may answer too slowly to be useful. The next lab,
Deployment & serving, turns a model’s size into memory and speed. This module gives you two ways to
bring that size down without training a new model from scratch.

Without distillation, you would have to train a small model on the right answers alone, and this lab
measures what that costs. In the lab’s starting setup, a small model trained that way ends up about
twice as far from the teacher as a model of the same size that copied it. Its score on the right answers
looks almost the same, so you only see the difference when you measure against the teacher. Pruning, for
its part, lets you trim a model that is only a little too big instead of replacing it.

Shrinking also has to be checked at every step. Each step passes its errors on to the next, so only a
reading after every step tells you which one cost the most quality. The last card in the lab gives you
exactly that reading.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- what a teacher’s full set of guesses, called **soft labels**, carries that a single right answer,
  called a **one-hot label**, throws away;
- what the **Distillation temperature** slider changes about the lesson the student gets, and what it
  leaves alone;
- how three patterns of pruning differ in what they really save, whether that is storage space, speed on
  certain special chips, or less multiplying on any computer;
- how to read a model’s size and quality after each step of distil, prune and quantize;
- how three ways of choosing what to delete compare, both in what they keep and in how much work they
  take to work out.

### What the lab lets you see and try

The lab is a set of cards, and each one lets you change something and watch what happens:

- **Student controls** sets how big the students are, the temperature, and how long they train. Its
  **Training target** switch picks which student the readouts show: the one copying the teacher or the
  one learning from right answers.
- **Distillation** trains two students of the same size side by side, one copying the teacher and one
  learning from right answers, and shows how close each one ends up.
- **Soft targets** shows the teacher’s guesses after one character, next to how often each character
  really follows it in the text.
- **Pruning controls** and **Pruning** delete a share of the teacher’s numbers in three different
  patterns and show how much worse the teacher gets.
- **Compose the three steps** shrinks, prunes and rounds one student in a row, and it weighs and tests
  the model after each step.

Everything in the lab is really computed: the students train in front of you, and every score comes from
running a model over real text. What is small is the scale, since the teacher holds only 900 numbers and
reads one character at a time. Nothing is timed, so the lab can show you size and quality, but never
speed. The sizes on the last card are not measured either. They are worked out by counting bits, not by
saving a real file.

## What it is

### The teacher, a model small enough to see whole

The teacher reads one character and guesses the next, choosing from 30 characters: the 26 letters, the
space, the period, and two special markers. The Instruction tuning & templates lab uses those markers to
lay out a chat, so they never appear in ordinary text like the text here. Everything the teacher learned
sits in one table of 900 numbers, with 30 rows and 30 columns. Each row belongs to the character the
model has been given, and each column belongs to a character that might come next. A model that looks at
one character to guess the next is called a **bigram** model.

The number where a row and a column meet is a score for how likely that pair is, and these learned
numbers are called **weights**. The teacher learned its weights from a few short weather notes about a
harbor, such as “the morning fog settles over the harbor and the air feels cool.” It went through that
text 80 times, and each full pass through the training text is called an **epoch**.

To make a guess, the model turns one row of scores into percentages that add up to 100%, so after “h”,
for example, the teacher gives “e” a 76.6% chance. In the harbor notes, “e” really does follow “h” 76.0%
of the time, as in the word “the”, so that guess is close to the real count.

The lab grades every model with **perplexity**, which measures how unsure a model is, on average, when
it guesses the next character of the text. A perplexity of 6.6 means the model is about as unsure as
someone choosing between six or seven equally likely characters, so lower is better. The teacher scores
6.600, and every smaller model in the lab is compared with that.

Perplexity is worked out from another score called the **loss**, which training tries to push down. The
loss works like a penalty. Each time the real next character turns up, the model loses more points the
lower the chance it gave that character. The loss is the average penalty over the whole text. So the
loss and perplexity always rise and fall together. The teacher’s loss appears on the **Student
controls** card as **Teacher loss**, where it reads 1.887.

### Two ways to get a smaller model

**Distillation** trains a new, smaller model, called the **student**, to copy the teacher’s guesses.
Pruning keeps the teacher’s own table but deletes some of its weights, and whatever survives becomes the
smaller model. Because both start from the same trained teacher, the lab can show you exactly what each
one costs. The teacher is needed only while the student trains, and after that the student runs on its
own.

### How the student is built

A student still has to give a score for every one of the 900 pairs of characters. It needs a way to
produce them all without storing a full table. Instead, it describes each character with two short lists
of numbers, one for the character when it is the one given and one for it as a possible next character.
To score a pair, the student multiplies the matching entries of the two lists and adds up the results.

Here is a small example with lists of two numbers. Say “h” as the given character has the list 2, 1, and
“e” as a possible next character has the list 3, −1. Multiply first with first and second with second,
then add: 2 × 3 + 1 × (−1) = 5, which is the score for “e” after “h”.

The length of these lists is called the **rank**, and the **Student size** slider sets it from 1 to 12.
At rank 6, each character gets two lists of 6 numbers. Across all 30 characters that makes 30 × 2 × 6 =
360 weights, and the slider shows this as “rank 6 · 360 weights”. Since 360 out of the teacher’s 900 is
40%, the badge on the **Distillation** card shows the same student as 40% of the teacher.

Short lists cannot hold every detail, so a small student has to squeeze what it learns about each
character into a few numbers. How much of the teacher a student of a given size can hold at all is
called its **student capacity**. Below some size, no amount of careful teaching closes the gap, because
the student has no room left for the details. It trains for as many passes through the text as **Student
epochs** says, from 1 to 40, starting at 18.

### What the teacher knows that the answer key does not

Picture a multiple-choice question on a quiz, where the answer key says only “C”. A good teacher marking
the same question might add, “B was nearly as good, and E is ridiculous.” That ranking of the wrong
answers is extra information, and the answer key throws it away.

Ordinary training uses the answer key, so at each spot in the text the right answer is the character
that really came next. It is written as a list of 30 numbers, with a 1 in that character’s slot and a 0
in every other slot. Only one slot is switched on, so this is called a **one-hot label**, and `One-hot
labels` is one setting of the **Training target** switch. One-hot labels are also called **hard
labels**, because they allow no shades in between.

Distillation uses the teacher’s full set of percentages instead, including the small shares it gives to
characters it rates as unlikely. These full sets of percentages are called **soft labels**, and the
`Teacher distribution` setting shows the student trained on them. The information hidden in how a
teacher ranks the wrong answers has an old nickname, **dark knowledge**.

Picture two friends getting ready for the same test. One studies from an answer key, so she learns which
option was right each time, and she has to piece together the rest from many questions. The other
studies with a tutor who says how close each wrong option came, so he learns more from every question he
practises. The student trained on soft labels is like the second friend, while the one-hot student is
like the first.

During training, the soft-label student is nudged after each small batch of examples, so that its
percentages move closer to the teacher’s. The one-hot student is nudged toward the right answers
instead, and it never looks at the teacher at all.

To check how close a student came, the lab compares its percentages with the teacher’s at every spot in
the text and averages the gap. If the teacher says 77% for “e” after “h” and the student says 60%, that
spot adds to the gap, while a spot where they agree adds nothing. The average is shown as **KL to
teacher**, where 0 would mean a perfect copy and a bigger number means the student is further away. KL
is short for Kullback–Leibler, the two mathematicians who described this way of measuring the gap
between two sets of percentages. The gap is measured in a unit called nats, which is why the callout
under the controls says “nats per position”. All you need to know about nats is that smaller means
closer.

Here is where the quiz picture stops matching. On a quiz only one answer is right, but in text several
characters can rightly come next, such as after a space. The one-hot student does learn how often each
one comes next in the end, but only by averaging many single answers. The teacher, by contrast, hands
over the whole spread at every step.

This teacher also learned little beyond how often each character follows another, which is why its 76.6%
for “e” after “h” sits so close to the text’s 76.0%. Its small guesses for characters that never follow,
sometimes called the **tail**, carry little extra. So most of its help here comes from showing that
whole spread, not from ranking wrong answers. A big teacher trained on far more text has much more dark
knowledge to pass on.

### What temperature does

Turning a row of scores into percentages can be done sharply or gently, and **temperature** sets how
gently. Back in the quiz picture, think of the teacher handing out 100 points across the answers. At
temperature 1 it hands out the points as it really rates the answers, often with a big share for its
favourite. A higher temperature moves points away from the favourite and toward the long shots, so the
small guesses grow. Say the teacher gives three answers 80, 15 and 5 points at temperature 1. At
temperature 2 the same scores give about 59, 26 and 15, so the long shot’s share triples.

Inside the lab, temperature divides every score before the percentages are worked out, which shrinks the
gaps between big and small scores. Distillation softens both the teacher’s and the student’s guesses
this way while the student trains, and the **Distillation temperature** slider sets how much, from 0.5
to 6. At its starting point it reads “T = 1 (no tempering)”, where T stands for temperature.

To see the effect in real numbers, look at “h”: at temperature 1, the teacher puts 4.8% of its guesses
on characters that never follow “h” in the text. At temperature 2 that share grows to 39.3%, and at
temperature 4 it reaches 64.6%, which is more than half of everything the teacher guesses after “h”.

So temperature changes which parts of the teacher’s guesses the training pays most attention to. It does
not change what a perfect copy would be, because a student able to match the teacher exactly at one
temperature would match it at every temperature. These students are too small to do that, so temperature
decides where their unavoidable mistakes land. At a high temperature, training pushes a small student to
get the long shots right. It pays for that by matching the big guesses, like “e” after “h”, less well.
Since it is graded at temperature 1, where the big guesses matter most, it ends further away.

Once training is over, the student is used and graded at temperature 1, because that is how it will run.
That is why **KL to teacher** and the student’s bars in **Soft targets** always use temperature 1,
whatever the slider says.

### Three ways to prune

Pruning needs a rule for which weights to delete, where “delete” means setting a weight to exactly zero.
The plainest rule removes the weights closest to zero, on the guess that numbers so small add the least.
The size of a number with its minus sign ignored is called its **magnitude**, so this rule is called
**magnitude pruning**. For example, −0.02 and 0.02 both have a tiny magnitude, while −3 has a large one.

The share of a model’s weights that are exactly zero is called its **sparsity**. On the **Pruning
controls** card, **Pruned share** sets how much to delete, from 0% to 90%, and **Pruning mode** picks
the pattern the zeros must follow. Picture the teacher’s table as a spreadsheet with 30 rows and 30
columns, which is exactly what the grid on the **Pruning** card draws:

- `Unstructured`, which is **unstructured pruning**, blanks the smallest cells wherever they happen to
  be, so the gaps end up scattered across the sheet.
- `2:4 semi-structured`, which is **semi-structured sparsity**, walks down each column in groups of four
  cells and blanks the two smallest in every group. A column of 30 cells makes seven groups of four plus
  one leftover pair, where one cell goes, so exactly half the cells still go.
- `Structured rows`, which is **structured pruning**, blanks whole rows, choosing the rows whose numbers
  are smallest overall, and the **Rows removed** readout counts them.

In 2:4 mode the pattern fixes the share, so the slider reads “fixed at 50% by 2:4”. Each row of the
table holds everything the teacher learned about what follows one character. If every score in a row is
0, no character beats any other, so all 30 get the same share, about 3.3% each. That is as if the
teacher had never seen that character.

### What each pattern really saves

In a real model, every guess involves multiplying through big grids of weights like this one. A zero
still sits in its place in the grid, so the computer multiplies by it like any other number unless it
has a special way to skip it. That is why the three patterns save such different things, even when they
remove the same share of weights:

- Scattered zeros mostly save storage, and only when the model is stored in a layout that keeps the
  surviving values plus a note of where each one belongs. That layout is called a **sparse format**, and
  even with one, scattered zeros save little time.
- The 2:4 pattern is regular enough that some newer NVIDIA graphics chips, called GPUs, can skip its
  zeros, using special parts called **sparse tensor cores**. On those chips, that makes the multiplying
  up to about twice as fast.
- Removing whole rows makes the grid itself smaller, so there is less to multiply on any computer. In a
  real model, a row stands for a part such as a neuron, a single unit of calculation, which can be taken
  out completely. That is the saving people usually want, but at the same share it costs the most
  quality.

### Running the three steps together

The **Compose the three steps** card chains distillation, pruning and quantization into one
**compression pipeline**. It works like packing for a trip in three steps. First you swap to a smaller
suitcase, which is distilling into the student, and then you take out the items you need least, which is
pruning. Finally you vacuum-seal what is left, which is quantizing. After each step, the card weighs the
bag and checks how well you can still make the trip.

To weigh the bag, the card counts bits, and a **bit** is the smallest piece of computer storage, a
single 0 or 1. Eight bits make one **byte**, which the lab writes as B, and before any rounding, each
stored value takes 32 bits, which is 4 bytes.

So the teacher’s 900 weights take 3,600 bytes, and the rank-6 student’s 360 weights take 1,440 bytes.
The student is stored as its two sets of short lists, which is why its size depends on its rank.

Here the packing picture needs a warning. Taking an item out of a suitcase frees its space, but a zero
in a stored model still fills a full 32-bit slot. Pruning saves room only with a sparse format, which
stores just the kept values plus a note of where each one goes. Here that note is a **mask**: one bit
per position, 1 for a kept value and 0 for a removed one. Say you keep 4 of 8 values. You store those 4
values plus the 8 bits 1 0 1 1 0 0 1 0, so the computer knows where each kept value belongs.

Here is how the numbers work out when half the student’s values are pruned. The 180 values it keeps cost
720 bytes, and the mask adds one bit for each of the 360 positions, which comes to 45 bytes. Together
they make the 765 bytes shown on the **Pruned** row, about half of what the student took before pruning.

Quantizing then rounds each kept value so that it fits in fewer bits, which is the trick from the
Quantization lab. At 4 bits, a value can only be one of 16 levels, because 4 bits can be set 2 × 2 × 2 ×
2 = 16 ways. Every group of 32 values also stores two extra 16-bit numbers that say how to turn those
levels back into real values. **Final bit width** sets the bits per kept value from 2 to 8.

At 4 bits, the 180 kept values take 720 bits, which is 90 bytes. They fill 6 groups, and each group’s
two extra numbers add 4 bytes, so 24 bytes in all. The mask still adds 45 bytes, and 90 + 24 + 45 makes
the 159 bytes of the pruned student after quantizing.

The packing picture also stops matching at the last step. A vacuum-sealed jacket comes out as the same
jacket, but a rounded value is not quite the number it was before. Every step in this pipeline loses a
little quality, and the card measures how much.

### Choosing what to cut

The prune step on this card needs a way to decide which values matter least. Any rule like that is
called an **importance score**, and the **Importance score** control offers three of them:

- `Magnitude` uses the size of each value, read straight off the numbers, so it needs no extra work at
  all.
- `Activation-weighted` multiplies each value’s size by how big its input usually is while the model
  runs. That input is called an activation, so this is an **activation-weighted score**, and it needs
  one pass through the text to measure the inputs. In this lab, the input tracks how often a character
  shows up in the text. So values linked to common characters, like “e” and the space, score higher than
  values linked to rare ones.
- `Measured ablation` removes each value on its own, runs the model over the text, and records how much
  worse the loss gets. This is the ablation test from the interpretability labs, applied to one value at
  a time. It needs one run through the text for every value, so 360 runs at rank 6.

The text a score studies to decide what to cut is called the **calibration text**, and here it is the
harbor notes. The **Passes to score** readout shows how many runs through the text the chosen score needs, which is 0,
1 or 360.

Picture a coach who has to cut half the players from a big squad. She could cut the players with the
weakest stats, which is quick. She could instead weigh each player’s stats by how many minutes they
usually play, which means going through the season’s records once. A third choice is to play a full
practice match without each player in turn and see how much the team’s score drops. That tells her the
most, but it costs one whole match per player.

## Why it’s here

Distillation solves a specific problem with learning from right answers, which you can see by looking at
what follows a space in the harbor notes. Sometimes it is “t”, sometimes “a”, and sometimes “s” or “w”,
but a one-hot label shows only one of these at a time. So a student sees “a” at one step and “t” at the
next, and it has to average many of these noisy nudges to work out the real shares.

A soft label carries the whole spread at every step, so the student gets the full picture each time
instead of piecing it together. That is why a student that copies the teacher can get closer with the
same size and the same amount of training.

Pruning solves a different problem, and it suits a model that is nearly the right size already. It keeps
the trained weights and trims a share of them without building a new model. The share and the pattern
you choose then decide whether you save storage, time, or both.

These steps also stack, because distillation cuts the number of weights, pruning removes a share of what
remains, and quantization shrinks the bits each survivor takes. Together, they can make a model far
smaller than any one step could on its own.

## How to play with it

### Copy the teacher

**Try this:** leave **Student size** at rank 6 and **Distillation temperature** at 1, then flip
**Training target** between `Teacher distribution` and `One-hot labels` while you watch **KL to
teacher**. The copying student ends 0.1348 away from the teacher, while the one that saw only right
answers ends 0.2577 away, almost twice as far.

Now look at the chart on the **Distillation** card, which tracks each student’s loss on the right
answers as it trains. The solid line for soft labels and the dashed line for hard labels nearly overlap,
even though one student ends up almost twice as close to the teacher. The copying student ends at a
**Student loss** of 2.119, and the other student ends at 2.175. The dotted line is the finished teacher,
which no student here is expected to reach.

### Tune the temperature

Keep `Teacher distribution` selected and move **Distillation temperature** from 0.5 up to 6, reading
**KL to teacher** at each stop:

- At 0.5, which sharpens the teacher’s guesses instead of softening them, it reads 0.4037.
- At 1, the starting point, it reads 0.1348.
- At 1.5 it reads 0.1340, the closest the student gets at any of these stops.
- At 2 it reads 0.1407, which is a little further away again.
- At 4 it reads 0.2193, which is still closer than the one-hot student.
- At 6 it reads 0.2737, which is now further away than the one-hot student’s 0.2577.

Then go to **Soft targets** and pick “h” with **Context character**. Each of the ten likeliest next
characters gets three bars, and a “rest” row collects everything else. The top bar, “one-hot labels,
averaged”, shows how often that character really follows “h” in the text. The middle bar shows the
teacher at the current temperature, and the bottom bar shows the student. The student is always shown at
temperature 1, so compare it with the teacher bar only when the slider is at 1. At other settings the
teacher bar is softened, and the two are not meant to match.

Below the bars, the readout **Teacher mass on unseen next characters, T = 1** stays at 4.8%. Mass here
means share of the guesses, so this is the share the teacher puts on characters that never follow “h”.
The one beside it, labelled “Same, at T =” with the current temperature, follows the slider, reaching
39.3% at temperature 2 and 64.6% at 4.

Now set the temperature back to 1, switch **Training target** to `One-hot labels`, and pick “␣”, the
space. The hard-label student gives “a” a 46.2% chance after a space, even though the text says 14.1%.
Switch back to `Teacher distribution`, and the soft-label student gives “a” 14.6%, close to the real
count.

### Prune by magnitude

On **Pruning controls**, keep **Pruning mode** on `Unstructured` and drag **Pruned share** down to 0%,
since it starts at 30%. Then raise it step by step, watching the perplexity badge at the top of the
**Pruning** card. With nothing removed, the badge reads 6.60, and at 30% it has barely moved, to 6.61.
At 50% it still reads only 6.65, so half of the teacher’s weights are gone at almost no cost. After
that, the curve bends upward, reaching 6.84 at 70% and 8.07 at 90%.

Back on the **Pruning controls** card, the teacher and the pruned table each write 68 characters
starting from “the ”. Each one picks a next character from its guesses, adds it to the text, and then
guesses again from it. The label above the pruned sample counts how many characters differ, so you can
see the damage in actual text as well as in the score.

### Compare the three shapes

Set **Pruned share** to 50% and switch **Pruning mode** between its three settings. The perplexity badge
reads 6.65 for `Unstructured` and 7.15 for `2:4 semi-structured`, while `Structured rows` reaches 8.22.
Watch the grid change as you switch: blanks scattered everywhere, then exactly two in every four down
each column, and then whole empty rows, with **Rows removed** at 15 / 30.

### Compose the stages

Put the **Student controls** card back to its starting values, which are rank 6, temperature 1, 18
epochs and `Teacher distribution`. Then go to **Compose the three steps**, set **Share pruned from the
student** to 50%, **Final bit width** to 4 and **Importance score** to `Magnitude`, and read the four
rows:

- `Teacher` shows 3,600 B at perplexity 6.600, the starting point for everything else.
- `Distilled student` drops to 1,440 B, while perplexity rises to 8.326.
- `Pruned` drops further to 765 B, with perplexity at 8.560.
- `Quantized` ends at 159 B, with perplexity at 8.619.

The badge says the final model is 22.6 times smaller than the teacher. In exchange, its perplexity rose
from 6.600 to 8.619, which makes it 30.6% worse.

### Change the importance score

Raise **Share pruned from the student** to 70% and switch **Importance score** between its three
settings. The second bar list shows the perplexity right after the prune step for all three scores,
along with the passes each one needs:

- `Magnitude` leaves a perplexity of 9.682 and needs 0 passes, since it reads the values directly.
- `Activation-weighted` leaves 9.999 and needs 1 pass to measure the inputs.
- `Measured ablation` leaves 9.480 and needs 360 passes, one for every value.

## What to notice

- **Copying wins at every size, at temperature 1:** at rank 2 the copying student lands at 0.4200,
  against 0.4668 for the one-hot student. At rank 12 it lands at 0.0722, against 0.1295. Each soft-label
  update shows the whole spread of guesses, while each one-hot update shows one sampled answer.
- **A low loss does not mean a close copy:** the two loss curves nearly overlap, even though one student
  is twice as close to the teacher. Fitting the right answers and matching the teacher are different
  questions, and only **KL to teacher** shows the second one.
- **Size sets a ceiling:** **student capacity** decides how much a student can match at all, and rank 12
  gets nearly six times closer to the teacher than rank 2.
- **Temperature has a sweet spot**, which here sits near 1 to 1.5. From 4 upward, the rank-6 student
  spends its limited room on the tail of small guesses, and at 6 it ends further away than the one-hot
  student.
- **Right answers alone can mislead a small student:** after a space, the one-hot student’s 46.2% on “a”
  is more than three times the text’s 14.1%. The soft-label student’s 14.6% is close to the real count.
- **The first cuts are nearly free:** at 30%, unstructured pruning costs almost nothing, since
  perplexity moves from 6.60 to only 6.61. Both modes first remove the four rows the text never uses,
  for “j”, “x” and the two markers, whose weights are already zero. After that, unstructured removes 150
  more of the smallest weights, wherever they sit. Structured has to blank 5 whole rows the text does
  use, big weights included, so at 30% it already reaches 6.95.
- **A pruned table can still write the same text**, because the weights it lost may be ones that this
  particular piece of writing never needed.
- **2:4 sits between the two curves:** a forced pattern costs more than free choice at the same share,
  but less than deleting whole rows.
- **The three steps are not priced alike:** distilling saves 2,160 bytes for 1.73 more perplexity.
  Pruning saves 675 bytes for 0.23 more, and quantizing saves 606 bytes for only 0.06 more. Drop **Final
  bit width** to 2, though, and the quantize step costs 1.34.
- **The best score is the slow one:** `Measured ablation` leaves the lowest perplexity at 70%, because
  it asks the loss directly what each value is worth. The price is one test per value, which is 360
  here.
- **The quick shortcut loses on this toy:** `Activation-weighted` does worse than plain `Magnitude`.
  Here, the size of a value’s input only tracks how often its character shows up. That tells you how
  many guesses a value touches, but not how much each of those guesses changes when the value is
  removed. On this toy, that leads it to keep the wrong values.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- Every loss, KL and perplexity in the lab is really computed, but on a toy. It has 900 weights and one
  character of context. A real model has many stacked **layers**, steps of calculation done one after
  another, and this toy has none of them.
- The teacher learned almost exactly the text’s own counts, so its dark knowledge is a thin tail. The
  lab shows how distillation works, not how much it helps when the teacher is big.
- **KL to teacher** skips the four characters that never appear in the harbor text at all, which are
  “j”, “x” and the two markers. Neither model was trained on those rows.
- Each student is a slimmed-down copy of the teacher’s own shape, not a model with a different design.
- Each student trains either purely on the teacher or purely on right answers, with no mix, and every
  result comes from one random starting point, called a seed.
- Pruning happens once, with no retraining afterward to win back quality. Nothing is timed either, so
  the speed claims come from how the chips work, not from a stopwatch.
- The compose card does no retraining between steps and removes the same share from both sets of lists.
  It also uses the harbor text twice: as the calibration text that picks what to cut, and as the text
  that tests the result. So for the scores that study that text, the perplexity after pruning looks
  better than it would on new text.
- A row of the teacher’s table stands in for a neuron or a group of neurons that a real model would
  remove. Here the row is only set to zero and stays in the table, so nothing actually gets smaller.
- The compose card’s sizes are bit counts worked out on paper, not the size of a real file on a disk.

### Where the real methods fall short

**A common mix-up:** deleting half the weights does not make a model twice as fast. Picture a friend
helping to mark a stack of quizzes in which half the answer boxes are blank, using a marking sheet that
makes him check every box anyway. The blanks save him no time, and only taking those questions off the
quiz would.

Scattered zeros work the same way, since the computer still multiplies by every one of them. So
unstructured **sparsity** mostly saves storage, and only with a sparse format. A strict 2:4 pattern can
make the multiplying up to about twice as fast, but only on GPUs built to spot that pattern. Deleting
whole rows shrinks the grid, which saves work on any computer.

Magnitude is the crudest **importance score**. The authors of a pruning method called Wanda report that,
on large models, also weighing how big each weight’s input is does much better than size alone. On large
models a few inputs are far bigger than the rest, and weighing by input catches weights that size alone
would cut. You can read more about Wanda in Going deeper, and this toy reverses that result for the
reason given in What to notice.

A student also copies its teacher’s mistakes along with its skills, and matching the next-character
guesses does not, on its own, pass on behaviour that takes many steps. That is why a variant called
sequence-level distillation exists, aimed at exactly that gap.

Measured ablation has limits of its own, since it tests one value at a time and misses values that
matter only in combination. Think of two players who can each cover for the other. Benching either one
alone barely changes the score, so the test rates both as unimportant, even though cutting both would
hurt badly. Ablation also needs one test per value, which is out of reach when a model has billions of
them.

## Going deeper

Real distillation usually mixes the softened copying lesson with ordinary right-answer training, while
this lab keeps the two apart. Both the lab and real recipes multiply the copying lesson by the
temperature squared, so each training step stays about the same size whatever temperature you pick. Real
teachers learn far more than how often one character follows another, so the way they rank wrong answers
carries much more. A higher temperature lifts those small guesses, which is where that ranking lives.

There are two explanations for why soft labels help. The first says each position carries a whole set of
percentages instead of one sampled answer, so there is more to learn from. The second says the teacher’s
percentages are smoother than the raw right answers, so they make gentler targets to aim at. This lab
mostly shows the first, since its teacher barely differs from the text’s own counts.

### Composing the steps

The usual order is to prune or distil first, retrain to win back quality, and quantize last, because
quantizing assumes the weights are final. Each step also inherits the errors of the one before it, and
its own cost depends a lot on the settings. In the lab, the 4-bit quantize step adds only 0.06 to the
pruned student’s perplexity. At 2 bits the same step adds 1.34 to that pruned student. Set **Share
pruned from the student** to 0% so nothing is pruned, and at 2 bits it costs 1.76. The Quantization lab
prices that last step in detail, and Deployment & serving turns the result into memory and speed.

### Importance scores beyond magnitude

A method called Wanda multiplies each weight’s size by the size of the input it meets, and it compares
weights only against others that feed the same output. Its authors report that it does much better than
size alone on a family of large language models called LLaMA. Another method, SparseGPT, works layer by
layer, choosing what to remove and adjusting what stays so that each layer’s output changes as little as
possible.

Both methods prune large models in one go without retraining, and they exist to close the gap between
magnitude, which is cheap but crude, and measured ablation. Ablation is the most direct test, but it
needs one run per weight.
