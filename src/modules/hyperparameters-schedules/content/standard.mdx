## The big picture

A **hyperparameter** is a setting chosen before training rather than learned. This module covers
the ones that size each update: the **learning rate**, the **schedule** that changes it over the
run, and the gradient clip, weight decay and batch size around them. It asks how large each step
should be at each point in training, and when a gap between two recipes is real.

It builds on Anatomy of a training run, which named the stages of one training loop. Here that
loop runs four times side by side, changing only the learning-rate rule, so each choice is judged
from a measured loss curve.

### Where it shows up

Both GPT-3 and Llama 2 report a short linear **warmup** followed by **cosine decay** to 10
percent of the peak, weight decay of 0.1, and a clip of 1.0 on the global gradient norm.
Warmup-stable-decay schedules hold the rate constant for most of training and decay only at the
end. Clipping exists because the gradient norms of deep networks can spike by orders of
magnitude within one step.

### Why it matters

The peak rate can fail both ways. Too small leaves the loss high when the step budget runs out.
Too large makes each step longer than the local slope justifies, so it passes the minimum and the
loss rises: **overshooting**. Enough of it can end a run worse than a model that learned nothing.
In a deep network a gradient spike can push the weights to non-finite values that no later step
recovers: **divergence**.

Judging the result can fail too. One run is one sample of the shuffle order, so a gap smaller
than the seed-to-seed spread cannot be told from noise. Frontier settings come from sweeps on
small proxy models, so a sweep fooled by seed noise passes the mistake on.

### What you will be able to explain

- How to read a learning-rate schedule as a function of training progress.
- Why decay lets a run use a larger peak learning rate than a constant rate can.
- How to tell an overshooting run from a diverging one, and what a gradient clip changes.
- How to judge whether a difference between two runs is larger than the spread that the
  shuffle seed alone produces.
- How a gradient can explode or vanish with depth, and what a norm clip can and cannot fix.

### What the lab shows

The lab trains for real: four runs of a tiny 900-weight character model on one 499-character
corpus, by plain minibatch gradient descent from the same zeroed weights and **seed**, differing
only in schedule. **Loss for all four schedules** and **Stability readout** show measurements
from those runs, and every dial on **Optimizer controls** retrains all four. The model's
gradients are bounded, so a high rate makes it overshoot but never diverge;
**Gradient through depth** is where an explosion appears.

Four parts are not training results:

- **Learning-rate schedule shapes** plots the closed-form rate for each **Schedule** option
  (Constant, Cosine decay, Warmup + decay, One cycle), read at one point by **Inspect epoch**.
- The dotted "knows nothing" line on **Loss for all four schedules** is the fixed baseline
  ln 30 = 3.401.
- The dotted "√2 bound" on **Stability readout**, and the note under it, give the closed-form
  ceiling on every batch gradient norm the trace plots: √2 ≈ 1.41.
- **Gradient through depth** is closed-form, with no weights or data: **Layers** and **Gain per
  layer** set a gradient that compounds once per layer, and **Depth clip** caps its total length.

**Optimizer controls** holds **Peak learning rate**, **Warmup fraction**, **Gradient clip**,
**Weight decay** and **Batch size**. The key experiment: raise **Peak learning rate** from 0.6
and **Best schedule here** flips from Constant to a decaying schedule, then Constant ends above
the "knows nothing" line and **Worse than chance** counts it. **Five seeds** under **Seeds** shows
whether each gap beats the shuffle spread.

## What it is

A **schedule** sets the **learning rate** from how far through the run you are, rather than
holding it fixed. The top chart plots exactly that: **Peak learning rate** multiplied by a
factor that depends only on progress from 0 to 1. The four shapes differ in where they put the
step budget:

- **Constant** holds the full peak rate from the first batch to the last.
- **Cosine decay** starts at the peak and eases down to 3 percent of it.
- **Warmup + decay** ramps from near zero to the peak across the **Warmup fraction**, then
  falls linearly to 5 percent.
- **One cycle** starts at a tenth of the peak, rises to it over the **Warmup fraction**, then
  cosines down to 2 percent.

Below that, four separate runs train on the same corpus, from the same zeroed weights, with the
same **seed** — 25 epochs, 800 optimizer steps each. The only difference between them is that
factor. The dotted "knows nothing" line sits at ln 30 = 3.401 nats per token, and
**Worse than chance** counts how many of the four runs end above it.

Two further controls answer what a single run cannot. **Seeds** repeats every run over five
shuffle orders and shades the spread. **Gradient through depth** is a closed-form model, not a
trained network, of a gradient passing back through many layers, so the explosion that this
model cannot produce can be shown and clipped.

## Why it’s here

The learning rate decides whether a run converges at all. But the useful claim is sharper than
"tune the learning rate": a schedule is what makes a *large* peak rate usable.

A single fixed rate has to satisfy two conflicting requirements. Early on the weights are far
from anything good, so long steps make progress fast. Late on the weights are near a minimum,
so the same long steps overshoot it, and the loss stops falling or rises.
Decay lets one run be aggressive at the start and settled at the end, which is why the winning
schedule changes as you move **Peak learning rate**.

## How to play with it

Click through all four **Schedule** options and watch only the top chart first: the shaded bands
mark the selected schedule's warmup and decay phases, and **Inspect epoch** moves a dot along
the curve that reads the rate at that step. With **Warmup + decay** at the defaults the warmup
lasts 120 of 800 steps, and epoch 12 reads a rate of 0.378.

The first metric under the loss chart is named after whichever schedule is selected — **Cosine
decay final loss** on load — while **Best schedule here** and **Best final loss** always report
the winner of the four. Then compare at rising peak rates:

- **Peak learning rate** 0.6, the default: constant 2.075, cosine 2.255, warmup + decay 2.251,
  one cycle 2.254. **Best schedule here** reads Constant, and it wins by a wide margin.
- At 6: constant 1.880, cosine 1.846. The order has flipped.
- At 12: constant 2.093, cosine 1.816. Constant is going backwards; cosine keeps improving.
- At 30: constant 3.020, cosine 1.802. Between 35 and 38 the constant run's final loss on
  seed 1 crosses 3.401 (3.332 at 35, 3.537 at 38) and **Worse than chance** flips to 1 / 4 while the
  decaying runs stay near 1.80.

Now set **Peak learning rate** to 30 and **Batch size** to 2. The constant run lands at 12.325,
far worse than knowing nothing. Bring **Gradient clip** up to `norm ≤ 0.50` and the same run
returns 6.522. At **Batch size** 16 that clip moves 3.020 to 2.884.

Now set **Peak learning rate** back to 0.6, **Batch size** to 16, and switch **Seeds** to **Five
seeds**. Each schedule gains a shaded band and the table lists the final loss of every seed.
**Constant** spans 2.075 to 2.077, so its lead of 0.175 over the best decaying schedule is about
fifty times the band.

Cosine decay, One cycle and Warmup + decay each move by about 0.004 between seeds and end within
0.004 of each other, so their order is inside the band. The callout says which pairs fall where.

Raise **Peak learning rate** to 6, then 30, and read **Constant**'s band. At 6 it runs from 1.880
to 2.008 and Cosine decay's top is 1.847, so all five seeds agree that the order flipped. At 30
it runs from 2.395 to 4.673: seed 1's 3.020 is one point inside it, and two of the five seeds
already end worse than chance.

Last, scroll to **Gradient through depth**. At 24 layers and a **Gain per layer** of 1.20, the
input layer sees 66 times the length of the output layer and the whole gradient is about 120.
Set **Depth clip** to 1.0: the whole gradient becomes exactly 1.0, every layer scaled by the
same factor, 0.0083.

Then set 40 layers and a gain of 0.80. The first layer now sees under a thousandth of the
output gradient, and no setting of **Depth clip** raises it.

## What to notice

- **Warmup fraction** changes only two of the four curves. **Constant** and **Cosine decay**
  ignore it entirely; only **Warmup + decay** and **One cycle** read it.
- No schedule here reaches zero: cosine bottoms at 3 percent of the peak, warmup + decay at 5
  percent, one cycle at 2 percent. How low to decay is a real design choice: many recipes stop
  near 10 percent of the peak, and others decay linearly all the way to zero.
- Constant's final loss is not monotone in the peak rate — 2.075 at 0.6, 1.880 at 6, then back
  up through 2.093 at 12 and 3.020 at 30. There is a best fixed rate, and it is narrow.
  Cosine's own best is about 1.80 near a peak of 30, and at the top of the slider it has crept
  back up to 1.809: a schedule makes a large peak rate usable, not free.
- **Peak gradient norm** falls as **Batch size** rises: about 1.41 at 2, about 1.00 at 16, and
  0.181 at 64, all at a peak rate of 30. Averaging over more pairs shrinks the gradient; that is
  the gradient-noise side of batch size. **Gradient clip** above about 1.4 never binds anywhere.
- The trace on **Stability readout** shows where the peak comes from at 16: most steps sit near
  0.2, and each epoch's last batch holds only 2 of the 498 pairs, so its norm jumps toward 0.8.
- **Weight decay** makes the fit worse here, not better: with **Constant** at a peak of 0.6,
  0.02 of decay takes the final loss from 2.075 to 2.664. It applies to the rows updated in
  each step, so a row only decays on steps where its character appeared. With plain SGD, adding
  the decay to the gradient is the same step as decoupled decay. It is a regularizer, and this
  lab has no held-out score for it to improve.
- A gap is only evidence when it is larger than the **seed band**. At the default peak, Constant
  leads by 0.175 against a band of 0.004. The closest pair, One cycle and Cosine decay, differ by
  0.0002 on average against a spread of 0.004, so a single run cannot say which is better.
- The band is not the same width everywhere. Constant's is 0.002 wide at a peak of 0.6, 0.128 at
  6, 0.490 at 12 and 2.278 at 30. The decaying schedules stay under 0.011 at every one of those.
  A high peak makes Constant's result depend heavily on the shuffle, so a one-seed number there
  is close to a coin flip.
- The clip's rescue is larger than the band at **Batch size** 2: unclipped runs at a peak of 30
  end between 9.854 and 12.325, clipped at 0.50 between 6.168 and 6.522. At **Batch size** 16 the
  clipped range, 2.308 to 3.606, overlaps the unclipped one, so seed 1's 3.020 to 2.884 alone
  does not show a rescue.
- A clip rescales the whole gradient by one factor, so it keeps the direction across layers and
  caps only the length. It can only shorten: raising **Depth clip** never lifts a vanished
  gradient, and a clip set above the whole length does not bind at all.
- At a gain of exactly 1.00 every layer has length 1 and the whole gradient is the square root of
  the layer count. The explosion or collapse comes entirely from the gain differing from 1,
  compounded once per layer.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Four real runs, four real curves, one fixed **seed** by default so the
comparison is about the schedule and nothing else. That fixed seed is also a limit: each number
is one sample. **Five seeds** adds the missing error bar, but five is a small sample and the band
is the range of those five, not a confidence interval. It measures the shuffle order and nothing
else: not the corpus, the model size, or the learning-rate grid.

The more important caveat is instability. **This model cannot produce a gradient explosion.**
For softmax cross-entropy through a single linear layer, each row's gradient is a probability
vector minus a one at the true character, so its length is bounded — the measured **Peak
gradient norm** stays between about 0.17 and 1.41 at every setting the sliders allow.

What high rates produce here is repeated **overshooting**: steps longer than the local slope
justifies, travelling past the minimum. Strict **divergence** — non-finite weights that no later
step can recover — never happens inside these ranges; the worst run you can build stays finite
and merely terrible.

**Gradient clipping** still helps because it caps how far a single step can travel, limiting
the damage from a long step even when the gradient was never unusual. That is why it does most
at **Batch size** 2, where each batch's gradient is the noisiest estimate of the true one.

The **Gradient through depth** card is how this lab shows an **exploding gradient** at all, and
it is a different kind of object. It has no weights, activations, or data: each layer multiplies
the gradient by the same gain, and the output gradient is set to 1.

A real network's factor varies with its weights, activation function, and input. The card shows
the mechanism, a product that compounds once per layer, and what a clip does to it, not how a
real network behaves.

The rest are the usual limits of this model — one character of context, 900 weights, no hidden
layers, no tokenizer, a 499-character corpus, plain gradient descent rather than Adam, and a
**Peak learning rate** slider that stops at 60. The plotted loss is training loss, so nothing
here shows a hyperparameter helping generalization.

**In real systems.** Deep networks multiply many local derivatives together on the way back, so
their gradient norms genuinely can spike by orders of magnitude within one step. Those spikes
are what clipping was built for. Both GPT-3 and Llama 2 clip the global gradient norm at 1.0
(Brown et al., arXiv 2005.14165, Appendix B; Touvron et al., arXiv 2307.09288).

Do not read this lab as a demonstration of one. **Warmup** matters far more with Adam, whose
running estimate of each coordinate's gradient magnitude is built from almost no data for the
first few hundred steps, making early steps large and badly calibrated.

## Going deeper

**The shape people actually use.** A short linear **warmup**, then **cosine decay** to 10
percent of the peak, which is what both GPT-3 and Llama 2 report. Its drawback is that the shape
depends on the total step count, so you cannot extend the run afterwards without discarding it.
Warmup-stable-decay schedules hold a constant rate for most of training and decay only in a
short final phase, which leaves you free to stop anywhere and still get the settling.

**Learning rate and batch size are one knob.** Doubling the batch halves the variance of the
gradient estimate, which supports a larger step; the common rules scale the rate linearly or by
the square root of the batch ratio. This lab shows the same relationship from the other side:
on **Constant** at a peak of 30, **Batch size** 64 finishes at 1.883 and 2 finishes at 12.325.

**Two practical notes.** AdamW's `weight_decay` is decoupled — it subtracts a fraction of the
weight inside the update rather than adding an L2 penalty to the loss, because under Adam's
per-coordinate scaling those are not equivalent. And a frontier run happens once, so its
hyperparameters come from sweeps on much smaller proxy models plus a rule for transferring the
result. A wrong schedule is not an experiment you rerun.

### Toy versus real

- **Peak rate.** The lab's SGD peak is 0.6, and its useful range runs from about 0.5 to 40. Runs
  with Adam use far smaller rates, and the two kinds are not comparable. GPT-3 lists peak learning
  rates from 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ for its 125 million parameter model to 0.6 × 10⁻⁴ for the 175 billion
  one (Brown et al., arXiv 2005.14165, Table 2.1). Llama 2 lists 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ for its 7B and 13B
  models and 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ for 34B and 70B (Touvron et al., arXiv 2307.09288, Table 1).
- **Warmup.** The lab warms up for 15 percent of the run, 120 of 800 steps. Llama 2 warms up for
  2,000 steps, and GPT-3 over its first 375 million of 300 billion tokens. Against about 500,000
  updates for Llama 2 (2.0 trillion tokens at a batch of 4 million) that is roughly 0.4 percent of
  the run, and GPT-3's is 0.125 percent. Both numbers are arithmetic on the papers' figures.
- **The floor.** The lab's cosine bottoms at 3 percent of the peak. Both papers decay to 10
  percent of it, and both use weight decay of 0.1 and a clip of 1.0 on the global gradient norm.
- **Seeds.** The lab can afford five runs per setting. A frontier run happens once, which is why
  its settings come from sweeps on smaller proxy models, as the section above notes.
