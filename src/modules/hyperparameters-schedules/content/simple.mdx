## The big picture

A brand-new AI model starts out knowing nothing. It learns through a training program that makes the model guess and then scores how wrong each guess was. After every score, the program nudges the model's inner numbers a little. The next guesses come out slightly better, and the cycle repeats.

Each nudge is called a step. Before the first step, a person has to pick a few settings that the model never learns for itself. How big should each step be, and should that size change during training? How many examples should the model see before each step, and should any single step have a cap? These choices are called **hyperparameters**, and this module is about making them well.

The most important one is the size of each step, called the **learning rate**. If it is too small, the model creeps along and runs out of time before it gets anywhere good. If it is too big, the model keeps leaping past the good answer. In this lab, it can even finish worse than a model that never learned anything.

A **schedule** is a plan for changing the learning rate during training. The usual plan lets the steps shrink toward the end. Teams that train huge AI models make the same choices. The teams behind two well-known models, GPT-3 and Llama 2, published their settings. Both raised the step size gently over a short opening stretch. Then they slid it down along a smooth curve. Both also put a cap on how big the push behind any single step could be.

Wrong settings are costly, and the lab shows you several ways that can happen. Without a schedule, one step size has to suit both the start and the end of training. No single size does both jobs well. Without a fair way to compare two runs, you can crown a winner that only won by luck. For the largest models, running the job again cannot fix a wrong schedule. A run that big uses so much computer time that people do it only once.

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain these five things:

- How to read a learning-rate schedule. It is a chart of step size against how far through training the model is.
- Why letting the step size shrink toward the end makes it safe to start with much bigger steps.
- How a run that keeps overshooting, which means stepping past the good answer, differs from one that truly breaks. You will also see what a cap on each push, called gradient clipping, changes.
- How to tell whether one run really beat another, or whether the gap is no bigger than luck. The examples get shuffled into a new order each time, and that order alone can move a result.
- How big models pass a message backward through many stacked stages, called layers. The message tells each stage how to change. It can grow huge or fade to almost nothing, and a cap can fix only one of those.

The lab gives you five cards, and each one shows a different part of the story:

- **Learning-rate schedule shapes:** This card draws four schedules as curves. You can read the step size at every point in a run.
- **Loss for all four schedules:** This card trains four real tiny models side by side, one for each schedule. It plots how wrong each model is as it learns, and that score is called its loss. Its **Seeds** switch retrains every model with five different shuffles. You can see how much luck alone moves each result.
- **Optimizer controls:** This card holds five dials. The optimizer is the part of the program that takes the steps. Its **Peak learning rate** dial sets the top step size. It can make a run end up worse than knowing nothing.
- **Stability readout:** This card shows how big each step's push was. It also shows whether a cap is cutting it down. It also counts how many runs finished worse than a model that knows nothing and guesses evenly.
- **Gradient through depth:** This card is a separate piece of arithmetic, not a trained model. It shows what happens to a message passed back through many layers.

The first card draws each schedule straight from its formula. The loss and stability cards show numbers measured from real training runs. Those runs are retrained every time you move a dial.

## What it is

### The model and its loss

The model in this lab is tiny. It reads a 499-character text called Harbor weather notes, which begins "the morning fog settles over the harbor". Its job is to guess each character from the single character before it.

The model works with 30 characters. They are 26 letters, a space, a full stop and two marker symbols that never appear in the text. Everything it knows sits in a table of 900 numbers called **weights**. The table has one row of 30 scores for each character.

To guess what follows a t, the model looks up the t row. It turns the 30 scores into chances for the next character, and higher scores get bigger chances.

All 900 weights start at zero, so every score in a row is the same. That means the model gives every character the same 1-in-30 chance. The model learns from pairs of neighbouring characters, and the whole text holds 498 of them. The word "the" gives two pairs: t followed by h, and h followed by e.

To learn, the model needs a score for how wrong it is, called the **loss**. The loss is low when the model gives the real next character a big chance. It is high when the model gives that character a small chance.

The lab averages the loss over all 498 pairs. It measures the loss in units called nats per token, where a token here is one character. You will never need to work with the unit, because lower is always better. A few landmarks give the numbers meaning:

- A loss of 3.401 is what you get by spreading your bets evenly over all 30 characters. That is why the dotted line on the loss chart sits there.
- The dotted line's label reads "knows nothing · ln 30 = 3.401". Here ln is a calculator function that turns 30 into 3.401. You will never need to work it out.
- A loss of about 2.08 means the model is as unsure as someone choosing fairly among 8 characters.
- A loss of about 1.80 is about as unsure as choosing fairly among 6. The best of the four runs finish there when the **Peak learning rate**, the top step size, is high.

### One step of training

At each training step, the program takes a small group of pairs called a **batch**. The default is 16 pairs. For every weight, the program works out which way to push it and how hard. The goal is to make the loss on those pairs go down.

That list of pushes, one for each weight, is called the **gradient**. The program then moves every weight by its push multiplied by one number. That number is the **learning rate**, and it decides how big each step is.

Here is a worked example. Say the gradient's push for one weight is 0.5, and the learning rate is 0.6. The weight then moves by 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3, in the direction that lowers the loss. Doubling the learning rate would double the move.

One pass through all 498 pairs is called an **epoch**. With batches of 16, an epoch takes 32 steps. That is 31 full batches, plus a last batch holding the 2 pairs left over. A full run of 25 epochs adds up to 25 × 32 = 800 steps.

The overall size of the gradient is called its length, or its **gradient norm**. To find it, you square every push, add the squares together and take the square root of the total.

Picture a gradient with only two pushes, 3 and 4. Their squares are 9 and 16, which add up to 25. The square root of 25 is 5, so that gradient has a length of 5.

You would do the same sum on a map. Walk 3 steps east and 4 steps north, and you end up 5 steps from where you started. A gradient's length is that straight-line distance, worked out over 900 directions instead of two. It is not the number of pushes in the list.

The optimizer is the part of the training program that takes these steps. That is where the **Optimizer controls** card gets its name.

### The picture: walking downhill in fog

Picture yourself on a hillside in thick fog, trying to reach the lowest point of the valley. You cannot see the bottom. All you can do is feel which way the ground slopes, take a stride downhill, and feel again.

Training is that walk. Your height is the loss, and your position is the set of 900 weights. The slope you feel is the gradient. Each stride is the learning rate times how steep the ground feels. Steep ground and a big learning rate both mean a long stride.

Far from the bottom, long strides get you down quickly. Near the bottom, a long stride carries you across the valley floor and up the other side. The next long stride carries you back, and the walk turns into a swing from side to side.

That swing is called **overshooting**. A step is so long that it carries the model past the low point instead of down into it. The loss then stops falling or even rises. When a run keeps overshooting, its loss curve stops falling steadily. Instead, it jumps up and down from one epoch to the next.

### A schedule: stride length set by the clock

A fixed learning rate keeps the same stride multiplier from the first step to the last. The strides change only with the slope. A **schedule** changes the learning rate as the run goes on. It takes a top value, the **Peak learning rate**, and multiplies it by a factor of 1 or less. That factor depends only on how far through the run the model is.

The first card, **Learning-rate schedule shapes**, draws that factor times the peak. Progress through the run is along the bottom, and the learning rate is up the side. Progress counts in epochs, so the bottom of the chart runs from 0 to 25.

Here the valley picture stops matching. A hiker who sensed the bottom was near could choose to take more careful strides. A schedule never makes that choice. It follows its fixed curve by the clock, whether or not the model is anywhere near a good answer.

The ground in this lab also wobbles. Each step feels the slope of only one batch of 16 pairs. That reading is rough and points partly the wrong way. Near the bottom the real slope is gentle, but each batch's rough reading is often much bigger. A big learning rate turns that rough reading into a big hop away from the bottom.

Finally, the real valley has 900 directions to move in, one for each weight. A hillside has only north-south and east-west.

### The four shapes

The four schedules hand out long and short strides at different points in the run. The falling part of each curve is called its decay:

- **Constant:** This schedule holds the full peak rate from the first batch to the last. The learning rate stays the same for the whole walk.
- **Cosine decay:** This schedule starts at the peak, with no slow climb at the start. Then it eases down to 3 percent of the peak. Its name comes from cosine, a curve from trigonometry. You only need its shape. It is flat at first, steepest in the middle and flat again at the bottom.
- **Warmup + decay:** This schedule climbs in a straight line from near zero to the peak. Then it falls in a straight line to 5 percent of the peak. The **Warmup fraction** slider sets how long the climb lasts.
- **One cycle:** This schedule starts at a tenth of the peak. It climbs to the peak over the same warmup stretch, set by the same **Warmup fraction** slider. Then it curves down to 2 percent of the peak, which ends below where it began.

The climb at the start of the last two is called **warmup**. Each stride is the learning rate times how steep the ground feels. The ground is steepest right at the start. The full peak rate there could give an extra-long first stride that throws the model off course. Warmup keeps the rate low for those first steps, then hands over to the long, fast strides.

In this lab, the start is only a little steeper than later on. So warmup changes the results very little. It matters much more with a different update method called Adam, which comes up under "Where it breaks".

**Warmup fraction** sets the share of the run that the climb takes. At its default of 15 percent, warmup lasts 120 of the 800 steps.

Here is a worked example. The default **Peak learning rate** is 0.6. Take **Warmup + decay**. By epoch 12 its climb is over and it has come part of the way down. Its factor is 0.63. The learning rate at that moment is 0.6 × 0.63 = 0.378. That is the number the chart's dot shows at epoch 12.

### A cap on any single step

One more control works on the gradient itself instead of the clock. **Gradient clipping** sets a maximum length. Whenever a batch's gradient is longer than the cap, every push in it shrinks by the same factor. The shrinking stops when the length equals the cap.

In the valley, clipping means the ground can never count as steeper than a set amount. At a given learning rate, no stride can be longer than a set distance, however steep the ground feels. The direction stays the same.

Take the gradient with pushes 3 and 4, which has a length of 5. Set the cap at 1. Dividing both pushes by 5 gives 0.6 and 0.8, which point the same way as before. Their squares, 0.36 and 0.64, add up to 1, so the new length is exactly 1.

A gradient already shorter than the cap is left alone. So you can switch a cap on and still see no change. The lab's **Clip** readouts say "binding" when the cap shortened at least one gradient. They say "never binds" when no gradient was long enough to reach it.

### Four real runs, one difference

The next card, **Loss for all four schedules**, trains four real copies of the model at once. There is one copy for each schedule. All four read the same text and start from the same all-zero weights. They also see the pairs in the same order, so only the schedule differs.

The pairs get shuffled into a new order at the start of every epoch. A number called the **seed** fixes how that shuffle comes out. Use the same seed and you get the same order every time. In many models, the seed also picks random starting weights. Here every weight starts at zero, so the seed changes only the shuffle order.

Each curve shows the loss on the whole text at the end of each epoch. All four curves start on the dotted "knows nothing" line, because zeroed weights give every character the same chance.

The **Worse than chance** readout on **Stability readout** counts how many of the four runs finish above that line. Lower loss is better. Finishing above the line means doing worse than a model that knows nothing and spreads its bets evenly. That even guessing is the "chance" in the readout's name.

### A switch and a card for what one run cannot show

One run with one seed is one sample, and a different shuffle might have told a slightly different story. Switch **Seeds** to **Five seeds**, and the lab retrains every schedule with five shuffles. The range from the lowest result to the highest is called the **seed band**. The lab shades it in.

The trained model also cannot show one famous way that real training goes wrong. So the **Gradient through depth** card shows it with plain arithmetic instead. Large models, often called neural networks, are built from many **layers**. A layer is a stage of calculation, and the stages stack one after another.

To change an early layer, the program passes the gradient backward from the output. It moves one layer at a time. Each layer multiplies it by some factor. It starts at the output because that is where the guess comes out and the mistake is measured.

Picture a line of 24 people passing a message from the front of a hall to the back. The front of the line stands for the output layer. The back stands for layer 1, where the input comes in.

The first person speaks at normal volume. Everyone after that repeats the message 1.2 times as loudly as they heard it. Between 24 people there are 23 hand-offs, and each one multiplies the volume by 1.2. By the back of the line, it is 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 and so on, 23 times over. That makes it about 66 times as loud.

A gradient that grows like this on its way back is called an **exploding gradient**. Now make the line 40 people long and turn the factor down to 0.8. Each person now repeats the message at 80 percent of what they heard.

Over the 39 hand-offs, the message fades to under a thousandth of its starting volume. That is called a vanishing gradient. The card lets you cap an exploding gradient. Then you can watch the same cap fail to help a vanishing one.

Here the picture stops matching. In a real network, each layer's factor is different, because it changes with that layer's weights and the input. The card uses one fixed factor for every layer, and its **Gain per layer** slider sets that factor.

## Why it’s here

The learning rate decides whether a run works at all. The sharper lesson is that a schedule lets you use a big peak rate. You do not pay for it at the end of the run.

One fixed rate has to do two jobs that pull against each other. Early on, the weights are nowhere near good, so long strides make fast progress down the valley. Late in the run, the weights are close to the bottom. There, the same long strides keep carrying the model past it and back.

Near the bottom the real slope is gentle. But each step sees only a batch of 16 pairs, and that batch's slope is still rough. A big fixed rate turns every rough reading into a big hop. The model keeps getting knocked away from the bottom.

That is also why bigger batches help. At a peak of 30, Constant finishes at 1.883 with batches of 64. With batches of 16, it finishes at 3.020.

So a fixed rate forces a trade. Pick one small enough to settle at the end, and the start of the run crawls. Pick one big enough for a fast start, and the end bounces around the bottom instead of settling there.

Decay gets you out of that trade. It lets one run be bold at the start and careful at the end. Shrinking the learning rate shrinks every hop.

The trade also runs the other way. When the peak is small, decay throws away useful steps. The model is still far from the bottom when its steps become tiny.

Put together, that is why the winning schedule changes as you move **Peak learning rate**. At a small peak, Constant wins. At a big one, a decaying schedule wins.

The lab also separates overshooting from two problems that can look similar. One is **divergence**, where the numbers blow up until the run cannot go on. The other is an exploding gradient, where the push grows layer by layer in a deep model.

A cap on the push's length helps with overshooting and with an exploding gradient. No cap can rescue a gradient that has faded away, though.

Finally, every training result depends partly on luck. A different shuffle of the same examples gives a slightly different result. You need to measure how much the shuffle alone moves a result. Otherwise you cannot tell a real improvement from a lucky draw. That is why the lab can retrain everything with five seeds.

## How to play with it

### Read the schedule before any loss

Click through all four **Schedule** buttons, and at first watch only the schedule chart. The selected schedule is a thick solid line. The other three are thin dashed or dotted lines, and shaded bands mark its warmup and decay.

Pick **Warmup + decay** and drag **Inspect epoch**. A dot slides along the curve and reads the learning rate at that moment. Its label looks like "lr 0.378 at step 384", where lr is short for learning rate.

Set **Inspect epoch** to 1 and the rate reads 0.160. Set it to 3 and it reads 0.481, climbing through the shaded warmup toward the peak. Back at epoch 12, it reads 0.378, the example worked out above.

Next, drag **Warmup fraction** on **Optimizer controls**. Only the Warmup + decay and One cycle curves change shape, because Constant and Cosine decay never use that setting.

Then move **Peak learning rate** and watch the schedule chart again. The numbers on its side change, but every shape stays the same. The peak multiplies each schedule's factor. So it stretches every curve by the same amount without bending any of them.

### Watch the winner change

Three readouts sit under the loss chart, and the first is named after whichever schedule you picked. It reads **Cosine decay final loss** when the page first opens. It reads **Warmup + decay final loss** if you still have that schedule picked from the last step.

The other two always name the winner of all four and its score. They are **Best schedule here** and **Best final loss**.

**Try this:** Walk **Peak learning rate** up from 0.6. Watch **Best schedule here** change hands as the peak rises.

- At 0.6, the default, **Constant** finishes at 2.075 and **Cosine decay** at 2.255. The other two decaying schedules land within a few thousandths of Cosine decay.
- So at the default peak, **Best schedule here** reads Constant. It wins by a wide margin over all three decaying schedules.
- Somewhere between 4.5 and 5, **Best schedule here** stops reading Constant.
- At 6, Constant finishes at 1.880 and Cosine decay at 1.846. The order has flipped, and a decaying schedule now leads.
- At 12, Constant has gone backwards to 2.093, while Cosine decay has kept improving and reaches 1.816.
- At 30, Constant ends at 3.020, not far below the dotted line, while Cosine decay reaches 1.802.
- Between 35 and 38, Constant's final point crosses the dotted line on seed 1. It ends at 3.332 at a peak of 35 and at 3.537 at 38.

Look at the Constant curve itself at a peak of 30, though. It jumps up and down from epoch to epoch. It already pokes above the dotted line at almost half of its points. So 3.020 is only where a jumpy curve happened to stop. The seed band below will show this again.

Once the final point crosses, **Worse than chance** flips to 1 / 4. The **Stability readout** then names Constant as worse than a model that learned nothing. Meanwhile, the three decaying runs get exactly the same peak and still finish near 1.80.

### Break it, then cap it

Set **Peak learning rate** back to 30 and set **Batch size** to 2. Now every step averages over only 2 pairs. A batch that small gives a rough, jumpy push, as the class-poll example under "What to notice" explains.

The Constant run now ends at 12.325, which is far worse than knowing nothing. Its curve runs off the top of the chart, though the legend prints the true number.

Now raise **Gradient clip** to `norm ≤ 0.50`. Any gradient longer than 0.50 gets scaled down to that length before the step is taken. The same run comes back to 6.522. That is a real rescue, even though it still sits well above the line.

Then compare two more caps. A tighter cap of `norm ≤ 0.10` helps even more and brings the run down to 2.154. The far left of the slider means off, not an even tighter cap.

Slide the cap up toward 1.40, and it cuts fewer and fewer gradients. By about 1.0 the rescue is gone. One run there even reads 12.889, slightly worse than no clip. With five seeds, though, the clipped and unclipped results overlap. So that small difference is shuffle luck and not real harm.

Next, set **Gradient clip** back to `norm ≤ 0.50` and click **Constant** on the **Schedule** control. The dots on the **Stability readout** and the caption under them follow whichever schedule is selected. **Peak gradient norm**, by contrast, is the longest gradient from any of the four runs.

Each dot is one step's gradient length, and a dashed line marks the cap. The dots above the line are drawn darker. The **Clip** readout says "binding", and the caption under the dots counts how many steps the cap rescaled.

Set **Batch size** back to 16 and keep that `norm ≤ 0.50` cap. Constant now ends at 2.884, against 3.020 with no cap. The seed band in the next part shows why a change that small might be luck.

### Read the seed band

Put **Peak learning rate** back to 0.6, keep **Batch size** at 16, and slide **Gradient clip** back to off. Then switch **Seeds** to **Five seeds**. Every schedule now gets a shaded band. A table appears with each seed's final loss and a **Spread** column. Spread is the highest result minus the lowest.

The third readout also changes to **Best mean of five seeds**. The lab now picks the winner by its average over all five shuffles. With five seeds on, the lab trains twenty runs, so the sliders respond more slowly.

Read Constant's row first. It runs from 2.075 to 2.077, a band only 0.002 wide. The three decaying rows each move about 0.004 between shuffles, and they finish within 0.004 of each other.

Constant's average beats the best decaying schedule's average by 0.175. That is more than forty times the 0.004 that shuffling alone moves the decaying schedules. Every shuffle agrees that Constant wins at this peak.

The decaying schedules are another matter, because their order sits inside the band. The boxed note under the table warns that you cannot tell two of these schedules apart with one run.

Raise **Peak learning rate** to 6. Constant's band now runs from 1.880 to 2.008. The highest Cosine decay result is 1.847, which is lower than Constant's lowest. So all five shuffles agree that the order flipped.

Raise it to 30, and Constant's band stretches all the way from 2.395 to 4.673. Seed 1's 3.020, the number you saw before, is one of five results scattered across that wide range. Two of the five shuffles even end worse than chance.

**Worse than chance** still reads 0 / 4, though, because that readout counts only the seed 1 runs.

### Explode a gradient, then try to rescue a vanished one

Last, scroll to **Gradient through depth** and leave **Layers** at 24 and **Gain per layer** at 1.20. That slider reads "×1.20 · grows". It means each layer multiplies the gradient by 1.20 on its way back.

The chart draws each layer's gradient length. Layer 1, the input, is on the left, and the output is on the right. The side of the chart uses a log scale. Each gridline is ten thousand times the one below it. Here 1e4 means 10,000, 1e0 means 1 and 1e-4 means 0.0001.

On this kind of scale, multiplying by the same gain at every layer draws a straight sloping line. So an explosion shows as a steady climb. It runs from the output on the right to the input on the left.

**Input ÷ output gradient** is the gradient's length at layer 1 divided by its length at the output. It reads 66.2.

**Whole gradient length** reads 120. It combines all 24 layers the same way you found a gradient's length earlier. You square each layer's length, add the squares and take the square root.

The layers near the input are so large that the total comes to 120. That is more than any one layer. That is over 100 times the output's length of 1. The card uses that as its test for an explosion. So the boxed note at the bottom of the card says the gradient has exploded.

Set **Depth clip** to 1.0 and look at the table. The Whole gradient row now reads 1.000 under After the clip. The **Whole gradient length** readout still shows 120, because it always reports the length before the clip.

The clip has multiplied every layer's gradient by the same factor, 1 ÷ 120. That is about 0.0083, and the **Clip** readout rounds it to 0.008.

The first layer still gets 66 times what the output layer gets, because only the overall size changed. The boxed note now says the direction is kept. The whole gradient is scaled down to the threshold, which here means the cap.

Then set **Layers** to 40 and **Gain per layer** to 0.80. **Input ÷ output gradient** now reads 1.66 × 10⁻⁴. That is a short way of writing 0.000166. The × 10⁻⁴ part means "move the decimal point four places to the left".

The first layer now gets under a thousandth of the output layer's gradient. No position of **Depth clip** lifts it back up. The boxed note says the gradient has vanished before it reaches the early layers. It adds that a clip cannot bring it back.

Here the whole gradient is only about 1.67 long. A **Depth clip** of 1.0 multiplies every layer by 1 ÷ 1.67, which is about 0.600. That makes the faded gradient smaller still. From 2.0 upward, the clip never binds at all.

## What to notice

### On the schedule chart

- **Warmup fraction** moves only two of the four curves. **Warmup + decay** and **One cycle** read it, while **Constant** and **Cosine decay** ignore it completely.
- None of the shapes here reach zero. Cosine decay stops at 3 percent of the peak. Warmup + decay stops at 5 percent, and One cycle stops at 2 percent.
- How low to go is a real choice. Many published training setups stop near 10 percent of the peak. Others slide all the way down to zero.

### On the loss chart

- Constant does not get steadily worse as the rate rises. It first improves from 2.075 at a peak of 0.6 to about 1.86 near a peak of 4. It is already slipping by 6, where it finishes at 1.880.
- After that, it keeps getting worse, through 2.093 at 12 and 3.020 at 30. So the best fixed rate sits in a narrow window.
- Cosine decay bottoms out near 1.80 around a peak of 30. Then it creeps back up to 1.809 at the slider's top of 60. So a schedule makes a big peak rate usable, but not free.
- At a low peak, a decaying schedule can finish behind Constant. It spent the end of the run taking tiny steps. The run was not unstable. It was only unfinished.

### On the stability card

**Peak gradient norm** is the longest gradient any of the four runs produced. Here peak means longest, which is a different peak from **Peak learning rate**.

It shrinks as **Batch size** grows. With **Peak learning rate** at 30, the longest gradient is about 1.41 at a batch of 2. At a batch of 16, it is about 1.00. At a batch of 64, it drops to 0.181, which is smaller still.

Picture choosing a film for a class trip by asking classmates. Ask 2 people, and the answer swings wildly depending on which 2 you happened to ask. Ask 16, and the strong opinions partly cancel each other out, which leaves a steadier answer.

A batch works the same way. Pairs from different parts of the text push the weights in different directions. Averaging more of them lets those pushes partly cancel. The result is a shorter and steadier gradient.

The dots on the **Stability readout** show one gradient per step. At the default settings, with a batch of 16, most of them sit near 0.2.

A row of 25 higher dots, one per epoch, spreads from about 0.33 up to 0.8. Those belong to each epoch's last batch. It holds only 2 pairs, because 498 does not divide evenly by 16.

A **Gradient clip** set above about 1.4 never binds anywhere in this lab. "Where it breaks" explains why.

### Weight decay is not a schedule

**Weight decay** pulls weights a little toward zero on each step. Picture a rubber band tied between each number and zero. It shares a word with a schedule's decay, but it is a different thing. It shrinks the weights themselves, not the step size.

Here weight decay only makes the result worse. On **Constant** at a peak of 0.6, a weight decay of 0.02 changes the final loss. It goes from 2.075 to 2.664.

It also pulls only on the rows used in that step. A character's row decays only on steps where that character starts a pair in the batch.

Weight decay exists to keep weights small. That stops a model from leaning too hard on the quirks of its practice text. It is meant to help the model on text it has not seen. This lab never tests the model on such text, so it has nowhere to show that payoff.

### On the seed table

Picture two identical robot students, built exactly alike and starting with empty memories. They study the same deck of flashcards for the same test in exactly the same way. The only difference is that each deck is shuffled differently.

One scores 41 out of 50 and the other 42. Nothing else about them differs, so that one-point gap came from the order of the cards alone.

Say one robot later tries a new study method and gains one point. You could not tell that gain from a lucky shuffle. The seed band is the lab's way of measuring that kind of luck.

A gap between two runs counts as evidence only when it is bigger than the **seed band**. That band is the swing that the shuffle alone produces. At the default peak, Constant leads by 0.175 against a band of 0.004, which is a real lead.

The closest pair, One cycle and Cosine decay, differ by only 0.0002 on average. That is about a twentieth of their 0.004 spread, so one run cannot say which is better.

The band is not the same width everywhere. Constant's is only 0.002 wide at a peak of 0.6, but 0.128 wide at a peak of 6. At a peak of 30 it is 2.278 wide, nearly a thousand times wider than at the default.

The decaying schedules stay under 0.011 wide at all of these peaks. For Constant at a high peak, the result of a single shuffle is close to a coin flip.

The clip's rescue at **Batch size** 2 is bigger than the band. At a peak of 30, the five unclipped runs end between 9.854 and 12.325. Clipped at 0.50, they end between 6.168 and 6.522, so the two ranges never touch.

At **Batch size** 16, it is a different story. The clipped runs end anywhere from 2.308 to 3.606, which overlaps the unclipped range. So seed 1's move from 3.020 to 2.884 does not, on its own, prove that the clip helped.

### On the depth card

- A clip shrinks the whole gradient by one factor. Every layer keeps its share of the total, and only the overall length is capped.
- A clip can only shorten. Raising **Depth clip** never lifts a vanished gradient. A clip set above the whole length does not bind at all.
- At a gain of exactly 1.00, every layer's gradient has length 1. The squares are 24 ones that add up to 24. The whole gradient is then the square root of 24, about 4.9.
- The explosion or the collapse comes entirely from the gain being different from 1. The gain gets multiplied in once for every layer the gradient passes through.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Four real runs give four real curves. The single default **seed** makes sure the only difference between them is the schedule. That same fixed seed is also a limit, because every number you read is one sample from one shuffle.

**Five seeds** adds the missing measure of luck, but five is a small sample. The band is only the lowest and highest of those five results. It does not promise where a sixth shuffle would land.

The band also measures the shuffle order and nothing else. It says nothing about how a different text, model size or set of peak rates would change things.

**A common mix-up:** A rising loss is not an exploding gradient. This model cannot produce an exploding gradient at all, because of the way its pushes are built.

Each pair pushes only the row of its first character. On that row, the push for the right next character is 1 minus the model's chance for it. The push for every other character is minus the model's chance for that character.

Say the model is reading t and gives h a chance of 0.7 and e a chance of 0.2. It spreads the last 0.1 over the other 28 characters. Say the right next character is h. Then the pushes on the t row are 1 − 0.7 = 0.3 for h and −0.2 for e. The rest get tiny negative amounts.

The chances are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. So these pushes can never get very long. The worst case is a model that is completely sure of a wrong character, say e.

Then the push for e is 0 − 1 = −1. The push for h is 1 − 0 = 1, and every other push is 0. Squaring gives 1 and 1, which add up to 2. So the push's length is the square root of 2, about 1.41.

A batch averages these pushes, so its gradient can be no longer than that either. An average can never go past its biggest member. A class's average test score can never beat the top score.

That is why the measured **Peak gradient norm** stays between about 0.17 and 1.41. This holds at every setting the sliders reach. It is also why the **Stability readout** draws a dotted line labelled "√2 bound". A **Gradient clip** above that line could never bind.

What a high rate causes here is **overshooting**, over and over. Step after step carries the model past the low point instead of down into it. Real **divergence** is something else. The loss grows without limit until the weights become non-finite. That means values such as infinity, which the computer can no longer calculate with.

Once that happens, no later step can recover the weights. Divergence never happens inside these slider ranges, so here is how to tell the two apart in this lab. An overshooting run still ends at a number, even a terrible one such as 12.325.

A diverged run has no number left. The legend, the final-loss readout and the seed table would print "diverged" in its place. That label never appears here, because the worst run you can build stays finite and performs badly.

**Gradient clipping** still helps, and it is worth being exact about why. It caps how far one step can travel. That limits the damage from a long step, even when the gradient behind it was ordinary. It does the most at **Batch size** 2, where each batch gives the roughest estimate of the real slope.

The **Gradient through depth** card is how this lab shows an **exploding gradient**. It is a different kind of thing from the trained runs. It has no weights, no data and no training. Each layer multiplies the gradient by the same gain, and the gradient at the output is set to 1.

In a real network, each layer's factor changes. It depends on that layer's own weights, the kind of calculation it does and the input. So the card shows the mechanism, a multiplication that compounds once per layer. It also shows what a clip does to it. It does not show how any real network behaves.

The rest are this model's usual limits. It sees only one character before each guess and has only 900 weights. It has no hidden layers. Those are the extra stages of calculation that deeper models stack between input and output.

It has no tokenizer, the tool real models use to cut text into the pieces they read. It learns from a text only 499 characters long, and its **Peak learning rate** slider stops at 60.

It also uses plain gradient descent, the rule described earlier. Each weight moves by its push times the learning rate. It does not use Adam.

The plotted loss is training loss, measured on the same text the model learns from. So nothing here shows a setting helping the model handle new text.

### Where the real mechanism fails

Deep networks, the ones with many layers, differ in the way that matters most. Their gradients pass back through many layers and get multiplied together, so a small change in each factor compounds.

Move each of 24 factors from 1.20 to 1.25, and their product becomes about 2.7 times larger. If each factor grows from 1.20 to 1.50 instead, the product grows more than 200 times. More layers compound it further still. So after one update to the weights, a deep network's gradient norm really can jump. It can grow tenfold, a hundredfold or more in a single step.

A sudden spike like that warns of a step about to wreck the weights. Clipping was invented for those spikes. Both GPT-3 and Llama 2 clip the length of the whole gradient at 1.0. They measure that length across every weight in the model.

This lab never produces one of those spikes from a trained model. Do not read its runs as a demonstration of one. Only the arithmetic on the depth card shows the shape of the problem.

A clip also has a hard limit. It can only shorten. A gradient that has faded to almost nothing stays faded however you set the clip. Real models need other fixes for that. One is a shortcut that lets the gradient skip past layers, called a residual connection. Careful starting weights help too, and earlier labs cover both.

A schedule is a bet, too. It assumes the right step size changes in a fixed way as the run goes on. It follows the clock instead of how training is going. So the bet can be wrong for a given peak.

That is why this lab trains all four schedules instead of trusting the schedule chart. A curve that looks sensible can still lose.

**Warmup** is also played down here, because it matters much more with Adam. Adam is a more elaborate update method. It sizes each weight's step using an average of how big that weight's recent gradients have been. It keeps updating that average as training goes on.

In the first few hundred steps, that average is built from almost nothing. So early steps can be large and badly aimed. Warmup holds them back until the average has had time to settle.

## Going deeper

### The shape large runs report

A common choice is a short straight-line **warmup** followed by **cosine decay** down to 10 percent of the peak. Both GPT-3 and Llama 2 report this choice.

Its drawback is that the curve is drawn to reach its lowest point exactly at the planned last step. So you cannot extend the run later without redrawing the curve and throwing the old plan away.

Warmup-stable-decay schedules avoid that. They hold a steady rate for most of training and decay only in a short final stretch. You can keep training as long as you like and add the short decay whenever you decide to stop.

This lab does not include one. On its chart, such a schedule would sit between Constant and Warmup + decay.

### Learning rate and batch size move together

A bigger batch gives a steadier gradient, which can support a longer step. So the common rules raise the rate along with the batch. Double the batch, and those rules either double the rate or multiply it by about 1.4. That number is the square root of 2.

This lab shows the same link from the other side. Hold the rate fixed and shrink the batch, and the run gets much worse. On **Constant** at a peak of 30, a **Batch size** of 64 finishes at 1.883. A batch of 2 finishes at 12.325.

### Weight decay in large runs

There are two ways to add weight decay's pull toward zero. One folds it into each weight's push, so it travels along with the gradient. The other applies it to the weights directly, outside the push.

With plain gradient descent, the update rule this lab uses, the two ways give the same step. Adam resizes each weight's push by its own amount, though. A pull that travels inside the push gets resized too.

That makes the pull stronger for some weights and weaker for others. A version called AdamW keeps the pull outside the push. Every weight then gets pulled toward zero in the same proportion.

### One shot at the real thing

Picture a baker asked to make one enormous wedding cake, with no chance of a second try. Instead of experimenting on the big cake, the baker would bake small test cakes first. They would find the oven settings that work and use a rule to scale them up.

The largest AI training runs, sometimes called frontier runs, work the same way. Each one uses so much computer time that people do it only once. Their settings come from trial runs on much smaller stand-in models, plus a rule for carrying the results across. A wrong schedule cannot be run again.

### Toy versus real

- **Peak rate:** This lab's default peak is 0.6. Its useful range runs from about 0.5 to 40, using plain gradient descent. Runs that use Adam take far smaller rates, so the two kinds of number cannot be compared directly.
- **GPT-3's peak rates:** They run from 6.0 × 10⁻⁴, which is 0.0006, for its smallest version with 125 million weights. Its largest version, with 175 billion weights, uses 0.6 × 10⁻⁴, which is ten times smaller.
- **Llama 2's peak rates:** The published rate is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ for its 7 billion and 13 billion weight versions. Its 34 billion and 70 billion versions use half that, 1.5 × 10⁻⁴.
- **Warmup:** This lab warms up for 15 percent of the run, which is 120 of 800 steps. Llama 2 warms up for 2,000 steps.
- **Llama 2's run:** It trains on 2.0 trillion tokens, which are the pieces of text such models read. The batch is 4 million tokens, which makes about 500,000 steps in all. So its warmup is roughly 0.4 percent of its run.
- **GPT-3's warmup:** It warms up over its first 375 million of 300 billion tokens. That is 0.125 percent of its run. Both of those percentages are arithmetic on the papers' own figures, not numbers the papers state themselves.
- **The floor:** This lab's cosine decay bottoms out at 3 percent of the peak. Both papers decay to 10 percent of it. Both papers also use a weight decay of 0.1 and clip the whole gradient at a length of 1.0.
- **Seeds:** This lab can afford five runs per setting, but a frontier run happens once. That is why its settings come from trial runs on smaller stand-in models.

The real-world figures come from two papers. One is the GPT-3 paper by Brown and colleagues. The other is the Llama 2 paper by Touvron and colleagues.
