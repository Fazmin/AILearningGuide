## The big picture

A brand-new AI model, trained from scratch, starts out knowing nothing. It learns through a training program that has it make
guesses and then scores how wrong they were. After each score, the program nudges the model's
internal numbers a little so that the next guesses come out slightly better, and the cycle repeats.

Each nudge is called a step, and before the first one a person has to choose a handful of settings
that the model never learns for itself. How big should each step be, and should that size change as
training goes on? How many examples should the model look at before each step, and should any single
step be capped? These choices are called **hyperparameters**, and this module is about making them
well.

The most important one is the size of each step, called the **learning rate**. Set it too small and
the model creeps along, running out of time before it gets anywhere good. Set it too big and the
model keeps leaping past the good answer, and in this lab it can finish worse than a model that
never learned anything.

A **schedule** is a plan for changing the learning rate as training goes on, and the usual plan lets
the steps shrink toward the end. The same choices are made for large AI models trained on huge
amounts of text. The teams behind two well-known ones, GPT-3 and Llama 2, published their settings,
and both raised the step size gently over a short opening stretch. After that, both slid it down
along a smooth curve, and both put a cap on how big the push behind any single step could be.

Getting these settings wrong is costly, and the lab lets you see several of the ways that can
happen. Without a schedule, one step size has to suit both the start of training and the end, and no
single size does both jobs well. Without a fair way to compare two runs, you can crown a winner that
only won by luck. And for the largest models, a wrong schedule cannot be fixed by running it again.
A run that big uses so much computer time that it is done only once.

By the end of this module, you will be able to explain:

- how to read a learning-rate schedule, which is a chart of step size against how far through
  training the model is;
- why letting the step size shrink toward the end of training makes it safe to start with much
  bigger steps;
- how a run that keeps overshooting, stepping past the good answer, differs from one that truly
  breaks, and what putting a cap on each push, called gradient clipping, changes;
- how to tell whether one run really beat another, or whether the gap is no bigger than luck. The
  examples are shuffled into a new order each time, and the order alone can move a result;
- how big models, built from many stacked stages called layers, pass a message backward that tells
  each stage how to change. That message can grow huge or fade to almost nothing, and a cap can fix
  only one of those.

The lab gives you five cards to work with, and each one shows a different part of the story:

- **Learning-rate schedule shapes** draws four schedules as curves, so you can read how big the step
  is at every point in a run.
- **Loss for all four schedules** trains four real tiny models side by side, one per schedule. It
  plots how wrong each one is as it learns, a score called its loss. Its **Seeds** switch retrains
  every model with five different shuffles, so you can see how much luck alone moves each result.
- **Optimizer controls** holds five dials, and the optimizer it is named after is the part of the
  program that takes the steps. Its **Peak learning rate** dial sets the top step size, and it can
  make a run end up worse than knowing nothing.
- **Stability readout** shows how big the push behind each step was and whether a cap is cutting it
  down. It also counts how many runs finished worse than a model that knows nothing and guesses
  evenly.
- **Gradient through depth** is a separate piece of arithmetic, not a trained model, that shows what
  happens to a message passed back through many layers.

The first card draws each schedule straight from its formula, while the loss and stability cards
show numbers measured from real training runs. Those runs are retrained every time you move a dial.

## What it is

### The model and its loss

The model in this lab is tiny: it reads a 499-character text called Harbor weather notes, which
begins "the morning fog settles over the harbor". Its job is to guess each character of that text
from the single character before it.

It works with 30 characters: the 26 letters, a space, a full stop and two special marker symbols
that never appear in this text. Everything it knows sits in a table of 900 numbers called
**weights**, with one row of 30 scores for each character.

To guess what follows a t, the model looks up the t row and turns its 30 scores into chances for the
next character, with higher scores getting bigger chances.

All 900 weights start at zero, so every score in a row is the same, and the model gives every
character the same 1-in-30 chance. The examples it learns from are pairs of neighbouring characters,
and the whole text holds 498 of them. The word "the", for instance, gives two pairs: t followed by
h, and h followed by e.

To learn, the model needs a score for how wrong it is, called the **loss**. The loss is low when the
model gives the real next character a big chance, and high when it gives that character a small one.

The lab averages the loss over all 498 pairs and measures it in units called nats per token, where a
token here is one character. You will not need to work with the unit itself, because lower is always
better, and a few landmarks give the numbers meaning:

- A loss of 3.401 is what you get by spreading your bets evenly over all 30 characters, which is why
  the dotted line on the loss chart sits there.
- The dotted line's label reads "knows nothing · ln 30 = 3.401", where ln is a calculator function
  that turns 30 into 3.401, and you will never need to work it out.
- A loss of about 2.08 means the model is, overall, about as unsure as someone choosing fairly among
  8 characters each time.
- A loss of about 1.80 is about as unsure as choosing fairly among 6. That is where the best of the
  four runs finish when the top step size, the **Peak learning rate**, is set high.

### One step of training

At each training step, the training program takes a small group of pairs called a **batch**, 16
pairs by default. For every one of the model's weights, it works out which way to push that weight,
and how hard, so that the loss on those pairs would go down.

That list of pushes, one for each weight, is called the **gradient**. The program then moves every
weight by its push multiplied by one number, the **learning rate**, which decides how big each step
is.

As a worked example, say the gradient's push for one weight is 0.5 and the learning rate is 0.6.
That weight then moves by 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3 in the direction that lowers the loss, and doubling the
learning rate would double the move.

One pass through all 498 pairs is called an **epoch**. With batches of 16 it takes 32 steps: 31 full
batches, plus a last batch holding the 2 pairs left over. A full run of 25 epochs therefore adds up
to 25 × 32 = 800 steps.

The overall size of the gradient is called its length, or its **gradient norm**. To find it, you
square every push, add the squares together and take the square root of the total.

Picture a gradient with only two pushes, 3 and 4, whose squares are 9 and 16. Those add up to 25,
and the square root of 25 is 5, so that gradient has a length of 5.

It is the same sum you would do on a map: walk 3 steps east and 4 steps north, and you end up 5
steps from where you started. A gradient's length is that straight-line distance, worked out over
900 directions instead of two. It is not the number of pushes in the list.

The part of the training program that takes these steps is called the optimizer, which is where the
**Optimizer controls** card gets its name.

### The picture: walking downhill in fog

Picture standing on a hillside in thick fog, trying to reach the lowest point of the valley. You
cannot see the bottom, so all you can do is feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, take a
stride downhill, and then feel again.

Training is that walk: your height is the loss, your position is the set of 900 weights, and the
slope you feel is the gradient. The length of each stride is the learning rate times how steep the
ground feels, so steep ground and a big learning rate both mean a long stride.

Far from the bottom, long strides get you down quickly, but near the bottom a long stride carries
you right across the valley floor and up the other side. Then the next long stride carries you back
again, and the walk turns into a swing from side to side.

That is **overshooting**: a step so long that it carries the model past the low point instead of
down into it, so the loss stops falling or even rises. When a run keeps overshooting, step after
step, its loss curve stops falling steadily and starts jumping up and down from one epoch to the
next.

### A schedule: stride length set by the clock

A fixed learning rate keeps the same stride multiplier from the first step to the last, so the
strides change only with the slope. A **schedule** changes the learning rate as the run goes on, by
taking a top value, the **Peak learning rate**, and multiplying it by a factor of 1 or less. That
factor depends only on how far through the run the model is.

The first card, **Learning-rate schedule shapes**, draws that factor times the peak, with progress
through the run along the bottom and the learning rate up the side. Progress is counted in epochs,
so the bottom of the chart runs from 0 to 25.

Here the valley picture stops matching, because a hiker who sensed the bottom was near could choose
to take more careful strides. A schedule never makes that choice: it changes the learning rate by
the clock, following its fixed curve whether or not the model is anywhere near a good answer.

The ground in this lab also wobbles, because each step feels the slope of only one batch of 16
pairs. That slope is rough and points partly the wrong way. Near the bottom the real slope is
gentle, but each batch's rough reading is often much bigger. A big learning rate turns that rough
reading into a big hop away from the bottom.

Finally, the real valley has 900 directions to move in, one for each weight, while a hillside has
only north-south and east-west.

### The four shapes

The four schedules hand out long and short strides at different points in the run, and the falling
part of each curve is called its decay:

- **Constant** holds the full peak rate from the first batch to the last, which means the same
  learning rate for the whole walk.
- **Cosine decay** starts at the peak and eases down to 3 percent of it, with no slow climb at the
  start. Its name comes from cosine, a curve from trigonometry, but all you need is its shape: flat
  at first, steepest in the middle and flat again at the bottom.
- **Warmup + decay** climbs in a straight line from near zero to the peak, then falls in a straight
  line to 5 percent of the peak. How long the climb lasts is set by the **Warmup fraction** slider.
- **One cycle** starts at a tenth of the peak and climbs to the peak over the same warmup stretch,
  set by the same **Warmup fraction** slider. Then it curves down to 2 percent of the peak, ending
  below where it began.

The climb at the start of the last two is called **warmup**. Each stride is the learning rate times
how steep the ground feels, and the ground is at its steepest right at the start. The full peak rate
there could give an extra-long first stride that throws the model off course. Warmup keeps the rate
low for those first steps, then hands over to the long, fast strides.

In this lab the start is only a little steeper than later on, so warmup changes the results very
little. It matters much more with a different update method called Adam, which comes up under "Where
it breaks".

**Warmup fraction** sets the share of the run the climb takes: at its default of 15 percent, warmup
lasts 120 of the 800 steps.

As a worked example, the default **Peak learning rate** is 0.6. Take **Warmup + decay**: by epoch 12
its climb is over and it has come part of the way down, so its factor is 0.63. The learning rate at
that moment is then 0.6 × 0.63 = 0.378, which is the number the chart's dot shows at epoch 12.

### A cap on any single step

One more control works on the gradient itself rather than on the clock. **Gradient clipping** sets a
maximum length: whenever a batch's gradient is longer than that cap, every push in it shrinks by the
same factor until the length equals the cap.

In the valley, clipping means the ground can never count as steeper than a set amount. So at a given
learning rate, no stride can be longer than a set distance, however steep the ground feels, while
the direction stays the same.

Take the gradient with pushes 3 and 4, which has a length of 5, and set the cap at 1. Dividing both
pushes by 5 gives 0.6 and 0.8, which still point the same way as before. Their squares, 0.36 and
0.64, add up to 1, so the new length is exactly 1.

A gradient already shorter than the cap is left alone, so a cap can be switched on and still do
nothing. The lab's **Clip** readouts say "binding" when the cap shortened at least one gradient, and
"never binds" when none was long enough to reach it.

### Four real runs, one difference

The next card, **Loss for all four schedules**, trains four real copies of the model at once, one
for each schedule. All four read the same text, start from the same all-zero weights and see the
pairs in the same order, so only the schedule differs.

The pairs are shuffled into a new order at the start of every epoch, and a number called the
**seed** fixes how that shuffle comes out. Use the same seed and you get the same order every time.
In many models the seed also picks random starting weights, but here every weight starts at zero, so
the shuffle order is the only thing the seed changes.

Each curve shows the loss on the whole text at the end of each epoch. All four curves start on the
dotted "knows nothing" line, because zeroed weights give every character the same chance.

The **Worse than chance** readout on **Stability readout** counts how many of the four runs finish
above that line. Lower loss is better, so finishing above it means doing worse than a model that
knows nothing and spreads its bets evenly. That even guessing is the "chance" in the readout's name.

### A switch and a card for what one run cannot show

One run with one seed is one sample, and a different shuffle might have told a slightly different
story. Switch **Seeds** to **Five seeds** and every schedule is retrained with five shuffles, and
the range from its lowest result to its highest, the **seed band**, is shaded in.

The trained model also cannot show one famous way that real training goes wrong, so the **Gradient
through depth** card shows it with plain arithmetic instead. Large models, often called neural
networks, are built from many **layers**, stages of calculation stacked one after another.

To work out how to change an early layer, the gradient is passed backward from the output one layer
at a time, and each layer multiplies it by some factor. It starts at the output because that is
where the guess comes out and the mistake is measured.

Picture a line of 24 people passing a message from the front of a hall to the back. The front of the
line stands for the output layer, and the back stands for layer 1, where the input comes in.

The first person speaks at normal volume, and everyone after that repeats the message 1.2 times as
loudly as they heard it. Between 24 people there are 23 hand-offs, and each one multiplies the
volume by 1.2. So by the back of the line it is 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 and so on, 23 times over, which
makes it about 66 times as loud.

A gradient that grows like this on its way back is called an **exploding gradient**. Now make the
line 40 people long and turn the factor down to 0.8, so each person repeats the message at 80
percent of what they heard.

Over the 39 hand-offs the message fades to under a thousandth of its starting volume, which is
called a vanishing gradient. The card lets you cap an exploding gradient and then watch the same cap
fail to help a vanishing one.

Where the picture stops: in a real network each layer's factor is different, changing with that
layer's weights and the input. The card, by contrast, uses one fixed factor for every layer, which
its **Gain per layer** slider sets.

## Why it’s here

The learning rate decides whether a run works at all. The sharper lesson is that a schedule is what
lets you use a big peak rate without paying for it at the end of the run.

One fixed rate has to do two jobs that pull against each other. Early on, the weights are nowhere
near good, so long strides make fast progress down the valley. Late in the run, the weights are
close to the bottom, and the same long strides keep carrying the model past it and back.

Near the bottom the real slope is gentle, but each step sees only a batch of 16 pairs, and that
batch's slope is still rough. A big fixed rate turns every rough reading into a big hop, so the
model keeps getting knocked away from the bottom.

That is also why bigger batches help: at a peak of 30, Constant finishes at 1.883 with batches of
64, but at 3.020 with batches of 16.

So a fixed rate forces a trade: pick one small enough to settle at the end, and the start of the run
crawls. Pick one big enough for a fast start, and the end bounces around the bottom instead of
settling there.

Decay gets you out of that trade by letting one run be bold at the start and careful at the end,
because shrinking the learning rate shrinks every hop.

The trade also runs the other way, because when the peak is small, decay throws away useful steps.
The model is still far from the bottom when its steps become tiny.

Put together, that is why the winning schedule changes as you move **Peak learning rate**: at a
small peak Constant wins, and at a big one a decaying schedule does.

The lab also separates overshooting from two problems that can look similar. One is **divergence**,
where the numbers blow up until the run cannot go on. The other is an exploding gradient, where the
push grows layer by layer in a deep model.

A cap on the push's length helps with overshooting and with an exploding gradient. No cap can rescue
a gradient that has faded away, though.

Finally, every training result depends partly on luck, because a different shuffle of the same
examples gives a slightly different result. Without measuring how much the shuffle alone moves a
result, you cannot tell a real improvement from a lucky draw, so the lab can retrain everything with
five seeds.

## How to play with it

### Read the schedule before any loss

Click through all four **Schedule** buttons, and at first watch only the schedule chart. The
selected schedule is drawn as a thick solid line and the other three as thin dashed or dotted ones,
while shaded bands mark its warmup and decay.

Pick **Warmup + decay** and drag **Inspect epoch**: a dot slides along the curve and reads the
learning rate at that moment. Its label looks like "lr 0.378 at step 384", where lr is short for
learning rate.

Set **Inspect epoch** to 1 and the rate reads 0.160, then set it to 3 and it reads 0.481, climbing
through the shaded warmup toward the peak. Back at epoch 12 it reads 0.378, the example worked out
above.

Next, drag **Warmup fraction** on **Optimizer controls**: only the Warmup + decay and One cycle
curves change shape, because Constant and Cosine decay never use that setting.

Then move **Peak learning rate** and watch the schedule chart again: the numbers on its side change,
but every shape stays the same. That is because the peak multiplies each schedule's factor, so it
stretches every curve by the same amount without bending any of them.

### Watch the winner change

Three readouts sit under the loss chart, and the first is named after whichever schedule you picked.
It reads **Cosine decay final loss** when the page first opens, but **Warmup + decay final loss** if
you still have that schedule picked from the last step.

The other two, **Best schedule here** and **Best final loss**, always name the winner of all four
and its score.

**Try this:** walk **Peak learning rate** up from 0.6 and watch **Best schedule here** change hands
as the peak rises.

- At 0.6, the default, **Constant** finishes at 2.075 and **Cosine decay** at 2.255, with the other
  two decaying schedules within a few thousandths of Cosine decay.
- So at the default peak, **Best schedule here** reads Constant, and it wins by a wide margin over
  all three decaying schedules.
- Somewhere between 4.5 and 5, **Best schedule here** stops reading Constant.
- At 6, Constant finishes at 1.880 and Cosine decay at 1.846, so the order has flipped and a
  decaying schedule now leads.
- At 12, Constant has gone backwards to 2.093, while Cosine decay has kept improving and reaches
  1.816.
- At 30, Constant ends at 3.020, not far below the dotted line, while Cosine decay reaches 1.802.
- Between 35 and 38, Constant's final point crosses the dotted line on seed 1, ending at 3.332 at a
  peak of 35 and at 3.537 at 38.

Look at the Constant curve itself at a peak of 30, though. It jumps up and down from epoch to epoch
and already pokes above the dotted line at almost half of its points. So 3.020 is only where a jumpy
curve happened to stop, which the seed band below will show again.

Once the final point crosses, **Worse than chance** flips to 1 / 4, and the **Stability readout**
names Constant as worse than a model that learned nothing. Meanwhile the three decaying runs, fed
exactly the same peak, still finish near 1.80.

### Break it, then cap it

Set **Peak learning rate** back to 30 and set **Batch size** to 2, so every step averages over only
2 pairs. A batch that small gives a rough, jumpy push, as the class-poll example under "What to
notice" explains.

The Constant run now ends at 12.325, far worse than knowing nothing, and its curve runs off the top
of the chart, though the legend prints the true number.

Now raise **Gradient clip** to `norm ≤ 0.50`, so any gradient longer than 0.50 is scaled down to
that length before the step is taken. The same run comes back to 6.522, which is a real rescue even
though it still sits well above the line.

Then compare two more caps, starting with a tighter cap of `norm ≤ 0.10`, which helps even more and
brings the run down to 2.154. The far left of the slider means off, not an even tighter cap.

Caps slid up toward 1.40 cut fewer and fewer gradients, and by about 1.0 the rescue is gone. One run
there even reads 12.889, slightly worse than no clip. With five seeds, though, the clipped and
unclipped results overlap, so that small difference is shuffle luck rather than real harm.

Next, set **Gradient clip** back to `norm ≤ 0.50` and click **Constant** on the **Schedule**
control. The dots on the **Stability readout** and the caption under them follow whichever schedule
is selected. **Peak gradient norm**, by contrast, is the longest gradient from any of the four runs.

Each dot is one step's gradient length, a dashed line marks the cap, and the dots above it are drawn
darker. Its **Clip** readout says "binding", and the caption under the dots counts how many steps
the cap rescaled.

Set **Batch size** back to 16 and keep that `norm ≤ 0.50` cap: Constant now ends at 2.884, against 3.020 with
no cap. The seed band in the next part shows why a change that small might be luck.

### Read the seed band

Put **Peak learning rate** back to 0.6, keep **Batch size** at 16, slide **Gradient clip** back to
off, and switch **Seeds** to **Five seeds**. Every schedule now gets a shaded band, and a table
appears with each seed's final loss and a **Spread** column, which is the highest minus the lowest.

The third readout also changes to **Best mean of five seeds**, because the winner is now picked by
its average over all five shuffles. With five seeds on, the lab trains twenty runs, so the sliders
respond more slowly.

Read Constant's row first: it runs from 2.075 to 2.077, a band only 0.002 wide. The three decaying
rows each move about 0.004 between shuffles, and they finish within 0.004 of each other.

So Constant's average beats the best decaying schedule's average by 0.175. That is more than forty
times the 0.004 that shuffling alone moves the decaying schedules, and every shuffle agrees that
Constant wins at this peak.

The decaying schedules are another matter: their order sits inside the band. The boxed note under
the table warns that two schedules here cannot be told apart with one run.

Raise **Peak learning rate** to 6, and Constant's band now runs from 1.880 to 2.008. The highest
Cosine decay result is 1.847, lower than Constant's lowest, so all five shuffles agree that the
order flipped.

Raise it to 30, and Constant's band stretches all the way from 2.395 to 4.673. Seed 1's 3.020, the
number you saw before, is just one of five results scattered across that wide range, and two of the
five shuffles even end worse than chance.

**Worse than chance** still reads 0 / 4, though, because that readout counts only the seed 1 runs.

### Explode a gradient, then try to rescue a vanished one

Last, scroll to **Gradient through depth** and leave **Layers** at 24 and **Gain per layer** at
1.20. That slider reads "×1.20 · grows", meaning each layer multiplies the gradient by 1.20 on its
way back.

The chart draws each layer's gradient length, with layer 1, the input, on the left and the output on
the right. Its side uses a log scale, where each gridline is ten thousand times the one below it:
1e4 means 10,000, 1e0 means 1 and 1e-4 means 0.0001.

On this kind of scale, multiplying by the same gain at every layer draws a straight sloping line. So
an explosion shows as a steady climb from the output on the right to the input on the left.

**Input ÷ output gradient**, the gradient's length at layer 1 divided by its length at the output,
reads 66.2.

**Whole gradient length** reads 120, because it combines all 24 layers the same way you found a
gradient's length earlier. You square each layer's length, add the squares and take the square root.

The layers near the input are so large that the total comes to 120, more than any one layer. That is
over 100 times the output's length of 1, which is the card's test for an explosion. So the boxed
note at the bottom of the card says the gradient has exploded.

Set **Depth clip** to 1.0 and look at the table, where the Whole gradient row now reads 1.000 under
After the clip. The **Whole gradient length** readout still shows 120, because it always reports the
length before the clip.

The clip has multiplied every layer's gradient by the same factor, 1 ÷ 120, which is about 0.0083
and which the **Clip** readout rounds to 0.008.

The first layer still gets 66 times what the output layer gets, because only the overall size
changed. The boxed note now says the direction is kept and the whole gradient is scaled down to the
threshold, which here means the cap.

Then set **Layers** to 40 and **Gain per layer** to 0.80, and **Input ÷ output gradient** now reads
1.66 × 10⁻⁴. That is a short way of writing 0.000166, because × 10⁻⁴ means "move the decimal point
four places to the left".

So the first layer now gets under a thousandth of the output layer's gradient, and no position of
**Depth clip** lifts it back up. The boxed note says the gradient has vanished before it reaches the
early layers, and that a clip cannot bring it back.

Here the whole gradient is only about 1.67 long, so a **Depth clip** of 1.0 multiplies every layer
by 1 ÷ 1.67, about 0.600. That makes the faded gradient smaller still, and from 2.0 upward the clip
never binds at all.

## What to notice

### On the schedule chart

- **Warmup fraction** moves only two of the four curves: **Warmup + decay** and **One cycle** read
  it, while **Constant** and **Cosine decay** ignore it completely.
- None of the shapes here reach zero, since Cosine decay stops at 3 percent of the peak,
  Warmup + decay at 5 percent and One cycle at 2 percent.
- How low to go is a real choice: many published training setups stop near 10 percent of the peak,
  while others slide all the way down to zero.

### On the loss chart

- Constant does not get steadily worse as the rate rises, because it first improves from 2.075 at a
  peak of 0.6 to about 1.86 near a peak of 4. It is already slipping by 6, where it finishes at
  1.880.
- After that it keeps getting worse, through 2.093 at 12 and 3.020 at 30, so the best fixed rate
  sits in a narrow window.
- Cosine decay bottoms out near 1.80 around a peak of 30, then creeps back up to 1.809 at the
  slider's top of 60. So a schedule makes a big peak rate usable, but not free.
- At a low peak, a decaying schedule can finish behind Constant because it spent the end of the run
  taking tiny steps, so the run was not unstable, only unfinished.

### On the stability card

**Peak gradient norm** is the longest gradient any of the four runs produced, and peak here means
longest, a different peak from **Peak learning rate**.

It shrinks as **Batch size** grows. With **Peak learning rate** at 30, the longest gradient is about
1.41 at a batch of 2 and about 1.00 at a batch of 16. At a batch of 64 it drops to 0.181, smaller
still.

Picture choosing a film for a class trip by asking classmates: ask 2 people, and the answer swings
wildly depending on which 2 you happened to ask. Ask 16, and the strong opinions partly cancel each
other out, leaving a steadier answer.

A batch works the same way, because pairs from different parts of the text push the weights in
different directions. Averaging more of them lets those pushes partly cancel, which gives a shorter
and steadier gradient.

The dots on the **Stability readout** show one gradient per step, and at the default settings, with
a batch of 16, most of them sit near 0.2.

A row of 25 higher dots, one per epoch, spreads from about 0.33 up to 0.8. Those belong to each
epoch's last batch, which holds only 2 pairs because 498 does not divide evenly by 16.

**Gradient clip** set above about 1.4 never binds anywhere in this lab, for a reason explained under
"Where it breaks".

### Weight decay is not a schedule

**Weight decay** pulls weights a little toward zero on each step, like a rubber band tied between
each number and zero. Despite the shared word, it is a different thing from a schedule's decay,
because it shrinks the weights themselves rather than the step size.

Here weight decay only makes the result worse: on **Constant** at a peak of 0.6, a weight decay of
0.02 takes the final loss from 2.075 to 2.664.

It also pulls only on the rows used in that step. So a character's row decays only on steps where
that character was the first character of a pair in the batch.

Weight decay exists to keep weights small, which stops a model from leaning too hard on the quirks
of its practice text. That is meant to help it on text it has not seen. This lab never tests the
model on such text, so it has nowhere to show that payoff.

### On the seed table

Picture two identical robot students, built exactly alike and starting with empty memories. They
study the same deck of flashcards for the same test in exactly the same way, except that each deck
is shuffled differently.

One scores 41 out of 50 and the other 42, and since nothing else about them differs, that one-point
gap came from the order of the cards alone.

If one robot later tries a new study method and gains one point, you could not tell that gain from a
lucky shuffle. The seed band is the lab's way of measuring that kind of luck.

A gap between two runs counts as evidence only when it is bigger than the **seed band**, the swing
the shuffle alone produces. At the default peak, Constant leads by 0.175 against a band of 0.004,
which is a real lead.

The closest pair, One cycle and Cosine decay, differ by only 0.0002 on average. That is only about a
twentieth of their 0.004 spread, so one run cannot say which is better.

The band is not the same width everywhere: Constant's is only 0.002 wide at a peak of 0.6, but 0.128
wide at a peak of 6. At a peak of 30 it is 2.278 wide, nearly a thousand times wider than at the
default.

The decaying schedules stay under 0.011 wide at all of these peaks. For Constant at a high peak,
though, the result of a single shuffle is close to a coin flip.

The clip's rescue at **Batch size** 2 is bigger than the band. At a peak of 30, the five unclipped
runs end between 9.854 and 12.325. Clipped at 0.50, they end between 6.168 and 6.522, so the two
ranges never touch.

At **Batch size** 16 it is a different story, because the clipped runs end anywhere from 2.308 to
3.606, which overlaps the unclipped range. So seed 1's move from 3.020 to 2.884 does not, on its
own, prove that the clip helped.

### On the depth card

- A clip shrinks the whole gradient by one factor, so every layer keeps its share of the total and
  only the overall length is capped.
- A clip can only shorten, so raising **Depth clip** never lifts a vanished gradient, and a clip set
  above the whole length does not bind at all.
- At a gain of exactly 1.00 every layer's gradient has length 1, so the squares are 24 ones that add
  up to 24. The whole gradient is then the square root of 24, about 4.9.
- The explosion or the collapse comes entirely from the gain being different from 1, multiplied in
  once for every layer the gradient passes through.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Four real runs give four real curves, and the single default **seed** makes sure the only difference
between them is the schedule. That same fixed seed is also a limit, because every number you read is
one sample from one shuffle.

**Five seeds** adds the missing measure of luck, but five is a small sample. The band is only the
lowest and highest of those five results, not a promise about where a sixth shuffle would land.

The band also measures the shuffle order and nothing else, so it says nothing about how a different
text, model size or set of peak rates would change things.

**A common mix-up:** a rising loss is not an exploding gradient. This model cannot produce an
exploding gradient at all, because of the way its pushes are built.

Each pair pushes only the row of its first character. On that row, the push for the right next
character is 1 minus the model's chance for it. The push for every other character is minus the
model's chance for that character.

Say the model is reading t and gives h a chance of 0.7 and e a chance of 0.2, spreading the last 0.1
over the other 28 characters. If the right next character is h, the pushes on the t row are
1 − 0.7 = 0.3 for h, −0.2 for e, and tiny negative amounts for the rest.

The chances are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1, so these pushes can never get very long. The worst
case is a model that is completely sure of a wrong character, say e.

Then the push for e is 0 − 1 = −1, the push for h is 1 − 0 = 1, and every other push is 0. Squaring
gives 1 and 1, which add up to 2, so the push's length is the square root of 2, about 1.41.

A batch averages these pushes, so its gradient can be no longer than that either. An average can
never go past its biggest member, in the same way that a class's average test score can never beat
the top score.

That is why the measured **Peak gradient norm** stays between about 0.17 and 1.41 at every setting
the sliders reach. It is also why the **Stability readout** draws a dotted line labelled "√2 bound",
and why a **Gradient clip** above that line could never bind.

What a high rate causes here is **overshooting**, over and over, with step after step carrying the
model past the low point instead of down into it. Real **divergence** is something else: the loss
grows without limit until the weights become non-finite, values such as infinity that the computer
can no longer calculate with.

Once that happens no later step can recover them, but divergence never happens inside these slider
ranges. So here is how to tell the two apart in this lab: an overshooting run still ends at a
number, even a terrible one such as 12.325.

A diverged run has no number left, and the legend, the final-loss readout and the seed table print
"diverged" in its place. That label never appears here, because the worst run you can build stays
finite and performs badly.

**Gradient clipping** still helps, and it is worth being exact about why. It caps how far one step
can travel, which limits the damage from a long step even when the gradient behind it was ordinary.
It does the most at **Batch size** 2, where each batch gives the roughest estimate of the real
slope.

The **Gradient through depth** card is how this lab shows an **exploding gradient**, and it is a
different kind of thing from the trained runs. It has no weights, no data and no training: each
layer multiplies the gradient by the same gain, and the gradient at the output is set to 1.

In a real network, each layer's factor changes with its own weights, with the kind of calculation
that layer does, and with the input. So the card shows the mechanism, a multiplication that
compounds once per layer, and what a clip does to it, but not how any real network behaves.

The rest are this model's usual limits, starting with the fact that it sees only one character
before each guess and has only 900 weights. It has no hidden layers, the extra stages of calculation
that deeper models stack between input and output.

It has no tokenizer, the tool real models use to cut text into the pieces they read, and it learns
from a text only 499 characters long. Its **Peak learning rate** slider stops at 60.

It also uses plain gradient descent, the simple rule described earlier of moving each weight by its
push times the learning rate, instead of Adam.

The plotted loss is training loss, measured on the same text the model learns from, so nothing here
shows a setting helping the model handle new text.

### Where the real mechanism fails

Deep networks, the ones with many layers, differ in the way that matters most. Their gradients pass
back through many layers and get multiplied together, so a small change in each factor compounds.

Moving each of 24 factors from 1.20 to 1.25, for example, makes their product about 2.7 times
larger. If each factor grows from 1.20 to 1.50 instead, the product grows more than 200 times, and
more layers compound it further still. So after one update to the weights, a deep network's gradient
norm really can jump tenfold, a hundredfold or more in a single step.

A sudden spike like that is the warning sign of a step about to wreck the weights, and those spikes
are what clipping was invented for. Both GPT-3 and Llama 2 clip the length of the whole gradient,
measured across every weight in the model, at 1.0.

This lab never produces one of those spikes from a trained model, so do not read its runs as a
demonstration of one. Only the arithmetic on the depth card shows the shape of the problem.

A clip also has a hard limit, because it can only shorten, so a gradient that has faded to almost
nothing stays faded however you set the clip. Real models need other fixes for that, such as
shortcuts that let the gradient skip past layers, called residual connections. Careful starting
weights help too, and earlier labs cover both.

A schedule is a bet, too, because it assumes the right step size changes in a fixed way as the run
goes on. It goes by the clock rather than by how training is going, so the bet can be wrong for a
given peak.

That is why this lab trains all four schedules instead of trusting the schedule chart, since a curve
that looks sensible can still lose.

**Warmup** is also played down here, because it matters much more with Adam. Adam is a more
elaborate update method that sizes each weight's step using an average of how big that weight's
recent gradients have been. It keeps updating that average as training goes on.

In the first few hundred steps, that average is built from almost nothing, so early steps can be
large and badly aimed. Warmup holds them back until the average has had time to settle.

## Going deeper

### The shape large runs report

A common choice is a short straight-line **warmup** followed by **cosine decay** down to 10 percent
of the peak, which is what both GPT-3 and Llama 2 report.

Its drawback is that the curve is drawn to reach its lowest point exactly at the planned last step.
So you cannot extend the run later without redrawing the curve and throwing the old plan away.

Warmup-stable-decay schedules avoid that by holding a steady rate for most of training and decaying
only in a short final stretch. You can keep training as long as you like and add the short decay
whenever you decide to stop.

This lab does not include one, but on its chart such a schedule would sit between Constant and
Warmup + decay.

### Learning rate and batch size move together

A bigger batch gives a steadier gradient, which can support a longer step, so the common rules raise
the rate along with the batch. Double the batch, and those rules either double the rate or multiply
it by about 1.4, the square root of 2.

This lab shows the same link from the other side: hold the rate fixed and shrink the batch, and the
run gets much worse. On **Constant** at a peak of 30, a **Batch size** of 64 finishes at 1.883,
while a batch of 2 finishes at 12.325.

### Weight decay in large runs

There are two ways to add weight decay's pull toward zero: one folds it into each weight's push, so
it travels along with the gradient. The other applies it to the weights directly, outside the push.

With plain gradient descent, the update rule this lab uses, the two ways give the same step. Adam,
though, resizes each weight's push by its own amount, so a pull that travels inside the push gets
resized too.

That makes the pull stronger for some weights and weaker for others. A version called AdamW keeps
the pull outside the push, so every weight is pulled toward zero in the same proportion.

### One shot at the real thing

Picture a baker asked to make one enormous wedding cake, with no chance of a second try. Instead of
experimenting on the big cake, they would bake small test cakes first, find the oven settings that
work, and use a rule to scale them up.

The largest AI training runs, sometimes called frontier runs, work the same way, because each one
uses so much computer time that it is done only once. Their settings come from trial runs on much
smaller stand-in models, plus a rule for carrying the results across, and a wrong schedule cannot be
run again.

### Toy versus real

- **Peak rate:** this lab's default peak is 0.6, and its useful range runs from about 0.5 to 40,
  using plain gradient descent. Runs that use Adam take far smaller rates, so the two kinds of
  number cannot be compared directly. GPT-3's published peak rates run from 6.0 × 10⁻⁴, which is
  0.0006, for its smallest version with 125 million weights. Its largest version, with 175 billion
  weights, uses 0.6 × 10⁻⁴, which is ten times smaller. Llama 2's published peak rate is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴
  for its 7 billion and 13 billion weight versions. Its 34 billion and 70 billion versions use half
  that, 1.5 × 10⁻⁴.
- **Warmup:** this lab warms up for 15 percent of the run, which is 120 of 800 steps, while Llama 2
  warms up for 2,000 steps. Llama 2 trains on 2.0 trillion tokens, the pieces of text such models
  read, at a batch of 4 million tokens, which makes about 500,000 steps in all. So Llama 2's warmup
  is roughly 0.4 percent of its run. GPT-3 warms up over its first 375 million of 300 billion
  tokens, which is 0.125 percent of its run. Both of those percentages are arithmetic on the papers'
  own figures, not numbers the papers state themselves.
- **The floor:** this lab's cosine decay bottoms out at 3 percent of the peak, while both papers
  decay to 10 percent of it. Both papers also use a weight decay of 0.1 and clip the whole gradient
  at a length of 1.0.
- **Seeds:** this lab can afford five runs per setting, but a frontier run happens once, which is
  why its settings come from trial runs on smaller stand-in models.

The real-world figures come from the GPT-3 paper by Brown and colleagues and the Llama 2 paper by
Touvron and colleagues.
