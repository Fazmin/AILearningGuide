## Which way should you turn the dial?

A machine has one dial, `w`, and one meter, `L`, that reads how wrong it currently is. You cannot
see inside it. Which way should you turn the dial to lower the meter, and how far? Trying every
setting works for one dial and fails for a million, so the decision has to come from local
information: nudge the dial, watch the meter, and act on the ratio.

That ratio is a derivative. One derivative per dial makes a gradient, derivatives multiply along a
chain of computations, and repeatedly stepping against the gradient is gradient descent. The story
of the dial is deliberately kept free of machine-learning vocabulary until the challenge, where the
dial is named a parameter and the meter a loss.

### What you will be able to explain

- Why the difference quotient `ΔL/Δw` converges as the nudge shrinks, and what its limit measures.
- How the sign, size and zeros of `L′(w)` describe a curve, and why `L′(w) = 0` does not identify a
  minimum.
- How partial derivatives assemble into a gradient vector that is normal to the contour line and
  points in the direction of steepest ascent.
- Why the derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives of its links, and what one
  near-zero factor does.
- How the step size decides whether gradient descent crawls, converges, oscillates or diverges, and
  why it converges to a local minimum.

### What the lab shows

Every picture is a closed-form function evaluated in the browser. Nothing is measured from a trained
model and no data is involved, so every readout can be checked by hand. All dials run from 0 to 6.

## Build the idea, one card at a time

The lab has six cards, one idea each, and shows one per step; **Next** under a card opens the
following one. The first five are below, in order; the sixth is the challenge.

### Nudge the dial

The meter is `L(w) = (w − 3)² + 1`. With the dial at 4 and a nudge `h`, the card draws the two
points, the secant through them, and the difference quotient `[L(w + h) − L(w)] / h` as **Ratio**.

**Pause and predict:** if the nudge is halved from 1 to 0.5, does the ratio halve, double, or barely
move?

It barely moves. Numerator and denominator shrink together, and for this quadratic the quotient is
exactly `2(w − 3) + h`. At `w = 4` it reads 3, 2.5, 2.25, 2.1, 2.05 and 2.01 for nudges of 1, 0.5,
0.25, 0.1, 0.05 and 0.01. The limit as `h → 0` is the derivative, `L′(4) = 2`, shown as **Slope it
settles on**. The magnifier rescales the picture to the size of the nudge, and the curve inside it
straightens as `h` shrinks: a differentiable function is locally linear, which is why the quotient
converges.

Read `L′(w)` as an exchange rate that is valid only for small changes. The error of the quotient
here is the nudge itself, so a nudge of 1 is off by 1. At `w = 1.5` the derivative is −3, meaning
the meter falls as the dial rises.

### A slope at every point

The derivative is a function of `w`. The second card plots `L(w)` above `L′(w)` on a shared axis for
a curve with two minima, `L(w) = 0.2u² + 0.9 cos(1.8u) − 0.3u + 2.2` with `u = w − 3`. At `w = 1` the
meter reads 2.79 and the slope is −1.82. The sign of `L′` gives the direction of increase, its
magnitude gives steepness, and the hatched bands mark the intervals where `L` is decreasing.

**Pause and predict:** `L′` has three zeros on this curve. Are they all minima?

They are not. The stationary points are minima at `w ≈ 1.57` (L = 2.28) and `w ≈ 4.62` (L = 1.36)
and a maximum at `w ≈ 2.88` (L = 3.12). The alternative curve, **A shelf**, is `L(w) = 5 + 0.15u³`,
whose derivative `0.45u²` touches zero at `w = 3` without changing sign: an inflection point with a
horizontal tangent. A zero derivative is a first-order condition. Classifying the point needs the
sign change of `L′`, or the second derivative.

### Two dials, one slope each

With a second dial `b`, the meter is a surface `L(w, b)`, drawn as a contour map. Holding `b` fixed
and differentiating in `w` gives the partial derivative `∂L/∂w`; holding `w` fixed gives `∂L/∂b`.
On the round bowl `L = 1 + ½[(w − 3)² + (b − 3)²]` at `(4.5, 4)` they are +1.50 and +1.00, and the
card draws each as a leg.

**Pause and predict:** joined tip to tail, do the two legs make an arrow that points downhill,
uphill or along the contour?

Uphill. The vector of partials is the gradient, `∇L = (∂L/∂w, ∂L/∂b) = (1.50, 1.00)`. It is normal
to the contour line through the point and points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its
length, 1.80, is the rate of ascent in that direction. Descent uses `−∇L`.

The other two surfaces separate ideas that the bowl hides. On **Long valley**, `L = 1 + ½[0.25(w −
3)² + 2(b − 3)²]`, the gradient at `(1, 4)` is `(−0.50, +2.00)`: it points up the steep wall, and
`−∇L` does not point at the minimum. On **Saddle**, `L = 5 + ½[(w − 3)² − (b − 3)²]`, the gradient
vanishes at the centre although the surface rises along `w` and falls along `b`.

### Dials that drive dials

Now the dial reaches the meter through an intermediate value: `u = (w + 3)² / 4`, then `L = 10 σ(0.8
(u − 9))`, where `σ` is the logistic function. At `w = 3` the local derivatives are `du/dw = 3` and
`dL/du = 2`.

**Pause and predict:** how far does `L` move for a nudge of 0.1 in `w`?

About 0.6. The chain rule gives `dL/dw = (dL/du)(du/dw) = 2 × 3 = 6`: each link scales the change
passing through it. The card also evaluates the composition directly, and the true change for a
nudge of 0.1 is 0.602, because derivatives are first-order approximations.

At `w = 5` the first link is steeper, 4.00, but the logistic has saturated and its derivative is
0.029, so `dL/dw` is 0.12. The meter reads 9.96 there, near its maximum of 10. A vanishing factor
makes the upstream derivative vanish whatever the other factors are, and it says nothing about
whether the loss is low.

### Walk downhill

Gradient descent repeats `w ← w − η L′(w)` with a chosen step size `η`. The card opens on the
two-minimum curve, starting at 2.6, just left of the maximum at 2.88.

**Pause and predict:** the right-hand minimum is the lower one. Which does the walk reach?

The left one. `L′(2.6) = +0.608`, so the first step with `η = 0.2` goes to 2.478, and ten steps
bring the dial to about 1.57, where the meter reads 2.28. The update uses only local slope, so it
converges to the minimum of whichever basin it starts in. The steps shorten without any change to
`η`, because `L′ → 0` near the minimum.

The **Bowl** isolates the role of `η`. Since `L′ = 2(w − 3)`, each step multiplies the distance from
the minimum by `1 − 2η`. From a start of 5:

- `η = 0.05` gives a factor of 0.9, and the dial is still at 3.697 after ten steps;
- `η = 0.5` gives a factor of 0, an exact hit that is special to a quadratic with this curvature;
- `η = 0.9` gives −0.8, an oscillation that decays (5, 1.4, 4.28, …);
- `η = 1.1` gives −1.2, so the meter rises from 5.00 to 6.76 to 9.29 until the dial reaches its
  stop.

In general, descent on a quadratic with curvature `λ` is stable only when `η` is below `2/λ`. On the
two-minimum curve the left minimum has curvature of about 2.86, and at `η = 0.8` the walk never
settles there, alternating between about 1.30 and 1.98.

## Your turn: reach the valley floor

The challenge runs two-dial descent on the long valley from `(0.5, 4.5)`, where `L = 4.03`. The goal
is `L` under 1.05 within 12 steps; the minimum is 1.00. The only control is `η`.

The curvatures are 0.25 along `w` and 2 along `b`, so each step multiplies the two error components
by `1 − 0.25η` and `1 − 2η`. A small step, 0.10, kills the `b` error quickly but leaves the `w`
error at `0.975¹²` of its start, and the meter ends at 1.436. A step of 1.00 makes the `b` factor
exactly −1: the walk reflects between the walls without decaying and ends at 3.25.

On the slider’s grid of 0.05, every step size from 0.45 to 0.90 succeeds; 0.60 ends at 1.016. The
ratio of the two curvatures, 8, is the condition number, and it is what forces the compromise. The
step size must be below `2/λ` for the largest curvature, which makes progress along the smallest one
slow.

The step size is the learning rate, the dials are parameters, the meter is the loss, and the walk is
training.

## What this picture leaves out

### What the lab simplifies

- Every function is authored, low-dimensional and known everywhere. A real loss is evaluated only at
  the current parameters and has millions to billions of dimensions.
- The lab’s gradients are exact. Training estimates the gradient from a minibatch, so each step is
  noisy.
- The nudge in the first card is numerical differentiation. It is how the idea is introduced, not
  how gradients are computed: finite differences need one extra evaluation per parameter and suffer
  truncation and round-off error. Frameworks use reverse-mode automatic differentiation instead.
- Dials are clamped to the interval from 0 to 6, which hides true divergence.
- Landing on the minimum in one step at `η = 0.5` is a property of this quadratic.

### Where the real idea runs into trouble

- **Locality.** A gradient is a first-order statement about a neighbourhood. Its usable range is set
  by curvature, and ill-conditioned valleys make any single step size a poor compromise. Measured on
  real networks, full-batch gradient descent tends to operate near the stability threshold, with the
  largest Hessian eigenvalue hovering around `2/η`.
- **Stationary points that are not minima.** In high dimensions saddle points are much more numerous
  than local minima and are surrounded by plateaus where gradients are small and progress is slow.
- **Products of many factors.** A deep network’s gradient is a product of many local derivatives.
  Saturating units contribute factors near zero, which is the vanishing-gradient problem; factors
  above one compound into exploding gradients.
- **Local minima.** Descent converges to a local minimum of the basin it starts in. The one-dial
  picture overstates how much this matters for large networks, whose loss surfaces vary a great deal
  with architecture.
- **Training loss is not generalisation.** Descent lowers the loss on the data it was computed on,
  and this lab has no data.

## Where you will meet this again

- *Loss & gradient descent* runs the same walk on a loss surface built from data, over `w` and `b`,
  and studies the settle, zigzag and blow-up regimes in depth.
- *Backpropagation* applies the chain rule through a whole computation graph, from the loss back to
  every weight.
- *Training dynamics* follows the loss over thousands of steps and separates training loss from
  validation loss.
- *Hyperparameters & schedules* varies the learning rate over the course of a run.
- *Optimizers in practice* keeps the gradient and changes the step: momentum and adaptive methods
  target exactly the ill-conditioning in the challenge.
- *Vectors & matrices* supplies the object itself: the gradient is a vector.
