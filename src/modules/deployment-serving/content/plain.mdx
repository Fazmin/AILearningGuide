## The big picture

Training a model is only half of the work. Someone then has to run it on a real machine, so that
people can type questions and get answers back. Putting a trained model to work like this is called
**deployment**, and the everyday job of answering people’s requests with it is called **serving**.

This module covers the three questions behind serving a model: does it fit in the machine’s memory,
how fast does it write, and what does each answer cost? You will work out all three with plain
arithmetic, and you will see why the answers keep pulling against each other.

A model writes its reply one piece of text at a time. Each piece is called a **token**, and a token
is often a short word or part of a longer one. Almost every number in this lab is counted in tokens:
how long a conversation is, how many come out each second, and what a million of them cost.

This lesson keeps returning to one picture, where the machine is a reader at a desk with an enormous
book of numbers that stands for the model. To write each new token of a reply, the reader has to
flip through the whole book once, from the first page to the last. Each conversation also leaves its
own growing pile of notes on the desk. The desk is only so big, and that turns out to matter a great
deal.

### Where you have met it

If you have used a chatbot, a model like this was serving you, and you may have noticed a short
pause before the reply began. After that pause the words arrived a few at a time, and both the pause
and the pace are things this lab predicts.

This app serves a model too: in Settings, it can download a small model called Qwen3.5 2B and run it
on your computer as its local guide. The lab includes that exact model, so you can see why it fits
so comfortably on an ordinary machine. If you pick a cloud provider in the app instead, your words
travel to that company’s computers and are answered there.

The lab also includes real machines that people rent by the hour, such as the RTX 4090, a graphics
card made for home computers. Another is the H100 SXM, a much bigger chip made for data centres, the
buildings full of computers that run online services. On the price pages the lab quotes, an hour
costs $0.74 on the first and $3.49 on the second.

### Why it matters

A model that loads with plenty of room to spare can still run out of memory once many people use it
at the same time. That catches people out, because the part of memory that grows is not the model
itself but the notes kept for each conversation. Without a budget, the busiest moment of the day is
exactly when the machine runs out of room.

Speed and money pull against each other too, because one machine can answer many people at once and
spread its hourly rent over far more tokens. The catch is that each person then waits a little
longer for every token. Choosing where to sit on that trade is a big part of serving a model well.

Running a model for real is also a job that never quite ends. The questions people ask change over
time, and new versions have to replace old ones safely. Someone also has to decide where people’s
words are allowed to go once they have typed them.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- why the model’s own numbers take memory once, while the notes kept for each conversation, called
  the **KV cache**, take memory again for every person and every token;
- why serving many people together raises the total number of tokens written each second, while each
  person’s own tokens arrive more slowly;
- what a trained model has to be turned into, step by step, before a program can serve it to people;
- how swapping the machine, or switching on one of three tricks, changes how many conversations fit,
  their speed and their cost. The tricks are handing out memory in small pages (**paged
  attention**), writing the notes in shorthand (**KV-cache quantization**), and letting a small
  helper guess ahead (**speculative decoding**);
- what running a model for real adds beyond speed: watching for changes, swapping in new versions
  safely, putting rules around the model, and knowing where people’s prompts go.

## What it is

This page does arithmetic, not measurement: you state your assumptions, do the division, and find
out which resource runs out first. The note under the throughput chart lists every assumption the
first three cards use, so you can disagree with any of them.

The models in the lab use the real shapes of published models, so `1.5B` and `3B` follow two Qwen2.5
models and `8B` follows Llama-3.1-8B. The fourth, `2B · this app`, is the very model this app
downloads to run on your computer. For the first three, the size of each storage format comes from
real file sizes reported by llama.cpp, the program this app uses to run its own model.

### A few words you need first

Everything a model learned during training is stored as a huge set of numbers called **weights**,
which are also called **parameters**. The model names in the lab tell you roughly how many there
are, so `8B` has about 8 billion weights and `1.5B` about 1.5 billion. The `8B` model has exactly
8.03 billion, and the lab uses that precise count in all of its sums.

A model is built as a stack of stages called **layers**, and every token passes through all of them
in order. The `8B` model has 32 of them, and they matter here because a model keeps a separate set
of notes in every layer.

Memory is measured in bytes, where a **bit** is a single 0 or 1 and 8 bits make one **byte**, enough
room for one letter of plain text. Larger amounts use units that each step up by 1,024, which suits
computers because 1,024 is ten 2s multiplied together, 2 × 2 × 2 and so on, ten times:

- a **KiB**, or kibibyte, is 1,024 bytes, about as much as a paragraph of plain text;
- a **MiB**, or mebibyte, is 1,024 KiB, which is a little over a million bytes;
- a **GiB**, or gibibyte, is 1,024 MiB, which is a little over a billion bytes.

A **GB**, or gigabyte, is exactly a billion bytes, so the same amount of memory looks a little
bigger when it is written in GB than in GiB. Times are mostly given in milliseconds, written **ms**,
where 1 ms is a thousandth of a second. Speeds are given in tokens per second, which the lab writes
as **tok/s**.

### Weights: memory you pay for once

Before a model can write anything, all of its weights have to be loaded into the machine’s memory.
How much room they take depends on how many bits each weight is stored in, which is what the
**Weight format** control picks.

`F16` stores each weight in 16 bits, which is 2 bytes, while the other formats, such as `Q4_K_M`,
are rounded versions from the *Quantization* lab. Those squeeze each weight into fewer bits at some
cost in quality. For example, a weight like 0.73128 might be stored as the nearest of a small set of
allowed values, such as 0.75.

Rounded formats handle the weights in small groups called **blocks**. Each block stores a few extra
bits that record how its weights were rounded, and the recipe a format follows for its blocks is
called its **block type**. Those shared extra bits are spread over every weight in the block, so the
bits per weight need not be a whole number.

The note on **Serving controls** gives `Q4_K_M` as 4.90
bits per weight for `8B`, as an average over the whole file.

To get the size of the weights, multiply the number of weights by the bits per weight, then divide
by 8 to turn bits into bytes. For `8B` at `Q4_K_M`, that is 8.03 billion × 4.90 ÷ 8, which comes to
about 4.9 billion bytes. Because each GiB is a little more than a billion bytes, the lab shows that
as 4.58 GiB.

At `F16`, the same model needs 2 bytes for every weight, which comes to about 16 billion bytes, or
14.96 GiB. That is more than three times the room for exactly the same model, with nothing changed
but how its numbers are written down.

The weights are loaded **once** and then shared, so whether one person or thirty people are using
the model, there is only one copy in memory. That is why the weights part of the **Memory budget**
bar never moves when you add users or make conversations longer.

### The KV cache: memory you pay for every conversation

To write each new token, a model looks back over every token that came before it. This
looking-back step is called **attention**, and the *Attention* lab showed it in detail.

For each earlier token, the model uses two lists of numbers that it worked out when it first read
that token. One list is a **key**, a label describing what that token offers. The other is a
**value**, the information that token hands over, in proportion to how much attention it gets.

Take the sentence “The dog chased the ball because it was bored.” To make sense of “it”, the model
compares what it is looking for with the keys of the earlier words. “Dog” matches best, so the model
borrows mostly from the value of “dog”, and only a little from the other words.

Working out those lists again for every earlier token, each time a new token is written, would
repeat a huge amount of work. So the model saves every token’s keys and values the moment it makes
them, in every layer, and reads them back later. That store of saved keys and values is the **KV
cache**, where KV stands for keys and values.

The cache grows by one token’s worth every time a token is read or written. Each conversation also
needs a cache of its own, because its words are different from everyone else’s. That makes the cache
the part of memory that grows as more people use the model, and as their conversations get longer.

The lab calls one conversation being served a **stream**, which is why the **Concurrent users**
slider reads in streams, such as `8 streams`.

### How big one token’s notes are

The **Memory budget** card shows the cost of one token’s notes as **Per token, per stream**, and the
card’s formula multiplies a few numbers that the model’s design fixes:

```text
2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × 2 bytes
```

Inside each layer, the looking-back is split into several lookups that run side by side. Each lookup
reads from a set of saved keys and values, and several lookups may share one set. These saved sets
are called **KV heads**. Each saved key or
value is a list of numbers, and the length of that list is the **head dim**, short for head
dimension.

The first 2 is there because every token saves one key and one value. The last 2 is the bytes each
number takes in **fp16**, the 16-bit format that the lab uses for the cache.

Now work it out one step at a time for `8B`, which has 32 layers, 8 KV heads in each layer and a
head dim of 128. One key and one value in one head make 2 × 128 = 256 numbers. Eight heads make that
2,048, and 32 layers make it 65,536 numbers for each token.

At 2 bytes per number, that is 131,072 bytes, which is exactly 128 KiB. That is room for about
131,000 letters of plain text, saved for every single token of every single conversation.

The `3B` model has 36 layers but only 2 KV heads, so the same sum gives 36 KiB per token, and the
`1.5B` model pays 28 KiB. That means the `8B` model pays 3.6 times as much as the `3B` model for
each token. The KV heads cause all of that gap and a little more. Keeping 8 saved sets per layer
instead of 2 costs 4 times as much, and the `3B` model’s 4 extra layers pull that back down to 3.6.

A model can keep so few saved sets because several lookups share each one, a design called
**grouped-query attention**. The `3B` model keeps only 2 sets in each layer for all of its lookups
to share, while the `8B` model keeps 8.

### A whole conversation’s notes

One token’s notes are small, but a conversation holds thousands of tokens, and the **Context
length** slider sets how many, from 512 up to 32,768. The lab starts at 4,096 tokens, and **Per
stream at this context** multiplies that length by the cost of one token’s notes.

For `8B` at 4,096 tokens, 128 KiB × 4,096 comes to 512 MiB, which is half a GiB for a single
conversation. With 8 users, the cache is 8 × 0.5 = 4.00 GiB, which is already close to the 4.58 GiB
that the weights take.

The lab charges each conversation for its full context from the very start, even if it has used only
a few tokens so far. That is the worst case, as if every conversation might run to its limit, and
the part on paged attention shows how real servers do better.

### The memory budget: will it fit?

The machine the first three cards use, called `Lab default`, has 24 GiB of memory, and three things
have to fit inside it. Those are the weights, every stream’s cache, and a flat 1 GiB for
**activations + runtime**. That last part is working space for calculations in progress and for the
serving program itself.

**Streams that fit** is how many full conversations the leftover memory can hold, once the weights
and working space have taken their share. To find it, take the 24 GiB, subtract the weights and the
1 GiB of working space, and divide what is left by one conversation’s cache.

At the start, 24 − 4.58 − 1 leaves 18.42 GiB, and dividing 18.42 by 0.5 gives 36.84. The lab rounds
that down to 36, because a 37th conversation would only partly fit, and half a conversation is no
use to anyone.

**Headroom** is the memory still spare at the current load. With 8 users, the weights, cache and
working space add up to 9.58 GiB, which leaves 14.42 GiB spare. The badge at the top of the card
shows the same total as `9.58 GiB of 24.00 GiB`.

In the bar itself, thin lines split the cache into one slice per stream, so you can count the
conversations. A heavy line marks the 24 GiB limit, and anything that spills past it gets a dashed
outline you can spot at a glance.

### The book and the desk

Here is a picture that holds the memory budget together, and it will also explain where the speed
limits come from. Think of the weights as a giant reference book of numbers. The machine’s memory is
a desk, and that book must lie open on it the whole time the model is in use.

Each conversation keeps its own pile of notes on the desk, which is its KV cache, and every token
adds a little to its pile. A small corner of the desk stays clear as working space, and the size of
the whole desk decides how many piles fit beside the book.

To write one token for a conversation, a reader flips through every page of the book and reads that
conversation’s pile of notes. How fast the pages turn decides how quickly one person is served, and
that page-turning speed is the idea behind the next part.

The picture stops matching in three places, so keep them in mind as you use it. First, the book is
not words that anyone reads with understanding, because it is numbers fed into calculations. Second,
a real model keeps a separate pile of notes for every layer. So picture the book as having one
chapter per layer, each chapter with its own notes. Third, reading a prompt works differently, as
the part on the first token explains.

### Writing speed: why the chip waits on memory

A model handles a request in two stages. It reads your whole prompt first, which is called
**prefill**, and then writes its answer, which is called **decode**. During decode, every new token
needs a full trip through all the layers, which means reading every weight out of memory once. The
next token cannot start until the last one exists, because each token looks back at all the ones
before it.

How fast memory can hand numbers to the chip that does the calculations is called **memory
bandwidth**. It is measured in GB/s, or billions of bytes per second. The `Lab default` machine
reads 400 GB/s, which sounds enormous until you remember that the weights alone are almost 5 billion
bytes.

Here is one decode step for a single user, which reads the weights, about 4.92 billion bytes, plus
that user’s half-GiB cache, about 0.54 billion bytes. That makes about 5.46 billion bytes to read
for every single token the user gets.

At 400 billion bytes a second, reading those 5.46 billion bytes takes about 0.0136 seconds, which is
about 14 ms. One token every 14 ms works out to 73.3 tokens per second, which is the number the lab
shows for one user.

Now compare that with the arithmetic, where each weight is used in about two calculations for every
token, one multiplication and one addition. A single calculation like this on decimal numbers is
called a **FLOP**, so each token of the `8B` model costs about 16 billion FLOPs.

The `Lab default` chip can do 120 trillion FLOPs a second, written 120 TFLOP/s, so the arithmetic
alone could produce about 7,472 tokens a second. That is roughly 100 times what memory allows, so
the chip’s calculators spend most of each step sitting idle, waiting for numbers to arrive.

When the time it takes to read memory sets the speed, rather than the time it takes to calculate, a
task is called **memory bandwidth bound**. The **Throughput and latency** card has a badge that says
`bandwidth bound` for this reason. In the book picture, writing is slow because of the page-turning,
not because of the sums.

### Batching: one flip through the book for many conversations

If turning the pages is the slow part, the natural fix is to share each flip among many
conversations at once. While the reader turns through the book once, they write the next token for
every open conversation. Grouping streams into one step like this is called batching.

In a real server, finished conversations leave the group at every step while waiting ones join it.
Swapping conversations in and out at every step keeps the group full, which is why this is called
**continuous batching**. The lab’s `Continuous batching` setting of the **Scheduler** control keeps
it simpler, because a fixed number of streams share every step and nobody arrives or leaves. Every
stream in the group shares one read of the weights.

There is a catch, though, because each step still has to read every conversation’s own notes, and
each one’s cache is different. So the more conversations share a step, the longer that step takes to
finish.

Here is the step for 8 users, which reads the weights once, 4.58 GiB, plus eight caches of half a
GiB each, for 4.00 GiB more. Those 8.58 GiB are about 9.2 billion bytes, and reading them at 400
billion bytes a second takes about 23 ms. That one step produces one token for each of the 8 users.

So each user gets one token every 23 ms, which is 43.4 tokens per second. Together, the 8 users get
8 × 43.4 = 347 tokens a second. Compared with one user alone, the machine now writes almost five
times as many tokens in total, while each person’s tokens arrive more slowly.

Those two numbers have names, starting with **throughput**, the total tokens per second across
everyone, which the card shows as **Total**. **Latency** is how long one person waits, and here it
is the gap between one person’s tokens, shown as **Time per output token**. **Per user** shows each
person’s own tokens per second, which is one second divided by that gap.

Batching raises throughput and worsens latency. This trade is what the card’s name, **Throughput and
latency**, refers to, and its two charts show it side by side. The top chart, total tokens per
second, climbs as you add users, while the bottom chart, each user’s own speed, sinks.

Each extra user adds the same amount to every step, the time to read one more pile of notes. Early
on, that is small next to the shared weights, so adding a second user costs each person only a
little speed, from 73.3 down to 66.8 tokens a second.

As the piles of notes become most of each read, the machine spends most of each step reading notes,
so each extra user adds less and less to **Total**. The 2nd user adds about 60 tokens a second to
**Total**, but the 9th adds only about 22. By the 36th user, each newcomer adds only about 3, and
their tokens come mostly out of everyone else’s speed.

### One at a time

The other **Scheduler** setting, `One at a time`, has the reader serve a single conversation per
flip through the book. Each step still produces only one token, so the total stays at 73.3 tokens a
second however many people are waiting.

With 8 users, those 73.3 tokens are shared out at 9.2 each, and every user waits 109 ms between
tokens while the others take their turns. Batching gives each of those same 8 users 43.4 tokens a
second, so here it is better for everyone, even though nobody gets the machine alone.

### The first token: reading the prompt

Prefill is different from decode, because the whole prompt is already there, so the machine can push
every prompt token through each layer together. That means it reads the weights once for the whole
prompt instead of once per token, which changes which limit matters. With thousands of tokens
sharing one read, the reading is quick and the arithmetic becomes the wall, because each prompt
token still needs 16 billion FLOPs.

For a 4,096-token prompt, the `8B` model needs about 2 × 8.03 billion × 4,096 FLOPs, which is about
66 trillion. At 120 trillion a second, that takes 0.548 seconds, or 548 ms, which is about half a
second.

That wait is the **time to first token**, which is how long you wait before the first token of the
answer appears. In a real service it also includes any time spent waiting in a queue, but the lab
ignores queues entirely. The lab also assumes a prompt that fills the whole context, so its **Time
to first token** is the wait for the longest prompt allowed.

Here the book picture needs one addition. For a prompt, the reader turns through the book once and
handles every prompt token on each page, so page-turning costs little. The time spent on the sums,
which the picture leaves out, becomes the limit instead.

### The model this app runs: hybrid attention

The app’s own model, `2B · this app`, is Qwen3.5 2B, and it saves its notes in a different way from
the other three. It has 24 layers, but only every fourth one keeps a growing cache. Those 6 layers
are called full-attention layers, because they look back over every earlier token.

Run the same formula for those 6 layers, which have 2 KV heads and a head dim of 256: 2 × 6 × 2 ×
256 × 2 bytes. That gives 12,288 bytes, or 12 KiB per token. If all 24 layers kept a cache, it would
cost 48 KiB per token, four times as much.

The other 18 layers are a different kind, called Gated DeltaNet layers. Instead of a pile of notes
that grows, each one keeps a single running summary for each conversation. It rewrites that summary
as every new token arrives, and a summary that is carried forward and updated like this is called a
**recurrent state**.

The summary never grows however long the conversation gets, and for all 18 layers together it takes
19.27 MiB per conversation. The lab shows that as **Fixed state per stream**, and a model that mixes
growing-cache layers with fixed-summary layers like this is using **hybrid attention**.

In the book picture, 6 of the 24 chapters keep a growing pile of notes, while the other 18 keep one
index card per conversation. That card gets rubbed out and rewritten again and again, but it never
gets any longer.

Here is the payoff. At 4,096 tokens, one conversation on this model needs 12 KiB × 4,096 = 48 MiB of
cache. It also carries its 19.27 MiB summary, so the total is about 67 MiB per conversation. That is
roughly an eighth of the 512 MiB the `8B` model needs at the same length.

So 331 conversations fit in the same 24 GiB, where the `8B` model fits only 36. The 2B model’s
weights are small too. The file this app downloads is 1.19 GiB, which works out to 5.445 bits per
weight.

### The machine, and what a million tokens cost

So far the machine has been `Lab default`, but any real machine can be described by the same few
numbers. The **Hardware, levers, and cost** card lets you change each one of them, starting from a
preset:

- **Memory** is the size of the desk, measured in GiB, and it decides how many conversations can fit
  beside the book.
- **Memory bandwidth** is how fast the pages turn, measured in GB/s, and it decides how fast one
  conversation is served.
- **Compute** is how fast the sums get done, in TFLOP/s, which decides how fast prompts are read and
  only limits decode when the sums fall behind.
- **Price** is the rent for the machine in dollars an hour, which you can edit to match any
  provider’s price list.

Bandwidth and memory do different jobs, and they are worth keeping apart. Bandwidth sets how fast
one stream goes, while memory sets how many streams can share each read.

**Cost per million tokens** turns the rent into a price for the tokens written, by dividing the
price for an hour by the tokens written in that hour. The answer is then scaled up to a million
tokens, so that it is a number you can compare across machines.

Here is a worked example on `Lab default`, whose $1.00 an hour is a placeholder rather than a real
quote. With 8 users, the machine writes 347.3 tokens a second, and since an hour has 3,600 seconds,
that is about 1.25 million tokens an hour. If 1.25 million tokens cost $1.00, then a million tokens
cost $1.00 ÷ 1.25 = $0.80, which is what the lab shows.

To give that a size, suppose every reply were 500 tokens long, so that a million tokens would make
2,000 replies. At $0.80 per million tokens, each of those replies would cost four hundredths of a
cent.

With one user, the machine writes only 73.3 tokens a second, about 264,000 an hour, so a million
tokens costs $3.79 instead. The rent stays the same however many people share the machine, while the
tokens it writes go up with every user. That is why batching makes each token cheaper, right up
until memory is full and no more users can fit.

The **Busy share of each hour** slider covers the time a machine sits idle. A machine that writes
for only half of each hour still pays the full rent for half the tokens, so each token costs twice
as much. The cost counts only the tokens written, not the prompt tokens read.

### Three levers that change the budget

The card also has three tricks that real servers use, which the lab calls levers, and each one
changes a different part of the budget.

**Paged attention** fixes the worst-case reservation by handing out cache space in small pages of 16
tokens, as each conversation grows. Instead of a full 4,096-token space set aside for every
conversation up front, each one holds only what it has used, wasting less than one page.

In the book picture, this is like handing out single sheets of paper as they are needed, instead of
a thick, empty notebook for every new arrival. The **Average fill of the reserved context** slider
sets how full the average conversation is, and it starts at 50%. At that setting, each conversation
holds 2,048 tokens instead of 4,096, so its notes take half the room and half the time to read.

**KV-cache quantization** writes the notes in shorthand. The **KV cache type** control stores each
saved number in `fp16` at 16 bits, `q8_0` at 8.5 bits or `q4_0` at 4.5 bits. Fewer bits mean a
smaller cache to store and fewer bytes to read each step, at some cost in accuracy.

The 2B model’s running summary is a separate kind of memory, so the shorthand does not shorten it.
The app’s **engine**, the program that actually runs the model, keeps that summary in a 32-bit
format.

**Speculative decoding** adds a helper, a small and fast draft model that guesses the next few
tokens for the big model to check. The big model checks all of the guesses in one step instead of
writing them one at a time, keeping them up to the first one it turns down. After the kept guesses
it always adds one token of its own, so every check moves the reply forward by at least one token.

Checking can happen all at once, even though writing cannot, because the guessed tokens already
exist. The big model can push them through its layers together, the same way it reads a prompt. At
each spot, it works out the odds it gives every possible next token, and uses those odds to decide
whether to keep the guess there.

In the book picture, an assistant with a thin pocket version of the book scribbles four words ahead.
The reader then checks all four in a single flip through the big book, rather than flipping once for
every word.

A model picks each next token partly by chance, like rolling dice that are weighted toward some
words. A guess the big model finds likely is usually kept, and one it finds unlikely is usually
thrown out. The checking is arranged so that those odds stay exactly the same as if the big model
had written every token itself. So speculation changes the speed, not what the model is likely to
write.

Three sliders control the helper, and each starts at a set value:

- **Acceptance rate** sets how often a guess is kept, and starts at 0.80, meaning 8 guesses in 10.
- **Draft length** sets how many tokens the helper guesses each time, and starts at 4.
- **Draft cost** sets what one guess costs as a share of a big step, and starts at 0.10, a tenth.

At those values, the lab works out 3.36 tokens per check and a speed-up of 2.4 times. Going deeper
counts out where both of those numbers come from.

A lever only helps where the slow part is. Speculation speeds up decode while reading memory is the
limit, because one check reads the book once for several tokens. If the sums are already the limit,
the trick backfires. Each check works through five tokens per conversation, the four guesses plus
the big model’s own, which means five times the sums.

### Getting a model ready to serve: the export path

Packing a trained model into the right kind of file, so that a server can run it, is the job of the
export path. The **Export the model you trained** card walks through it with the 900-weight table
from earlier labs in this app. That table belongs to the tiny model that guesses the next character
of a text from 30 possible characters.

The same five steps apply to a real 8-billion-weight model, and only the byte counts change from one
to the other:

1. **Merge the adapter**: a model can be taught a new job after training by adding a small extra
   set of weights, called an adapter, as in the *LoRA & adapters* lab. Merging adds the adapter’s
   numbers into the main table, so only one table is left. Serving that one table costs exactly
   what the original model cost.
2. **Quantize to Q4_K_M**, or to whichever format you picked. This rounds the weights in blocks,
   using the block type of your format, the rounding recipe from the *Quantization* lab. For
   `Q4_K_M`, the block type is `Q4_K`, which uses 4.5 bits per weight.
3. **Write the GGUF file**: a **GGUF** file holds the rounded weights, notes on how they were
   rounded, and the **tokenizer**, the tool that splits text into tokens. Everything travels
   together in one file, behind a **header**, a short section at the start of the file that says
   what is inside. A loading program can check the header before it trusts the rest.
4. **Verify before running**: the server checks the header and the file’s **hash**, a short
   fingerprint worked out from every byte in the file. If even one byte has changed, the fingerprint
   no longer matches, so a damaged or swapped file is caught before it runs.
5. **Serve it**: the server starts with the context length you budgeted and a cap on how many
   conversations it accepts at once, no higher than **Streams that fit**.

The cap in the last step does important work. Without it, a rush of users would push the cache past
the memory limit and crash the server. With the cap, the extra users wait in a queue for a free
slot, which is slower for them but keeps everyone else running.

Two more parts of the job never show up as numbers in the lab. They are keeping a model working over
time and deciding where people’s prompts go. The end of Going deeper covers both of them, under the
heading “Operating it”.

## Why it’s here

The *Inference & KV cache* lab showed the machinery, meaning why a model saves its notes and why
writing an answer is slower than reading a prompt. This lab turns that machinery into a budget you
can blow, and then into a bill you have to pay.

In real life the question arrives all at once: this model, this many people, this much context and
this machine. Does it fit, how fast is it for each person, and what does a million tokens cost? Each
part is a simple division, but the parts are tangled together, because one setting moves several
answers at the same time.

Adding users lowers the cost per token, but it also slows each person down and fills up the desk.
Longer conversations fill the desk faster still, and they stretch the wait for the first token at
the same time. A bigger weight format keeps more detail in each weight, but it takes more room and
slows every single step. The lab puts all of these on one page, so that you can watch each knock-on
effect as it happens.

Picture a made-up school club that sets up the `8B` model on one 24 GiB machine to serve as a
homework helper for the whole school. The members test it themselves, one or two at a time. The
memory bar sits at about 6 GiB, so there seems to be room for a crowd.

On launch day, 40 classmates log on at once, each with a conversation that may reach 4,096 tokens.
Forty caches of half a GiB each come to 20 GiB. With the weights and working space added, the total
is 25.58 GiB, which is more than the machine has, so the server runs out of memory.

Nothing about the model changed between the test and the launch. Only the number of conversations
changed, and that was the one cost the club never measured. A cap of 36 conversations would have
kept the helper running, with the last 4 classmates waiting in a short queue for a free slot.

This lab also finishes a story that the *Quantization* lab started, when it rounded weights and
weighed the very file this app downloads. Here, the export checklist works out the file size of the
900-weight table, using the block type of your chosen format. The `2B · this app` preset is that
same downloaded file, now being served.

## How to play with it

The lab starts at `8B`, `Q4_K_M`, 8 users, 4,096 tokens and `Continuous batching`. At those
settings, the **Memory budget** card shows weights of 4.58 GiB and a cache of 4.00 GiB, and 36
streams fit. The steps below follow the lab’s own six steps in order, so you can check each one off
as you go.

### Add users

**Try this:** drag **Concurrent users** from 1 up to 64 and watch the **Throughput and latency**
card as the numbers change. With one user, **Total** and **Per user** both read 73.3 tok/s, because
that one person has the whole machine to themselves.

At 8 users, **Total** reads 347 tok/s while **Per user** reads 43.4, and **Time per output token**
reads 23 ms. Now keep going, and past 36 users both charts turn shaded, because the saved notes no
longer fit in 24 GiB. The shading is labelled `over 24.00 GiB beyond 36`, which marks the point
where memory runs out.

At 64 users, the **Memory budget** badge reads 37.58 GiB, which is more than one and a half times
what the machine has. The warning below the bar says you are over budget by 13.58 GiB, and that only
36 streams fit at this context.

Through the whole drag, watch which part of the bar moves, because only the cache grows while the
weights stay put at 4.58 GiB. The badge on the throughput card keeps saying `bandwidth bound`, since
even 64 users reach only 652 tok/s, far below the 7,472 the arithmetic could handle.

Then set the users back to 8 and switch **Scheduler** to `One at a time`, and watch **Total** fall
to 73.3 and **Per user** to 9.2. **Time per output token** rises to 109 ms, so every one of the 8
users is worse off than with batching. Switch back to `Continuous batching` before the next step.

### Extend the context

Set **Concurrent users** to 1, then drag **Context length** up to 32,768, and watch the cache
segment of the bar creep up close to the weights. One conversation’s notes now take 4.00 GiB, which
is the same as eight conversations took at 4,096 tokens.

**Streams that fit** drops from 36 to 4, because each conversation now reserves eight times the
room. **Time to first token** grows from 548 ms to 4,385 ms, more than four seconds, because the
lab’s prompt now fills a context eight times as long.

Here is a neat check on the speed rule: **Per user** now reads 43.4 tok/s, exactly what each of 8
users got at 4,096 tokens. Each step reads the same number of bytes in both cases, the weights plus
4.00 GiB of notes, so each step takes the same time.

Now set **Context length** back to 4,096, then switch **Weight format** to `F16`, and the weights
jump to 14.96 GiB. One user drops to 24.1 tok/s, even though nothing changed but how the weights are
stored. Every step now reads about three times as many bytes, so every step takes about three times
as long. **Streams that fit** also falls from 36 to 16, because the bigger book leaves less of the
desk for notes.

### Balance the budget

Switch **Weight format** back to `Q4_K_M` and pick `2B · this app` under **Model**, and **Per token,
per stream** drops to 12.0 KiB. A new readout, **Fixed state per stream**, appears and reads 19.27
MiB, which is the running summary each conversation carries. Each conversation needs 48 MiB of notes
plus that summary, so 331 fit where the `8B` model fits 36.

The note under the controls explains why, since only the cache-holding layers are charged per token:
12.0 KiB a token here, against 48.0 KiB if all 24 cached.

Now set yourself a puzzle: find a **Model**, **Weight format**, **Context length** and **Concurrent
users** that fit with room to spare, using **Streams that fit** as your cap. For example, say you
need 8 users working on long documents of 16,384 tokens each, all with the `8B` model.

At `Q4_K_M`, **Streams that fit** reads 9, so the 8 users fit with one slot to spare, and
**Headroom** reads 2.42 GiB. Switch to `F16` and **Streams that fit** drops to 4, so the same plan
is now over budget by 7.96 GiB. The more precise format would cost you half of your users, which is
the kind of trade this step asks you to weigh.

### Change the hardware

Go back to `8B` at `Q4_K_M` and 4,096 tokens, set **Concurrent users** to 1, and open **Hardware,
levers, and cost**. Under **Hardware**, pick `RTX 4090` and then `H100 SXM`, and compare the **Per
user** readout on each of them.

One stream gets 185 tok/s on the `RTX 4090` and 614 on the `H100 SXM`, which is 3.3 times faster.
That matches how much faster the H100 turns its pages, which is its bandwidth, and not the 6.0 times
by which its sums are faster. Decode spends its time waiting on memory, so all that extra arithmetic
barely helps a single stream.

The **Streams that fit** readout on this card tells the other half of the story, with 36 on the 4090
and 148 on the H100. Read it here, not on the **Memory budget** card, which always uses `Lab
default` and stays at 36 whatever machine you pick. The H100 preset has 80 GiB of memory to the
4090’s 24, so its desk holds about four times as many piles of notes.

Now set **Concurrent users** to 8 and read **Cost per million tokens**, which comes to $0.23 on the
4090 against $0.33 on the H100. At this load, the H100 writes 3.3 times as many tokens an hour, but
its rent is 4.7 times as high. So each of its tokens costs about 4.7 ÷ 3.3, or 1.4 times as much,
and the cheaper card wins on cost.

Then edit **Price** while the H100 is picked. The same chip is $3.49 an hour on one provider’s page
and $4.29 on another’s, so try $4.29. At $4.29, the cost per million tokens rises from $0.33 to
$0.41, and no preset stays pressed, because the machine is now your own description.

Finally, try `M4 Max laptop` and `CPU box` at 1 user, where the laptop gets 100 tok/s from its 546
GB/s. The CPU box gets 16.4 tok/s from its 89.6 GB/s. The presets set both prices to $0 because you
already own the machines, so **Cost per million tokens** reads `$0.000`. That leaves out what the
machine cost to buy and the electricity it uses, which you could enter yourself as an hourly
**Price**.

### Pull the levers

Pick `Lab default` again with 8 users, then switch on **Paged attention** and leave **Average fill
of the reserved context** at 50%. On this card, **Streams that fit** goes from 36 to 73 and **Per
user** from 43.4 to 56.6 tok/s, because each conversation now holds and reads half as many notes.
The levers work only on this card, so the **Memory budget** card above still reads 36.

Switch paging off and set **KV cache type** to `q8_0`, and now 69 streams fit while each user gets
55.6 tok/s. The cache costs about half as much per token, though not quite half, for a reason that
Going deeper explains.

Set the cache back to `fp16` and switch on **Speculative decoding**, and **Per user** jumps from
43.4 to 104 tok/s, which is 2.4 times faster. **Cost per million tokens** falls from $0.80 to $0.33,
while **Streams that fit** stays at 36. That is because the lab ignores the helper model’s own
memory. In a real server, the helper’s weights and notes would take some desk space too.

The bar list below the readouts keeps all of this in view. It has a row for `No levers`, one row for
each lever alone, and one for `Your selection`. Each bar’s length shows total tokens per second, and
its label shows the cost per million tokens for that row.

Two more experiments show where speculation stops helping, and the first is to drag **Acceptance
rate** down to 0, so that every guess is wrong. **Per user** falls to 31.0 tok/s, slower than with
no levers at all, because you are now paying for guesses that never get used.

Put **Acceptance rate** back to 0.80, then pick `CPU box`, whose arithmetic is slow at an assumed 1
TFLOP/s. At 8 users, the sums are already the limit on this machine rather than memory, so watch
**Total** as you turn **Speculative decoding** off and on. Speculation cuts **Total** from 62.3 to
38.8 tok/s here. Checking five tokens per conversation means five times the sums, and the sums were
already the slow part.

### Export it

Go back to `8B` and look at the **Export the model you trained** card, which follows your **Weight
format**. At `Q4_K_M`, the heading reads `900 weights → Q4_K_M`, and the second step rounds the
table into `Q4_K` blocks at 4.5 bits per weight. The badge shows the toy file at `507 B`, where B
here means bytes, not billion. That is because 900 × 4.5 ÷ 8 is 506.25, which rounds up to whole
bytes.

Now compare that 4.5 with the note under **Serving controls**, which gives 4.90 bits per weight for
a real `Q4_K_M` file. A real file keeps some of its weight tables in a bigger type, which costs
extra bits. The toy has only one table, so there is nothing to keep bigger.

Switch to `F16` and the badge grows to 1.8 KiB. The 900 weights at 2 bytes each make 1,800 bytes,
and 1,800 ÷ 1,024 is about 1.76 KiB, which the badge rounds to 1.8.

The last step, **Serve it**, shows the cap, such as `≤ 8 streams`, which is the smaller of your user
count and **Streams that fit**.

## What to notice

- The weights never move when you add users or lengthen conversations. Only the KV cache grows,
  which is how a model that loads with room to spare still runs out of memory under load.
- Batching splits one read of the weights among everyone, so **Total** goes up while **Per user**
  goes down, which is the trade between throughput and latency.
- With `One at a time`, 8 users share 73.3 tok/s and each waits 109 ms between tokens, so batching
  beats it for every one of them.
- On the first three cards the badge says `bandwidth bound` at every user count, since even 64 users
  reach only 652 tok/s against a ceiling of 7,472.
- The `8B` model saves 128 KiB per token and the `3B` model only 36 KiB. That is because the
  smaller model keeps only 2 saved sets per layer to the `8B` model’s 8.
- Hybrid attention shrinks the notes another way, since the 2B model’s cache is a quarter of what 24
  caching layers would cost and its summary never grows.
- Bandwidth sets one person’s speed, while memory sets how many people share each read. The H100 is
  3.3 times faster per stream and holds 148 conversations to the 4090’s 36.
- **Cost per million tokens** is the hourly rent divided by the tokens written in that hour. On the
  4090 it falls from $1.11 at 1 user to $0.14 at 36.
- A lever helps only where the slow part is. Speculation is 2.4 times faster while memory is the
  limit, but it cuts the `CPU box` from 62.3 to 38.8 tok/s.
- A price is an input, not a fact about the model. The same H100 SXM is $3.49 an hour on one
  provider’s page and $4.29 on another’s.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

Every number here is division from stated assumptions, with nothing timed on a real machine, and the
arithmetic assumes 100% of peak bandwidth and compute. Real hardware never reaches its peak, so
every speed in the lab is a best case rather than a forecast.

The working space is a flat 1 GiB that never grows, whatever the load. Every conversation also
reserves its full context from the start, unless you turn paging on. That reservation is the worst
case rather than the usual one, so real conversations usually hold less. The lab also leaves out the
arithmetic of the looking-back step itself, called attention, and counts only the arithmetic on the
weights.

**Time to first token** assumes a prompt that fills the whole context, and no queue at all. It
prices the longest prompt allowed but ignores any waiting in line. The first three models all use
one file-size rate per format, taken from real files of one 8-billion-weight model. The toy export uses only
its block type’s bits per weight, such as 4.5 for `Q4_K`, while a real file mixes in some bigger
types.

The hardware figures were read on 30 September 2026 and will go out of date, while prices change
faster still. Apple publishes no arithmetic figure for its laptop chip and the CPU box’s figure is a
round number, so the lab labels both of those as assumptions. The makers quote the 4090 at 24 GB and
the H100 at 80 GB, and the lab enters those numbers as GiB, which flatters both cards slightly.

The lab also prices only memory and speed. It never measures how much quality is lost when the
weights or the cache are stored in fewer bits. That loss is the third side of the trade, and the
*Quantization* lab measures it.

The levers are simplified too, because paging uses one average fill instead of a real spread of
short and long conversations. Speculation uses one acceptance rate for all text and a draft whose
cost is a fixed share of a big step, and the draft model’s memory is ignored. Finally, the cost
counts only the tokens written and leaves out the time spent reading prompts, so real costs per
token run higher.

### Where the real thing differs

Real serving programs reach only a fraction of the peak bandwidth the lab assumes, so real speeds
come out lower than the lab’s numbers. Test with real traffic before you trust any number from a
page like this one, however carefully it was worked out.

Real servers also do better than the lab in places, and **paged attention** is what makes the
average-fill idea real. The paper that introduced it measured earlier serving systems and found only
20.4% to 38.2% of their cache memory holding actual token notes. The rest of that memory sat
reserved but unused, which is exactly the waste that handing out small pages avoids.

Sometimes every conversation starts with the same long set of instructions, called a system prompt.
Prefix sharing stores that prompt only once, so it is nearly free after the first request. Chunked
prefill breaks one long prompt into pieces, so that reading it does not stall everyone else’s
writing, and the lab models neither trick.

Real traffic also comes in bursts rather than as a steady number of users. The number that matters
is usually the wait for the slowest few requests. That slowest wait is called a **tail latency**,
and an average can hide it completely.

Picture a made-up group chat where nearly every message arrives at once, but one in fifty takes ten
seconds to appear. The average wait looks fine, yet that slow message is the one everybody notices,
which is why real services watch their slowest requests as well as their average.

**A common mix-up:** “the model is 4.6 GiB, so it needs 4.6 GiB.” That is only the part that never
changes. The cache grows with every user and every token, and the cache is what runs out under load.
The same weights also read as 4.9 GB, because a GB is a slightly smaller unit than a GiB, so check
which unit a number uses before comparing.

## Going deeper

### Speculative decoding, counted out

To see where 3.36 tokens per check comes from, picture 100 checks with **Acceptance rate** at 0.80
and **Draft length** at 4. The first guess survives in about 80 of those checks, since each guess is
kept 80% of the time. The second guess counts only if the first was kept too, so it survives in
about 80% of those 80 checks, which is 64.

The third guess survives in about 51 checks and the fourth in about 41, because each one needs every
guess before it to be kept as well. On top of the kept guesses, the big model adds one token of its
own in every check. That token is either a correction or an extra one after four good guesses.

That adds 100 more, so the total is 100 + 80 + 64 + 51 + 41 = 336 tokens over 100 checks, or 3.36
per check. The note under the bar list shows this number for whatever settings you choose.

The guesses are not free, though, because each of the 4 draft steps costs a tenth of a big step. So
one check costs 1 + 0.4 = 1.4 big steps. Dividing 3.36 tokens by 1.4 steps gives 2.4 tokens per big
step, so speculation is 2.4 times faster than writing one token per step.

Drag the sliders on `Lab default` with 8 users and the trade shows up. A better guesser helps, so an
**Acceptance rate** of 0.95 lifts each user to 140 tok/s. A pricier guesser hurts instead, so at a
**Draft cost** of 0.50, each user gets 48.7 tok/s, barely faster than with no helper at all.

In real use, the acceptance rate depends on the text and on how closely the helper matches the big
model. The 2.4 also holds only while reading memory is the limit, since the extra sums for checking
five tokens per conversation are then hidden behind the wait for memory. The paper behind this
method treats those sums as free, but the lab counts them, and that is exactly what slows the `CPU
box` down.

### Why `q8_0` is not exactly half

The shorthand formats store the cache in blocks of 32 numbers. A `q8_0` block spends 8 bits on each
of them, plus a 16-bit scale shared by the whole block. That makes 34 bytes for 32 values, or 8.5
bits per value rather than 8.

A `q4_0` block uses 4 bits per number plus the same scale, which makes 18 bytes for 32 values, or
4.5 bits each. Against the 16 bits of `fp16`, the notes therefore take 0.53 or 0.28 as much room as
before.

That small extra is why `q8_0` fits 69 conversations at the starting settings, not the 73 that an
exact half would fit, the same as paging at 50%. The KV cache type never touches the 2B model’s
running summary, which the app’s engine keeps in a 32-bit format called float32 whatever you choose.

### More on the export path

A real export locks in more than the weights, because it also freezes the tokenizer and the chat
template, the fixed pattern that marks out each message. It freezes the licence too, which says how
the model may be used, so that every piece of a release is pinned down.

This app carries out the last two steps for real, with its own engine and model. Before running
either one, it verifies each one’s SHA-256 hash, a standard kind of fingerprint. It then starts
llama.cpp’s server program, `llama-server`, on a loopback address, 127.0.0.1. A loopback address can
be reached only by programs on the same computer, so nothing outside your machine can talk to it.

### Operating it

A served model is not finished once it fits and runs fast enough, because four more jobs keep it
working from day to day. They are monitoring for drift, rolling out new versions safely, guardrails,
and deciding where prompts go.

### Monitoring and drift

**Monitoring** means watching the same numbers this lab computes while the model is in use, along
with how often requests fail or get refused. Those numbers are time to first token, time per output
token, tokens per second, the length of the queue, and how full the cache is against **Streams that
fit**.

**Drift** is the quiet failure, when the requests or the answers change underneath you while no
alarm goes off. Either way, yesterday’s test results stop describing today’s use, and nothing tells
you so unless you look.

Picture a made-up homework helper set up in September, when a **Context length** of 4,096 tokens was
plenty for students’ questions. By exam season, students are pasting in whole essays, so the
helper’s owners raise the context to 16,384 tokens to make room. The machine and the model have not
changed, yet **Streams that fit** on the `8B` model falls from 36 to 9, a quarter as many students
at once.

The fix has two parts, one for each kind of drift. Drift in what people send, like the exam-season
essays, shows up in the monitored numbers: longer prompts, a fuller cache and a longer queue. Drift
in the answers is caught by rerunning a fixed set of test questions on a schedule and comparing the
results, the kind of replay the *Evaluation* lab covers.

### Versioned rollouts and rollback

A **versioned rollout** names each release by its exact pieces, which are the model file, the engine
that serves it, and the settings. This app pins one engine version and one model version, and it
checks the fingerprint of both before it runs them.

To roll out a new version, a service sends it a small share of the traffic first. It then compares
the monitored numbers and the fixed tests against the old version. It widens the share only if the
new version holds up, while the old one stays ready the whole time. Going back to the old one,
called a **rollback**, is then a quick switch rather than a slow rebuild from scratch.

This app runs one pinned pair, so changing either part means shipping a new version of the app. A
larger service would run several versions side by side.

### Guardrails

**Guardrails** are checks and limits built around the model rather than inside it. They include a
cap on conversations at **Streams that fit**, a limit on prompt length, and checks on what goes in
and what comes out. Some models can also use outside programs, called tools, such as a calculator
or a web search. A guardrail can list what each of those tools is allowed to do.

Guardrails are ordinary computer code, so they have bugs of their own and need their own tests. The
*Failure modes, safety, and security* lab covers what they miss.

### Privacy and on-device inference

Running the model on the user’s own machine is called **on-device inference**, and this app’s local
guide is an example of it. The app starts and stops the engine itself on a loopback address, using a
channel number, called a port, that it picks at random. The model file it runs is kept in the app’s
data folder.

Your prompts stay on your machine and nothing is billed per token, which are the two big gains of
running the model on your own computer. On a Mac, the app runs every layer on the computer’s
graphics chip, and on other computers it runs on the main processor. Those two ways of running are
why the lab includes the `M4 Max laptop` and `CPU box` presets, and the app uses a 4,096-token
context either way.

The price is a small model and your own machine’s memory speed, so the 2B model’s ceiling at 1 user
is 404 tok/s on the `M4 Max laptop`. On the `CPU box` it is 66 tok/s, and both figures are
arithmetic rather than measurement. If you choose a cloud provider instead, your text leaves your
machine, and that provider’s own terms apply to it.
