## The big picture

Training a model is only half of the work. Someone then has to run it on a real machine, so people can type questions and get answers back. Putting a trained model to work is called **deployment**, and answering people's requests with it is called **serving**.

This lesson covers three questions about serving a model. Does it fit in the machine's memory, how fast does it write, and what does each answer cost? You will work out all three with plain arithmetic. You will also see why the answers pull against each other.

A model writes its reply one small piece of text at a time. Each piece is called a **token**, and it is often a short word or part of a longer one. Almost every number in this lab is counted in tokens. That includes how long a chat is, how many come out each second, and what a million cost.

This lesson keeps coming back to one picture: the machine is a reader at a desk. The model is an enormous book of numbers lying open on that desk. To write each new token, the reader flips through the whole book once, first page to last. Each conversation also leaves its own growing pile of notes on the desk. The desk is only so big, and that matters a great deal.

### Where you have met it

If you have used a chatbot, a model like this was serving you. You may have noticed a short pause before the reply began. After the pause, the words arrived a few at a time. This lab predicts both the pause and the pace.

This app serves a model too, and in Settings it can download a small model called Qwen3.5 2B. It then runs that model on your computer as its local guide. The lab includes that exact model, so you can see why it fits so comfortably on an ordinary machine. If you pick a cloud provider in the app instead, your words travel to that company's computers. They get answered there.

The lab also includes real machines that people rent by the hour. One is the RTX 4090, a graphics card made for home computers. Another is the H100 SXM, a much bigger chip made for data centres. A data centre is a building full of computers that run online services. On the price pages the lab quotes, an hour costs $0.74 on the first and $3.49 on the second.

### Why it matters

A model can load with plenty of room to spare. It can still run out of memory once many people use it together. This surprises people because the part that grows is not the model. It is the notes the machine keeps for each conversation. Without a budget, the busiest moment of the day is exactly when the machine runs out of room.

Speed and money pull against each other too. One machine can answer many people at once, so it spreads its hourly rent over far more tokens. The catch is that each person waits a little longer for every token. Choosing where to sit on that trade is a big part of serving a model well.

Running a model for real is also a job that never quite ends. The questions people ask change over time, and new versions have to replace old ones safely. Someone also has to decide where people's words are allowed to go after they type them.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- Why the model's own numbers take memory once. The notes for each conversation, called the **KV cache**, take memory again for every person and token.
- Why serving many people together raises the total tokens written each second. Each person's own tokens arrive more slowly, though.
- What a trained model has to become, step by step, before a program can serve it.
- How swapping the machine changes how many chats fit, how fast they run and what they cost. Three tricks change those numbers too. One hands out memory in small pages (**paged attention**). One writes the notes in shorthand (**KV-cache quantization**). One lets a small helper guess ahead (**speculative decoding**).
- What running a model for real adds beyond speed. It means watching for changes, safe version swaps, rules around the model, and knowing where prompts go.

## What it is

This lab does arithmetic, not measurement. You state your assumptions, do the division, and find out which resource runs out first. The note under the throughput chart lists every assumption the first three cards use. You can disagree with any of them.

### A few words you need first

A model stores everything it learned during training as a huge set of numbers called **weights**. People also call them **parameters**, and the model names tell you roughly how many there are. The `8B` model has about 8 billion weights, and `1.5B` has about 1.5 billion. The `8B` model has exactly 8.03 billion, and the lab uses that precise count in all of its sums.

A model is a stack of stages called **layers**. Every token passes through all of them in order. The `8B` model has 32 layers. They matter here because the model keeps a separate set of notes in every layer.

Memory is measured in bytes. A **bit** is a single 0 or 1, and 8 bits make one **byte**. A byte is enough room for one letter of plain text. Bigger units each step up by 1,024. That number suits computers because it is ten 2s multiplied together.

- A **KiB**, or kibibyte, is 1,024 bytes, which is about as much as a paragraph of plain text.
- A **MiB**, or mebibyte, is 1,024 KiB, which is a little over a million bytes.
- A **GiB**, or gibibyte, is 1,024 MiB, which is a little over a billion bytes.

A **GB**, or gigabyte, is exactly a billion bytes. So the same amount of memory looks a little bigger in GB than in GiB. Times are mostly given in milliseconds, written **ms**, and 1 ms is a thousandth of a second. Speeds are given in tokens per second, which the lab writes as **tok/s**.

### Weights: memory you pay for once

Before a model can write anything, the machine has to load all of its weights into memory. How much room they take depends on how many bits each weight uses, which the **Weight format** control picks.

The `F16` format stores each weight in 16 bits, which is 2 bytes. The other formats, such as `Q4_K_M`, are rounded versions from the *Quantization* lab. They squeeze each weight into fewer bits, and they lose a little quality in the process. A weight like 0.73128 might be stored as the nearest allowed value, such as 0.75.

Rounded formats handle the weights in small groups called **blocks**. Each block stores a few extra bits that record how its weights were rounded. The recipe a format follows for its blocks is called its **block type**. Those shared bits count toward every weight in the block. So the bits per weight need not be a whole number.

The note on **Serving controls** gives `Q4_K_M` as 4.90 bits per weight for `8B`, averaged over the whole file.

To get the size of the weights, multiply the number of weights by the bits per weight. Then divide by 8 to turn bits into bytes. For `8B` at `Q4_K_M`, that is 8.03 billion × 4.90 ÷ 8, which comes to about 4.9 billion bytes. A GiB is a little more than a billion bytes, so the lab shows this as 4.58 GiB.

At `F16`, the same model needs 2 bytes for every weight. That comes to about 16 billion bytes, or 14.96 GiB. That is more than three times the room for exactly the same model. Only the way its numbers are written down has changed.

The machine loads the weights **once** and shares them. Whether one person or thirty people use the model, there is only one copy in memory. That is why the weights part of the **Memory budget** bar never moves. Adding users or longer chats does not change it.

### The KV cache: memory you pay for every conversation

To write each new token, a model looks back over every token that came before it. This looking-back step is called **attention**, and the *Attention* lab showed it in detail.

Each earlier token has two lists of numbers that the model worked out when it first read that token. One list is a **key**, which is a label that says what the token offers. The other is a **value**, which is the information the token hands over. The more attention a token gets, the more of its value the model uses.

Take the sentence "The dog chased the ball because it was bored." To make sense of "it", the model compares what it needs with the keys of the earlier words. "Dog" matches best. So the model borrows mostly from the value of "dog" and only a little from the other words.

Redoing those lists for every earlier token would waste a huge amount of work. The model would have to do it again for each new token. So the model saves every token's keys and values the moment it makes them. It does this in every layer and reads them back later. That store of saved keys and values is the **KV cache**. KV stands for keys and values.

The cache grows by one token's worth every time the model reads or writes a token. Each conversation also needs a cache of its own, because its words differ from everyone else's. So the cache is the part of memory that grows with more people and longer chats.

The lab calls one conversation being served a **stream**. That is why the **Concurrent users** slider reads in streams, such as `8 streams`.

### How big one token's notes are

The **Memory budget** card shows the cost of one token's notes as **Per token, per stream**. The card's formula multiplies a few numbers that the model's design fixes:

```text
2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × 2 bytes
```

Inside each layer, the looking-back is split into several lookups that run side by side. Each lookup reads from a set of saved keys and values, and several lookups may share one set. These saved sets are called **KV heads**. Each saved key or value is a list of numbers. The length of that list is the **head dim**, short for head dimension.

The first 2 in the formula is there because every token saves one key and one value. The last 2 is how many bytes each number takes in **fp16**. That is the 16-bit format the lab uses for the cache.

Now work it out for `8B`. It has 32 layers, 8 KV heads in each layer and a head dim of 128. One key and one value in one head make 2 × 128 = 256 numbers. Eight heads make that 2,048, and 32 layers make it 65,536 numbers for each token.

At 2 bytes per number, that is 131,072 bytes, which is exactly 128 KiB. That is room for about 131,000 letters of plain text, saved for every single token of every single chat.

The `3B` model has 36 layers but only 2 KV heads, so it pays 36 KiB per token. The `1.5B` model pays 28 KiB. That means the `8B` model pays 3.6 times as much as the `3B` model for each token. The KV heads cause the whole gap, and a little more. Keeping 8 saved sets per layer instead of 2 costs 4 times as much. The `3B` model's 4 extra layers pull that back down to 3.6.

A model can keep so few saved sets because several lookups share each one, a design called **grouped-query attention**. The `3B` model keeps only 2 sets per layer, and the `8B` model keeps 8.

### A whole conversation's notes

One token's notes are small, but a conversation holds thousands of tokens. The **Context length** slider sets how many, from 512 up to 32,768. The lab starts at 4,096 tokens. **Per stream at this context** multiplies that length by the cost of one token's notes.

For `8B` at 4,096 tokens, 128 KiB × 4,096 comes to 512 MiB. That is half a GiB for a single chat. With 8 users, the cache is 8 × 0.5 = 4.00 GiB. That is already close to the 4.58 GiB that the weights take.

The lab charges each chat for its full context from the start. It does this even if the chat has used only a few tokens. That is the worst case, as if every chat might run to its limit. The part on paged attention shows how real servers do better.

### The memory budget: will it fit?

The machine behind the first three cards is called `Lab default`, and it has 24 GiB of memory. Three things have to fit inside it. They are the weights, every stream's cache, and a flat 1 GiB for **activations + runtime**. That last part is working space for calculations in progress and for the serving program itself.

**Streams that fit** is how many full chats the leftover memory can hold. To find it, take the 24 GiB and subtract the weights and the 1 GiB of working space. Then divide what is left by one chat's cache.

At the start, 24 − 4.58 − 1 leaves 18.42 GiB, and dividing 18.42 by 0.5 gives 36.84. The lab rounds that down to 36. A 37th chat would only partly fit, and half a chat is no use to anyone.

**Headroom** is the memory still spare at the current load. With 8 users, the weights, cache and working space add up to 9.58 GiB, which leaves 14.42 GiB spare. The badge at the top of the card shows the same total as `9.58 GiB of 24.00 GiB`.

In the bar, thin lines split the cache into one slice per stream, so you can count the chats. A heavy line marks the 24 GiB limit. Anything that spills past it gets a dashed outline you can spot at a glance.

### The book and the desk

Here is a picture that holds the memory budget together. It will also explain where the speed limits come from. Think of the weights as a giant reference book of numbers. The machine's memory is a desk. The book must lie open on it the whole time the model is in use.

Each conversation keeps its own pile of notes on the desk, and that pile is its KV cache. Every token adds a little to the pile. A small corner of the desk stays clear as working space. The size of the whole desk decides how many piles fit beside the book.

To write one token for a chat, the reader flips through every page of the book. The reader also reads that chat's pile of notes. How fast the pages turn decides how quickly one person gets served. That page-turning speed is the idea behind the next part.

The picture stops matching in three places. First, nobody reads the book with understanding, because it is numbers fed into calculations. Second, a real model keeps a separate pile of notes for every layer. So picture the book as having one chapter per layer, and each chapter has its own notes. Third, reading a prompt works differently, as the part on the first token explains.

### Writing speed: why the chip waits on memory

A model handles a request in two stages. It reads your whole prompt first, which is called **prefill**. Then it writes its answer, which is called **decode**. During decode, every new token needs a full trip through all the layers. The machine reads every weight out of memory once for each token. The next token cannot start until the last one exists. Each token looks back at all the ones before it.

How fast memory can hand numbers to the chip that does the calculations is called **memory bandwidth**. It is measured in GB/s, which means billions of bytes per second. The `Lab default` machine reads 400 GB/s. That sounds enormous until you remember that the weights alone are almost 5 billion bytes.

Here is one decode step for a single user. It reads the weights, about 4.92 billion bytes, plus that user's half-GiB cache, about 0.54 billion bytes. That makes about 5.46 billion bytes to read for every single token the user gets.

At 400 billion bytes a second, reading those 5.46 billion bytes takes about 0.0136 seconds, or about 14 ms. One token every 14 ms works out to 73.3 tokens per second. That is the number the lab shows for one user.

Now compare that with the arithmetic. Each weight takes part in about two calculations for every token, one multiplication and one addition. One calculation like this on decimal numbers is called a **FLOP**. So each token of the `8B` model costs about 16 billion FLOPs.

The `Lab default` chip can do 120 trillion FLOPs a second, written 120 TFLOP/s. The arithmetic alone could therefore produce about 7,472 tokens a second. That is roughly 100 times what memory allows. The chip's calculators spend most of each step sitting idle, waiting for numbers to arrive.

Sometimes the time to read memory sets the speed, not the time to calculate. Then the task is called **memory bandwidth bound**. The **Throughput and latency** card has a badge that says `bandwidth bound` for this reason. In the book picture, writing is slow because of the page-turning, not because of the sums.

### Batching: one flip through the book for many conversations

If turning pages is the slow part, the fix is to share each flip among many chats at once. While the reader turns through the book once, they write the next token for every open chat. Grouping streams into one step like this is called batching.

In a real server, finished chats leave the group at every step while waiting ones join it. This keeps the group full, which is why it is called **continuous batching**. The lab's `Continuous batching` setting of the **Scheduler** control keeps it simpler. A fixed number of streams share every step, and nobody arrives or leaves. Every stream in the group shares one read of the weights.

There is a catch, though. Each step still has to read every chat's own notes, and each chat's cache is different. So the more chats share a step, the longer that step takes to finish.

Here is the step for 8 users. It reads the weights once, 4.58 GiB, plus eight caches of half a GiB each, or 4.00 GiB. Those 8.58 GiB are about 9.2 billion bytes. Reading them at 400 billion bytes a second takes about 23 ms. That one step produces one token for each of the 8 users.

So each user gets one token every 23 ms, which is 43.4 tokens per second. Together, the 8 users get 8 × 43.4 = 347 tokens a second, against 73.3 for one user alone. So the machine now writes almost five times as many tokens, though each person's tokens arrive more slowly.

Those two numbers have names. **Throughput** is the total tokens per second across everyone, and the card shows it as **Total**. **Latency** is how long one person waits. Here it is the gap between one person's tokens, shown as **Time per output token**. **Per user** shows each person's own tokens per second, which is one second divided by that gap.

Batching raises throughput and worsens latency. That trade gives the card its name, **Throughput and latency**, and its two charts show it side by side. The top chart, total tokens per second, climbs as you add users. The bottom chart, each user's own speed, sinks.

Each extra user adds the same amount to every step. That amount is the time to read one more pile of notes. Early on, that is small next to the shared weights. So a second user costs each person only a little speed, from 73.3 down to 66.8 tokens a second.

Later, the piles of notes become most of each read. The machine then spends most of each step reading notes. So each extra user adds less and less to **Total**. The 2nd user adds about 60 tokens a second to **Total**, but the 9th adds only about 22. By the 36th user, each newcomer adds only about 3. Their tokens come mostly out of everyone else's speed.

A bus gives a rough comparison. One bus carries many riders for the price of one trip, so each rider's share of the cost falls. But the bus makes more stops, so each rider's trip takes longer. The comparison stops matching because a batch has no route. Every chat gets one token per step and then waits for the next step.

### One at a time

The other **Scheduler** setting is `One at a time`. The reader serves a single chat per flip through the book. Each step still produces only one token. So the total stays at 73.3 tokens a second, however many people are waiting.

With 8 users, those 73.3 tokens are shared out at 9.2 each. Every user waits 109 ms between tokens while the others take their turns. Batching gives each of those same 8 users 43.4 tokens a second. So batching is better for everyone here, even though nobody gets the machine alone.

### The first token: reading the prompt

Prefill is different from decode, because the whole prompt is already there. The machine can push every prompt token through each layer together. It reads the weights once for the whole prompt, not once per token, which changes which limit matters. Thousands of tokens share one read, so the reading is quick. The arithmetic becomes the wall, because each prompt token still needs 16 billion FLOPs.

For a 4,096-token prompt, the `8B` model needs about 2 × 8.03 billion × 4,096 FLOPs, which is about 66 trillion. At 120 trillion a second, that takes 0.548 seconds, or 548 ms, about half a second.

That wait is the **time to first token**. It is how long you wait before the answer's first token appears. A real service also counts any time spent waiting in a queue, but the lab ignores queues. The lab also assumes a prompt that fills the whole context. So its **Time to first token** is the wait for the longest prompt allowed.

Here the book picture needs one addition. For a prompt, the reader turns through the book once and handles every prompt token on each page. Page-turning then costs little. The time spent on the sums, which the picture leaves out, becomes the limit instead.

### The model this app runs: hybrid attention

The app's own model, `2B · this app`, is Qwen3.5 2B. It saves its notes in a different way from the other three. It has 24 layers, but only every fourth one keeps a growing cache. Those 6 layers are called full-attention layers, because they look back over every earlier token.

Run the same formula for those 6 layers, which have 2 KV heads and a head dim of 256. That is 2 × 6 × 2 × 256 × 2 bytes, which gives 12,288 bytes, or 12 KiB per token. If all 24 layers kept a cache, it would cost 48 KiB per token. That is four times as much.

The other 18 layers are a different kind, called Gated DeltaNet layers. Instead of a pile of notes that grows, each one keeps a single running summary for each conversation. It rewrites that summary as every new token arrives. A summary that is carried forward and updated like this is called a **recurrent state**.

The summary never grows, however long the chat gets. For all 18 layers together it takes 19.27 MiB per chat. The lab shows that as **Fixed state per stream**. A model that mixes growing-cache layers with fixed-summary layers like this uses **hybrid attention**.

In the book picture, 6 of the 24 chapters keep a growing pile of notes. The other 18 keep one index card per chat. That card gets rubbed out and rewritten again and again, but it never gets any longer.

Here is the payoff. At 4,096 tokens, one chat on this model needs 12 KiB × 4,096 = 48 MiB of cache. It also carries its 19.27 MiB summary, so the total is about 67 MiB per chat. That is roughly an eighth of the 512 MiB the `8B` model needs at the same length.

So 331 chats fit in the same 24 GiB, where the `8B` model fits only 36. The 2B model's weights are small too. The file this app downloads is 1.19 GiB, which works out to 5.445 bits per weight.

### The machine, and what a million tokens cost

So far the machine has been `Lab default`, but any real machine comes down to the same few numbers. The **Hardware, levers, and cost** card lets you change each one, starting from a preset:

- **Memory** is the size of the desk, in GiB. It decides how many chats can fit beside the book.
- **Memory bandwidth** is how fast the pages turn, in GB/s. It decides how fast one chat is served.
- **Compute** is how fast the sums get done, in TFLOP/s. It decides how fast prompts are read, and it limits decode only when the sums fall behind.
- **Price** is the rent for the machine in dollars an hour. You can edit it to match any provider's price list.

Bandwidth and memory do different jobs, so keep them apart. Bandwidth sets how fast one stream goes, while memory sets how many streams can share each read.

**Cost per million tokens** turns the rent into a price for the tokens written. It divides the price for an hour by the tokens written in that hour. The answer is then scaled up to a million tokens, so you can compare it across machines.

Here is a worked example on `Lab default`. Its $1.00 an hour is a placeholder, not a real quote. With 8 users, the machine writes 347.3 tokens a second. An hour has 3,600 seconds, so that is about 1.25 million tokens an hour. If 1.25 million tokens cost $1.00, then a million tokens cost $1.00 ÷ 1.25 = $0.80. That is what the lab shows.

To size that up, suppose every reply were 500 tokens long, so a million tokens make 2,000 replies. At $0.80 per million tokens, each of those replies would cost four hundredths of a cent.

With one user, the machine writes only 73.3 tokens a second, about 264,000 an hour. A million tokens then costs $3.79 instead. The rent stays the same however many people share the machine. The tokens it writes go up with every user. That is why batching makes each token cheaper, until memory is full and no more users can fit.

The **Busy share of each hour** slider covers the time a machine sits idle. A machine that writes for only half of each hour still pays the full rent. It writes half the tokens, so each token costs twice as much. A taxi works the same way. A cab that sits empty half the day must still cover its costs with the rides it does give. The comparison stops matching because rides differ in length, and the lab treats every token alike. The cost counts only the tokens written, not the prompt tokens read.

### Three levers that change the budget

The card also has three tricks that real servers use. The lab calls them levers, and each one changes a different part of the budget.

**Paged attention** fixes the worst-case reservation. It hands out cache space in small pages of 16 tokens, as each chat grows. Without it, the machine sets aside a full 4,096-token space for every chat up front. With it, each chat holds only what it has used, and it wastes less than one page.

In the book picture, this is like handing out single sheets of paper as people need them. The other way is to give every new arrival a thick, empty notebook. The **Average fill of the reserved context** slider sets how full the average chat is, starting at 50%. At that setting, each chat holds 2,048 tokens instead of 4,096. Its notes take half the room and half the time to read.

**KV-cache quantization** writes the notes in shorthand. The **KV cache type** control picks how many bits each saved number gets. The choices are `fp16` at 16 bits, `q8_0` at 8.5 bits and `q4_0` at 4.5 bits. Fewer bits mean a smaller cache to store and fewer bytes to read each step. The cost is a little accuracy.

The 2B model's running summary is a separate kind of memory, so the shorthand does not shrink it. The app's **engine** is the program that actually runs the model. It keeps that summary in a 32-bit format.

**Speculative decoding** adds a helper. This is a small, fast draft model that guesses the next few tokens for the big model to check. The big model checks all of the guesses in one step instead of writing them one at a time. It keeps the guesses up to the first one it turns down. After the kept guesses, it always adds one token of its own. So every check moves the reply forward by at least one token.

The big model can check everything at once, but it cannot write that way. The guessed tokens already exist, so it can push them through its layers together. That is the same way it reads a prompt. At each spot, it works out the odds it gives every possible next token. It uses those odds to decide whether to keep the guess there.

In the book picture, an assistant with a thin pocket version of the book scribbles four words ahead. The reader then checks all four in one flip through the big book, not one flip per word.

A model picks each next token partly by chance, like rolling dice that are weighted toward some words. A guess the big model finds likely is usually kept, and one it finds unlikely is usually thrown out. The checking keeps those odds exactly the same as if the big model had written every token itself. So speculation changes the speed, not what the model is likely to write.

Three sliders control the helper, and each starts at a set value:

- **Acceptance rate** sets how often a guess is kept. It starts at 0.80, which means 8 guesses in 10.
- **Draft length** sets how many tokens the helper guesses each time, and it starts at 4.
- **Draft cost** sets what one guess costs as a share of a big step. It starts at 0.10, which is a tenth.

At those values, the lab works out 3.36 tokens per check and a speed-up of 2.4 times. The Going deeper section counts out where both of those numbers come from.

A lever helps only where the slow part is. Speculation speeds up decode while reading memory is the limit. One check reads the book once for several tokens. If the sums are already the limit, the trick backfires. Each check works through five tokens per chat, which are the four guesses plus the big model's own. That means five times the sums.

### Getting a model ready to serve: the export path

The export path packs a trained model into the right kind of file. A server can then run that file. The **Export the model you trained** card walks through it with the 900-weight table from earlier labs. That table belongs to a tiny model that guesses the next character of a text from 30 possible characters.

The same five steps apply to a real 8-billion-weight model. Only the byte counts change from one to the other.

1. **Merge the adapter:** An adapter is a small set of extra weights for a new job. The *LoRA & adapters* lab covered it. Merging adds the adapter's numbers into the main table, so only one table is left. Serving that one table costs exactly what the original model cost.
2. **Quantize to Q4_K_M:** You can pick another format instead. This step rounds the weights in blocks, using the block type of your format. That block type is the rounding recipe from the *Quantization* lab. For `Q4_K_M`, the block type is `Q4_K`, which uses 4.5 bits per weight.
3. **Write the GGUF file:** A **GGUF** file holds the rounded weights and notes on how they were rounded. It also holds the **tokenizer**, the tool that splits text into tokens. Everything travels together in one file, behind a **header**. The header is a short section at the start that says what is inside. A loading program can check it before it trusts the rest.
4. **Verify before running:** The server checks the header and the file's **hash**. A hash is a short fingerprint worked out from every byte in the file. If even one byte has changed, the fingerprint no longer matches. So the server catches a damaged or swapped file before it runs.
5. **Serve it:** The server starts with the context length you budgeted. It also sets a cap on how many chats it accepts at once, no higher than **Streams that fit**.

The cap in the last step does important work. Without it, a rush of users would push the cache past the memory limit and crash the server. With the cap, the extra users wait in a queue for a free slot. That is slower for them, but it keeps everyone else running.

Two more parts of the job never show up as numbers in the lab. They are keeping a model working over time and deciding where people's prompts go. The end of Going deeper covers both of them, under the heading "Operating it".

## Why it’s here

The *Inference & KV cache* lab showed the machinery. It explained why a model saves its notes and why writing an answer is slower than reading a prompt. This lab turns that machinery into a budget you can blow. Then it turns it into a bill you have to pay.

In real life the question arrives all at once. You have this model, this many people, this much context and this machine. Does it fit, how fast is it for each person, and what does a million tokens cost? Each part is a simple division, but the parts are tangled together. One setting moves several answers at the same time.

Adding users lowers the cost per token, but it also slows each person down and fills up the desk. Longer chats fill the desk faster still and also stretch the wait for the first token. A bigger weight format keeps more detail in each weight. But it takes more room and slows every step. The lab puts all of these on one page, so you can watch each knock-on effect as it happens.

Picture a made-up school club that sets up the `8B` model on one 24 GiB machine. It serves as a homework helper for the whole school. The members test it themselves, one or two at a time. The memory bar sits at about 6 GiB, so there seems to be room for a crowd.

On launch day, 40 classmates log on at once, each with a chat that may reach 4,096 tokens. Forty caches of half a GiB each come to 20 GiB. Add the weights and the working space, and the total is 25.58 GiB. That is more than the machine has, so the server runs out of memory.

Nothing about the model changed between the test and the launch. Only the number of chats changed, and that was the one cost the club never measured. A cap of 36 chats would have kept the helper running. The last 4 classmates would have waited in a short queue for a free slot.

This lab also finishes a story that the *Quantization* lab started. That lab rounded weights and weighed the very file this app downloads. Here, the export checklist works out the file size of the 900-weight table. It uses the block type of your chosen format. The `2B · this app` preset is that same downloaded file, now being served.

## How to play with it

The lab starts at `8B`, `Q4_K_M`, 8 users, 4,096 tokens and `Continuous batching`. At those settings, the **Memory budget** card shows weights of 4.58 GiB and a cache of 4.00 GiB. It also shows that 36 streams fit. The steps below follow the lab's own six steps in order. You can check each one off as you go.

### Add users

**Try this:** Drag **Concurrent users** from 1 up to 64. Watch the **Throughput and latency** card as the numbers change. With one user, **Total** and **Per user** both read 73.3 tok/s. That one person has the whole machine.

At 8 users, **Total** reads 347 tok/s, **Per user** reads 43.4 and **Time per output token** reads 23 ms. Now keep going. Past 36 users, both charts turn shaded, because the saved notes no longer fit in 24 GiB. The shading is labelled `over 24.00 GiB beyond 36`, which marks the point where memory runs out.

At 64 users, the **Memory budget** badge reads 37.58 GiB. That is more than one and a half times what the machine has. The warning below the bar says you are over budget by 13.58 GiB. It adds that only 36 streams fit at this context.

Through the whole drag, watch which part of the bar moves. Only the cache grows, and the weights stay put at 4.58 GiB. The badge on the throughput card keeps saying `bandwidth bound`. Even 64 users reach only 652 tok/s, far below the 7,472 the arithmetic could handle.

Then set the users back to 8 and switch **Scheduler** to `One at a time`. **Total** falls to 73.3 and **Per user** falls to 9.2. **Time per output token** rises to 109 ms. Every one of the 8 users is worse off than with batching. Switch back to `Continuous batching` before the next step.

### Extend the context

Set **Concurrent users** to 1, then drag **Context length** up to 32,768. Watch the cache segment of the bar creep up close to the weights. One chat's notes now take 4.00 GiB, which is the same as eight chats took at 4,096 tokens.

**Streams that fit** drops from 36 to 4, because each chat now reserves eight times the room. **Time to first token** grows from 548 ms to 4,385 ms, which is more than four seconds. The lab's prompt now fills a context eight times as long.

Here is a neat check on the speed rule. **Per user** now reads 43.4 tok/s, exactly what each of 8 users got at 4,096 tokens. Each step reads the same number of bytes in both cases. That is the weights plus 4.00 GiB of notes, so each step takes the same time.

Now set **Context length** back to 4,096, then switch **Weight format** to `F16`. The weights jump to 14.96 GiB, and one user drops to 24.1 tok/s. Nothing changed except how the weights are stored. Every step now reads about three times as many bytes, so every step takes about three times as long. **Streams that fit** also falls from 36 to 16. The bigger book leaves less of the desk for notes.

### Balance the budget

Switch **Weight format** back to `Q4_K_M` and pick `2B · this app` under **Model**. **Per token, per stream** drops to 12.0 KiB. A new readout, **Fixed state per stream**, appears and reads 19.27 MiB. That is the running summary each chat carries. Each chat needs 48 MiB of notes plus that summary, so 331 fit where the `8B` model fits 36.

The note under the controls explains why: only the cache-holding layers are charged per token. That gives 12.0 KiB a token here, against 48.0 KiB if all 24 layers kept a cache.

Now set yourself a puzzle. Find a **Model**, **Weight format**, **Context length** and **Concurrent users** that fit with room to spare. Use **Streams that fit** as your cap. For example, say 8 users work on long documents of 16,384 tokens each, all with the `8B` model.

At `Q4_K_M`, **Streams that fit** reads 9, so the 8 users fit with one slot to spare. **Headroom** reads 2.42 GiB. Switch to `F16` and **Streams that fit** drops to 4. The same plan is now over budget by 7.96 GiB. The more precise format would cost you half of your users. That is the kind of trade this step asks you to weigh.

### Change the hardware

Go back to `8B` at `Q4_K_M` and 4,096 tokens, and set **Concurrent users** to 1. Open **Hardware, levers, and cost**. Under **Hardware**, pick `RTX 4090` and then `H100 SXM`, and compare the **Per user** readout on each of them.

One stream gets 185 tok/s on the `RTX 4090` and 614 on the `H100 SXM`, or 3.3 times faster. That matches how much faster the H100 turns its pages, which is its bandwidth. It does not match the 6.0 times by which its sums are faster. Decode spends its time waiting on memory, so all that extra arithmetic barely helps a single stream.

The **Streams that fit** readout on this card tells the other half of the story. It shows 36 on the 4090 and 148 on the H100. Read it here, not on the **Memory budget** card. That card always uses `Lab default`, so it stays at 36 whatever machine you pick. The H100 preset has 80 GiB of memory to the 4090's 24. Its desk holds about four times as many piles of notes.

Now set **Concurrent users** to 8 and read **Cost per million tokens**. It comes to $0.23 on the 4090 against $0.33 on the H100. At this load, the H100 writes 3.3 times as many tokens an hour. But its rent is 4.7 times as high. So each of its tokens costs about 4.7 ÷ 3.3, or 1.4 times as much. The cheaper card wins on cost.

Then edit **Price** while the H100 is picked. The same chip is $3.49 an hour on one provider's page and $4.29 on another's, so try $4.29. The cost per million tokens rises from $0.33 to $0.41. No preset stays pressed, because the machine is now your own description.

Finally, try `M4 Max laptop` and `CPU box` at 1 user. The laptop gets 100 tok/s from its 546 GB/s. The CPU box gets 16.4 tok/s from its 89.6 GB/s. The presets set both prices to $0 because you already own the machines. So **Cost per million tokens** reads `$0.000`. That leaves out what the machine cost to buy and the electricity it uses. You could enter those as an hourly **Price** yourself.

### Pull the levers

Pick `Lab default` again with 8 users. Switch on **Paged attention** and leave **Average fill of the reserved context** at 50%. On this card, **Streams that fit** goes from 36 to 73. **Per user** goes from 43.4 to 56.6 tok/s. Each chat now holds and reads half as many notes. The levers work only on this card, so the **Memory budget** card above still reads 36.

Switch paging off and set **KV cache type** to `q8_0`. Now 69 streams fit, and each user gets 55.6 tok/s. The cache costs about half as much per token, though not quite half, for a reason Going deeper explains.

Set the cache back to `fp16` and switch on **Speculative decoding**. **Per user** jumps from 43.4 to 104 tok/s, which is 2.4 times faster. **Cost per million tokens** falls from $0.80 to $0.33, while **Streams that fit** stays at 36. That is because the lab ignores the helper model's own memory. In a real server, the helper's weights and notes would take some desk space too.

The bar list below the readouts keeps all of this in view. It has a row for `No levers`, one row for each lever alone, and one for `Your selection`. Each bar's length shows total tokens per second. Its label shows the cost per million tokens for that row.

Two more experiments show where speculation stops helping. First, drag **Acceptance rate** down to 0, so that every guess is wrong. **Per user** falls to 31.0 tok/s. That is slower than with no levers at all, because you are paying for guesses that never get used.

Put **Acceptance rate** back to 0.80, then pick `CPU box`. Its arithmetic is slow, at an assumed 1 TFLOP/s. At 8 users, the sums are already the limit on this machine, not memory. Watch **Total** as you turn **Speculative decoding** off and on. Speculation cuts **Total** from 62.3 to 38.8 tok/s here. Checking five tokens per chat means five times the sums, and the sums were already the slow part.

### Export it

Go back to `8B` and look at the **Export the model you trained** card, which follows your **Weight format**. At `Q4_K_M`, the heading reads `900 weights → Q4_K_M`. The second step rounds the table into `Q4_K` blocks at 4.5 bits per weight. The badge shows the toy file at `507 B`, where B here means bytes, not billion. The sum is 900 × 4.5 ÷ 8, which is 506.25, and that rounds up to whole bytes.

Now compare that 4.5 with the note under **Serving controls**. The note gives 4.90 bits per weight for a real `Q4_K_M` file. A real file keeps some of its weight tables in a bigger type, and that costs extra bits. The toy has only one table, so there is nothing to keep bigger.

Switch to `F16` and the badge grows to 1.8 KiB. The 900 weights at 2 bytes each make 1,800 bytes. Then 1,800 ÷ 1,024 is about 1.76 KiB, which the badge rounds to 1.8.

The last step, **Serve it**, shows the cap, such as `≤ 8 streams`. The cap is the smaller of your user count and **Streams that fit**.

## What to notice

- The weights never move when you add users or lengthen chats. Only the KV cache grows. That is how a model that loads with room to spare still runs out of memory under load.
- Batching splits one read of the weights among everyone, so **Total** goes up while **Per user** goes down. That is the trade between throughput and latency.
- With `One at a time`, 8 users share 73.3 tok/s and each waits 109 ms between tokens. Batching beats it for every one of them.
- On the first three cards, the badge says `bandwidth bound` at every user count. Even 64 users reach only 652 tok/s against a ceiling of 7,472.
- The `8B` model saves 128 KiB per token and the `3B` model only 36 KiB. The smaller model keeps only 2 saved sets per layer to the `8B` model's 8.
- Hybrid attention shrinks the notes another way. The 2B model's cache is a quarter of what 24 caching layers would cost, and its summary never grows.
- Bandwidth sets one person's speed, while memory sets how many people share each read. The H100 is 3.3 times faster per stream and holds 148 chats to the 4090's 36.
- **Cost per million tokens** is the hourly rent divided by the tokens written in that hour. On the 4090 it falls from $1.11 at 1 user to $0.14 at 36.
- A lever helps only where the slow part is. Speculation is 2.4 times faster while memory is the limit. But it cuts the `CPU box` from 62.3 to 38.8 tok/s.
- A price is an input, not a fact about the model. The same H100 SXM is $3.49 an hour on one provider's page and $4.29 on another's.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

Every number here is division from stated assumptions, and nothing is timed on a real machine. The arithmetic assumes 100% of peak bandwidth and compute, but real hardware never reaches its peak. So every speed in the lab is a best case, not a forecast.

The working space is a flat 1 GiB that never grows, whatever the load. Every chat also reserves its full context from the start, unless you turn paging on. That reservation is the worst case, so real chats usually hold less. The lab also leaves out the arithmetic of the looking-back step itself, called attention. It counts only the arithmetic on the weights.

**Time to first token** assumes a prompt that fills the whole context, and no queue at all. It prices the longest prompt allowed but ignores any waiting in line. The first three models all use one file-size rate per format, taken from real files of one 8-billion-weight model. The toy export uses only its block type's bits per weight, such as 4.5 for `Q4_K`. A real file mixes in some bigger types.

The hardware figures were read on 30 September 2026, so they will go out of date. Prices change even faster. Apple publishes no arithmetic figure for its laptop chip, and the CPU box's figure is a round number. The lab labels both of those as assumptions. The makers quote the 4090 at 24 GB and the H100 at 80 GB. The lab enters those numbers as GiB, which flatters both cards slightly.

The lab also prices only memory and speed. It never measures how much quality is lost when the weights or the cache are stored in fewer bits. That loss is the third side of the trade, and the *Quantization* lab measures it.

The levers are simplified too. Paging uses one average fill instead of a real spread of short and long chats. Speculation uses one acceptance rate for all text. It treats the draft's cost as a fixed share of a big step. The lab ignores the draft model's memory, and its cost counts only the tokens written. It leaves out the time spent reading prompts, so real costs per token run higher.

### Where the real thing differs

Real serving programs reach only a fraction of the peak bandwidth the lab assumes. So real speeds come out lower than the lab's numbers. Test with real traffic before you trust any number from a page like this one.

Real servers also do better than the lab in places. **Paged attention** is what makes the average-fill idea real. The paper that introduced it measured earlier serving systems. It found that only 20.4% to 38.2% of their cache memory held actual token notes. The rest sat reserved but unused, which is exactly the waste that small pages avoid.

Sometimes every chat starts with the same long set of instructions, called a system prompt. Prefix sharing stores that prompt only once, so it is nearly free after the first request. Chunked prefill breaks one long prompt into pieces, so reading it does not stall everyone else's writing. The lab models neither trick.

Real traffic also comes in bursts, not as a steady number of users. The number that matters is usually the wait for the slowest few requests. That slowest wait is called **tail latency**, and an average can hide it completely.

Picture a made-up group chat where nearly every message arrives at once. But one in fifty takes ten seconds to appear. The average wait looks fine, yet that slow message is the one everybody notices. That is why real services watch their slowest requests as well as their average.

**A common mix-up:** "The model is 4.6 GiB, so it needs 4.6 GiB." That is only the part that never changes. The cache grows with every user and every token, and the cache is what runs out under load. The same weights also read as 4.9 GB, because a GB is a slightly smaller unit than a GiB. So check which unit a number uses before you compare.

## Going deeper

### Speculative decoding, counted out

To see where 3.36 tokens per check comes from, picture 100 checks. Set **Acceptance rate** at 0.80 and **Draft length** at 4. The first guess survives in about 80 of those checks, since each guess is kept 80% of the time. The second guess counts only if the first was kept too. It survives in about 80% of those 80 checks, which is 64.

The third guess survives in about 51 checks and the fourth in about 41. Each one needs every guess before it to be kept as well. On top of the kept guesses, the big model adds one token of its own in every check. That token is either a correction or an extra one after four good guesses.

That adds 100 more. The total is 100 + 80 + 64 + 51 + 41 = 336 tokens over 100 checks, or 3.36 per check. The note under the bar list shows this number for whatever settings you choose.

The guesses are not free, though. Each of the 4 draft steps costs a tenth of a big step. So one check costs 1 + 0.4 = 1.4 big steps. Divide 3.36 tokens by 1.4 steps and you get 2.4 tokens per big step. So speculation is 2.4 times faster than writing one token per step.

Drag the sliders on `Lab default` with 8 users and the trade shows up. A better guesser helps, so an **Acceptance rate** of 0.95 lifts each user to 140 tok/s. A pricier guesser hurts instead. At a **Draft cost** of 0.50, each user gets 48.7 tok/s. That is barely faster than with no helper at all.

In real use, the acceptance rate depends on the text and on the helper's match to the big model. The 2.4 also holds only while reading memory is the limit. Then the extra sums for checking five tokens per chat hide behind the wait for memory. The paper behind this method treats those sums as free, but the lab counts them. That is exactly what slows the `CPU box` down.

### Why `q8_0` is not exactly half

The shorthand formats store the cache in blocks of 32 numbers. A `q8_0` block spends 8 bits on each of them, plus a 16-bit scale shared by the whole block. That makes 34 bytes for 32 values, or 8.5 bits per value and not 8.

A `q4_0` block uses 4 bits per number plus the same scale. That makes 18 bytes for 32 values, or 4.5 bits each. Against the 16 bits of `fp16`, the notes take 0.53 or 0.28 as much room as before.

That small extra is why `q8_0` fits 69 chats at the starting settings. An exact half would fit 73, the same as paging at 50%. The KV cache type never touches the 2B model's running summary. The app's engine keeps that summary in a 32-bit format called float32, whatever you choose.

### More on the export path

A real export locks in more than the weights. It also freezes the tokenizer and the chat template, which is the fixed pattern that marks out each message. It freezes the licence too, which says how the model may be used. Every piece of a release is pinned down this way.

This app carries out the last two steps for real, with its own engine and model. Before it runs either one, it verifies that file's SHA-256 hash, which is a standard kind of fingerprint. It then starts llama.cpp's server program, `llama-server`, on a loopback address, 127.0.0.1. Only programs on the same computer can reach a loopback address. So nothing outside your machine can talk to it.

### Operating it

A served model is not finished once it fits and runs fast enough. Four more jobs keep it working from day to day. They are monitoring for drift, rolling out new versions safely, guardrails, and deciding where prompts go.

### Monitoring and drift

**Monitoring** means watching the same numbers this lab computes while the model is in use. It also means watching how often requests fail or get refused. The numbers are time to first token, time per output token and tokens per second. They also include the length of the queue and how full the cache is against **Streams that fit**.

**Drift** is the quiet failure. The requests or the answers change underneath you, and no alarm goes off. Either way, yesterday's test results stop describing today's use, and nothing tells you so unless you look.

Picture a made-up homework helper set up in September. A **Context length** of 4,096 tokens was plenty for students' questions then. By exam season, students are pasting in whole essays. So the helper's owners raise the context to 16,384 tokens to make room. The machine and the model have not changed. Yet **Streams that fit** on the `8B` model falls from 36 to 9. That is a quarter as many students at once.

The fix has two parts, one for each kind of drift. Drift in what people send, like the exam-season essays, shows up in the monitored numbers. You would see longer prompts, a fuller cache and a longer queue. You catch drift in the answers by rerunning a fixed set of test questions on a schedule. Then you compare the results, a kind of replay that the *Evaluation* lab covers.

### Versioned rollouts and rollback

A **versioned rollout** names each release by its exact pieces. These are the model file, the engine that serves it, and the settings. This app pins one engine version and one model version. It checks the fingerprint of both before it runs them.

To roll out a new version, a service sends it a small share of the traffic first. It then compares the monitored numbers and the fixed tests against the old version. It widens the share only if the new version holds up. The old one stays ready the whole time. Going back to the old one is called a **rollback**. It is a quick switch and not a slow rebuild from scratch.

This app runs one pinned pair, so changing either part means shipping a new version of the app. A larger service would run several versions side by side.

### Guardrails

**Guardrails** are checks and limits built around the model, not inside it. They include a cap on chats at **Streams that fit** and a limit on prompt length. They also check what goes in and what comes out. Some models can also use outside programs, called tools, such as a calculator or a web search. A guardrail can list what each of those tools is allowed to do.

Guardrails are ordinary computer code, so they have bugs of their own and need their own tests. The *Failure modes, safety, and security* lab covers what they miss.

### Privacy and on-device inference

Running the model on the user's own machine is called **on-device inference**. This app's local guide is an example. The app starts and stops the engine itself on a loopback address. It uses a channel number, called a port, that it picks at random. The model file it runs is kept in the app's data folder.

Your prompts stay on your machine, and nothing is billed per token. Those are the two big gains of running the model on your own computer. On a Mac, the app runs every layer on the computer's graphics chip. On other computers, it runs on the main processor. Those two ways of running are why the lab includes the `M4 Max laptop` and `CPU box` presets. The app uses a 4,096-token context either way.

The price is a small model and your own machine's memory speed. The 2B model's ceiling at 1 user is 404 tok/s on the `M4 Max laptop`. On the `CPU box` it is 66 tok/s, and both figures are arithmetic and not measurement. If you choose a cloud provider instead, your text leaves your machine. That provider's own terms apply to it.
