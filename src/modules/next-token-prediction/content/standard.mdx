## The big picture

This module is about the step a text-generating language model repeats: from the tokens so far, compute a probability for each possible next token, draw one, append it, and run again. It asks how a model that only ever predicts one step ahead can write a whole text, score one, and stop.

It builds on Tokens & embeddings, which turned text into token ids, and on Training dynamics, which separated training loss from validation loss. That split returns here as the rule for comparing models fairly, because a model can look certain about text it has already seen.

### Where it shows up

- Production models run this loop over vocabularies of tens of thousands of subword tokens, with contexts of thousands of tokens.
- Real systems, including Hugging Face Transformers and the Anthropic Messages API, end the loop at an end-of-sequence token, a maximum length or a stop sequence.
- Decoding settings such as temperature, top-p and Hugging Face's `repetition_penalty` reshape the distribution before each draw.
- Training minimizes cross-entropy, the average surprisal of each true next token, where surprisal is `−log p`, the negative log of the probability the model gave that token. Exponentiated, it gives perplexity, the effective number of equally likely choices per token.

### Why it matters

Because a text's probability factors into next-token conditionals, one trained next-token predictor is both a generator, by sampling and appending, and a scorer of any text. Without the loop, a one-step predictor could not write past its first token.

The surrounding pieces each solve one problem: decoding picks a concrete token, a stop rule ends a loop that has no ending of its own, and surprisal measures how expected each token was. Read loosely, they invite three mistakes: temperature taken for creativity, confidence for truth, and low loss on training text for generalization.

### What you will be able to explain

- How autoregressive generation appends one token at a time, and what ends it: an end-of-sequence token, a length limit or a stop sequence.
- How temperature, top-k, top-p and min-p reshape the model's odds without reordering them, and why that is not creativity.
- What surprisal and cross-entropy say about how expected each token was under the model, and why models are compared only on text they did not train on.
- How one prediction travels from characters to probabilities, and how a logit lens reads what each stage of the transformer would predict.

### What the lab shows

The lab runs three real character-level models, all built from Tiny Shakespeare over one 66-symbol vocabulary: a bigram table, a two-layer GRU (**RNN**) and a two-layer transformer. Every probability, surprisal bar and entropy is computed live; the **Held-out loss** and **Whole-corpus loss** rows were measured offline. Draws use a seeded hash, so the same settings reproduce the same text, and **Seen in training?** recognizes only the two preset lines.

In **Generation loop**, edit **Prompt**, then press **Sample next token** or **Sample 10 more** and watch each draw become context; with no end-of-sequence token, only the 120-character cap ends it. **Sampler controls** holds **Model**, **Temperature**, **Top-k**, **Top-p**, **Min-p** and **Seed**.

The key experiment: on the default prompt, ending in `thou J`, change both Juliets to Jason. The bigram, which sees only the `J`, does not move; the transformer, reading the earlier names, changes its top character.

## What it is

A language model computes one thing: a probability distribution over its whole vocabulary for the *next* token, given the tokens so far. Text appears because that single step is repeated.

The forward pass ends with a vector of **logits**, one unnormalized score per vocabulary entry. **Softmax** turns them into probabilities: `p_i = exp(z_i / T) / Σ exp(z_j / T)`. One token is drawn, appended to the context, and the calculation runs again on the longer sequence. That loop is what **autoregressive** means.

In this lab a token is one character, from a 66-symbol vocabulary, and the models are real: a bigram table, a two-layer GRU, and a two-layer transformer, all built from Tiny Shakespeare. The transformer trained for 8,000 steps and the GRU for 240, so the lab compares the three files as shipped, not the architectures.

### The factorization

A model that only predicts one step can still score a whole text, because the joint probability factors into a product of conditionals: `P(x₁…xₙ) = Π P(xᵢ | x₁…xᵢ₋₁)`.

Training maximizes that product on real text, which is the same as minimizing the average **surprisal**, `−log₂ p` of each true next token. That average is the **cross-entropy** loss.

### The whole model, end to end

Here is the path the transformer takes from characters to one sampled character, in the order the **Layer by layer** card draws it:

- **Tokens.** Each character becomes one of 66 ids.
- **Embeddings.** Each id looks up a 256-number vector, and a learned position vector is added. The sum starts the **residual stream**, the vector each position carries through the network.
- **Layers.** Two blocks, each with four attention heads and an MLP 768 wide. Each block adds its output to the stream.
- **Unembedding.** A final normalization, then a 256 × 66 matrix with no bias, turns the stream into one logit per character.
- **Softmax and sample.** The logits become probabilities, one character is drawn and appended, and everything runs again.

The model scores every position at once. Only the last position decides the next character.

### Reading the middle: the logit lens

The final normalization and unembedding run once, after layer 2. The **logit lens** runs them after the embeddings and after layer 1 as well, on the same last position, and shows what each stage would predict if the network stopped there.

On the default prompt the finished model's choice, `u`, ranks 56th after the embeddings and 3rd after layer 1, and first only after layer 2. Treat the readout as a view, not an explanation. Earlier layers were never trained to be read this way, so it shows what is linearly readable, not what a layer decided or caused.

### What decoding does

Decoding reshapes the model's distribution before the draw, in this order:

- **Temperature** divides the logits. `T` below 1 widens their gaps and sharpens the distribution; above 1 it compresses them toward uniform; near 0 it approaches **greedy** decoding, always taking the top character.
- **Top-k** keeps the `k` most likely tokens.
- **Top-p** keeps the smallest most-likely set whose probability reaches `p`.
- **Min-p** drops any survivor less likely than `p_min` times the top token (Nguyen et al., 2025). With `p_min = 0.10` the bar is a tenth of the top probability, so it rises when the model is confident and falls when it is not.
- The survivors are renormalized, and one is drawn by inverse CDF with a seeded number `u`.

None of these can reorder candidates or add one the model did not score.

### Where generation stops

The loop has no ending of its own. Real systems stop it in three ways:

- **End-of-sequence token.** A special vocabulary entry marking where a text ends. When the sampler draws it, generation stops. Hugging Face Transformers calls its id `eos_token_id`; the Anthropic Messages API reports a reply that finished naturally as the stop reason `end_turn`.
- **Maximum length.** The host stops after a fixed number of new tokens: `max_new_tokens` in Transformers, `max_tokens` in the Messages API.
- **Stop sequences.** Strings the caller chooses; generation stops once the output contains one: `stop_strings` in Transformers, `stop_sequences` in the Messages API.

Only the first is the model drawing a token that means "stop". The host checks the other two after every token.

This lab's 66 symbols include no end-of-sequence token, and it has no stop sequence. A newline is an ordinary character. So the lab keeps accepting characters until you stop or it reaches 120.

## Why it’s here

Everything a language model appears to do, answering, translating, summarizing, refusing, reasoning aloud, is this loop with different context. There is no separate mode for questions and no planning stage.

It is also why the loop can train on raw text. Nothing is hand-labelled: the label for each position is the character that comes next, so the text supplies its own training signal. That is self-supervised learning, introduced in What AI actually is.

It also gives precise language for a common complaint. A confidently wrong answer is a high-probability continuation that is not grounded in fact. Plausible and true come apart whenever the training text does not pin the answer down.

## How to play with it

### Context

The default prompt ends in `thou J`. It is a twin of a famous line with the name swapped, and it is not anywhere in Tiny Shakespeare: **Seen in training?** says so. With **Model** on Bigram the top next character is `U` (33%). The bigram sees only `J`, and 125 of the corpus's 320 capital Js start the speaker name JULIET. The RNN, reading the whole line, puts `o` first (26%). The transformer puts `u` first (99%).

Edit the start of **Prompt** and keep the final `J`: change both Juliets to Jason. The bigram bars do not move, and neither do the RNN's, because the GRU's memory of text more than about a dozen characters back has faded. The transformer's top character changes from `u` to `a` (87%): it is using the earlier names, not just the `J`. It is selective, though. A one-letter edit inside `wherefore` moves its odds by under two points.

### Seen and unseen

Press **Line from the training text**. `O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou R` appears once in Tiny Shakespeare, inside the part the transformer trained on, and **Seen in training?** now says yes. The transformer puts `o` first at 99.97%.

That near-certainty is weaker evidence than it looks. The transformer's mean surprisal on this line is 1.56 bits, against 1.82 on the default: a small gap. A line that is not in the corpus but repeats Romeo five times also gives `o`, at 99.8%, because the transformer copies from the prompt. So confidence on text a model trained on may be recall rather than prediction. Compare models on text they did not train on.

### Shaping

With the RNN, move **Temperature**: `o` goes from 26% at T = 1 to 32% at 0.80, 85% at 0.10, and 11% at 2.00, and entropy from 3.46 bits to 0.62 and 5.12. The order never changes. Even at 0.10 the RNN is not certain, because `a` is close behind; only **Top-k** 1 is greedy. Then set **Top-k** to 3 and **Top-p** to 0.50 and read the cut rows.

Now set **Temperature** to 1.00 and raise **Min-p** to 0.10. The RNN keeps 7 characters: the cut-off is 2.6%, a tenth of its top 26%. At Temperature 2.00 it keeps 19, because a flatter distribution leaves more characters above a tenth of the top one. Switch to Transformer and the same setting keeps one character at every temperature.

### The loop

Press **Sample next token**, then **Sample 10 more**. Each character is appended to the tape and changes the next distribution. Read **Surprisal −log2 p** and the draw log.

Then clear the continuation, set **Top-k** to 1, and sample with the RNN. It starts `ore the the the`, and keeps going. The transformer takes longer, then repeats `the said that we said`. The bigram settles on newlines. Nothing in the lab ends the text before 120 characters.

### Layer by layer

In **Layer by layer**, read the entropy of the three panels on the default prompt: 4.85, 4.52 and 0.09 bits. Nearly all of the drop is in layer 2, and that is where `u` first heads the list. Now change both Juliets to Jason: layer 2 puts `a` first.

## What to notice

- The outlined p bars are the model; the filled q bars are the sampler. Decoding only ever moves q.
- Low temperature is lower variance, not higher accuracy. If the top candidate is wrong, low temperature makes that error reliable.
- High temperature moves mass onto characters the model ranked lower, so the text gets more surprising to the model itself, and usually worse.
- Top-p and min-p adapt to confidence. At Top-p 0.50 the RNN keeps one character in the middle of `wherefo`, and between 4 and 11, about six on average, right after a space.
- Surprisal is highest where a character was unexpected. Across the corpus the GRU pays 4.4 bits for a word's first letter and about 3.0 for a letter that follows a letter; on the default prompt it pays 8.0, 10.4 and 12.0 bits for the three capital Js.
- On held-out text the transformer averages 2.23 bits per character, the GRU 3.04 and the bigram table 3.58. On the whole corpus they average 1.83, 3.06 and 3.54.
- The transformer's two figures differ because 90% of the corpus is its training text. The other two barely change.
- Before the layers, the stream holds only the last character and its position. Anything the model does with earlier words happens in the layers.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The models are tiny and character-level, trained on about 1.1 million characters, so continuations are Shakespeare-flavoured spelling rather than sense. Draws use a seeded hash, so the same state always reproduces the same text.

The loss ordering is transformer, GRU, bigram, and it is not a result about architectures. The transformer trained for 8,000 steps and the shipped GRU for 240, so the lab cannot say how much of the gap is wiring and how much is training. About 30% of each transformer batch was synthetic repeated strings, added for the Attention and circuits labs.

Only the transformer is strictly held out on the last 10%. The GRU trained on random windows of the whole corpus, and the bigram table was fitted to all of it. And **Seen in training?** knows only its two preset lines: the corpus is not shipped, so for your own text it says Unknown. A prompt missing from the corpus still has familiar parts: `wherefore art thou` is in it once, and Juliet fifty times.

The layer-by-layer card reads only three stages, at the last position, and the lab has no stop sequence, repetition penalty or beam search.

**In real systems.** Vocabularies hold tens of thousands of subword tokens, contexts run to thousands of tokens, and even at temperature 0 output is not perfectly reproducible across hardware, because floating-point reduction order can flip near-ties.

Two conceptual limits matter more. Nothing in the loop revisits an earlier token, so an unlucky draw becomes context and errors compound. And per-token probability is not a calibrated statement about truth; a model can be confident across an entirely fabricated sentence.

The logit lens has its own limit. It applies a readout trained for the last layer to states that were never trained to be read that way, so an early stage's distribution can look like noise.

## Going deeper

Other decoding tools restrict or rescore the candidates:

- **Min-p** is a recent adaptive cut: Nguyen et al. (ICLR 2025) define it as `p_scaled = p_base × p_max`, keeping every token at least that likely.
- **Beam search** keeps several partial sequences and scores them jointly. It suits translation and short constrained outputs, and tends to flat, repetitive text when open-ended.
- **Repetition penalties** lower the logits of tokens already in the context before the draw.

Penalties exist because greedy and low-temperature decoding loop. In the lab's greedy run the transformer gives its chosen character 83% on average over the first 22 characters and above 96% over the last 22, once the phrase repeats. The repeated text is the context that makes the repeat likely, so the loop feeds itself.

A penalty breaks it, at a cost: it also discourages repetition that is correct, such as a name that has to recur. Hugging Face's `repetition_penalty` is one: 1.0 means off, and above 1.0 penalizes tokens already seen, the prompt included.

Training differs from generation. During training the model is fed the true previous tokens, called **teacher forcing**, so it never practises recovering from its own mistakes. The surprisal bars on the tape are teacher-forced: one forward pass scores every character at once.

Cross-entropy exponentiated gives **perplexity**, the effective number of equally likely choices per token: `2^2.23 ≈ 4.7` characters for the transformer on held-out text, and `2^3.04 ≈ 8.2` for the GRU. It measures distribution quality and predicts usefulness only weakly, which is why the post-training lab exists.

The logit lens was introduced by nostalgebraist in 2020. Belrose et al. (2023) call their tuned lens a refinement of that technique, which they describe as often brittle. A check from outside the lab: switching off layer-2 heads 2 and 3 on the Jason prompt puts `u` back on top at 82%. That is evidence, not proof, that those heads supply the copied `a`.

The **Attention** and **Interpretability II: circuits** labs open this same transformer, and **Inference & KV cache** shows how production systems avoid recomputing the whole prefix at every step of this loop.
