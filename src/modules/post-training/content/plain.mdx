## The big picture

Type "Explain why the sky is blue." into a chatbot, and you expect a short answer about sunlight and
air. A model that has only had its first round of training might reply with more questions instead, such
as "Why is the sunset red?" and "Why is grass green?"

That model is not broken. Its first round of training, called **pretraining**, taught it to continue
text by guessing what comes next. Your line looks like a question from a homework sheet. On the web, a
question like that is usually followed by more questions from the same sheet, so more questions are a
good guess. A freshly pretrained model is great at continuing text but poor at helping you.
**Post-training** is the name for the stages that come next, and their job is to turn that text
continuer into an assistant that answers you.

### Where you have met it

Every modern AI chatbot built on a large language model goes through post-training. Much of what feels
like a chatbot's personality was set in these stages, not in pretraining. That includes how helpful it is, how much it hedges, what
it refuses to do, and whether it admits when it does not know something. It is also why two chatbots
built on the same pretrained model can feel completely different to talk to.

### Why it matters

Without post-training, a model would keep adding to your text instead of answering it, so there would be
no assistant to talk to. Post-training is also where one of the biggest problems in AI is clearest to
see. To train a model toward good answers, you need a number that a computer can push up, and that
number always stands in for what people really want. If you push hard on a stand-in, the model gets
better at the number rather than at the thing you meant. In this lab, you will watch that happen with a
set of starting clicks, and then fix it with one click of your own.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how the four stages differ: pretraining, copying good answers, training a scorer on people's picks,
  and tuning the model toward that scorer on a leash;
- how a scorer learns from your picks between pairs of answers, using a rule called Bradley-Terry, and
  how to check what it really learned;
- how to spot reward hacking, where a stand-in score keeps going up while the real quality of the
  answers goes down;
- what alignment means, which is the gap between what people can measure and what they really want, and
  which problems this training does not fix.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab shrinks the whole process down to one prompt, the sky question, and six possible answers labeled
A to F. With only six answers, you can see how likely the model is to give each one, and watch every
number change as you work. You will train the model on two good example answers, judge six pairs of
answers yourself, and watch a scorer learn from your clicks. The leash is a limit on how far the model
may move away from where copying left it. At the end, you will loosen that leash and watch the model
chase the scorer.

Next to all of this sits a separate grade called the gold score. The lesson's authors wrote it to stand
for what a careful human rater would give, and it plays no part in training. It is there so you can
tell whether the answers are really getting better or only scoring higher.

With the picks the lab starts with, the answers really do get a little better at first. Then, as the
leash loosens, they only score higher, because the model ends up favoring a long, flattering answer that
never explains anything. Working out why, and then fixing it with one click, is the heart of this
module.

Some parts of the lab are real and some are made up. The six answers, the model's starting chances and
the gold scores were written by the lesson's authors. So were the yes-or-no flags that say whether an
answer explains why the sky is blue. Each answer's word count, and whether it opens with praise, are
measured from its text instead. The copying stage and the scorer really learn,
step by step. The final tuned model is worked out with an exact formula instead of being trained, so
you see each result the moment you move a slider.

## What it is

### The model in this lab

A real language model writes an answer one small piece at a time, choosing each piece from a long list
of options. That is far too much to watch, so this lab shrinks the model to one prompt, "Explain why the
sky is blue.", and six whole answers it could give. The model is nothing more than six chances, one for
each answer, saying how likely it is to give that answer, and the six always add up to 100%.

Each whole answer counts as one choice, which is the setup that **Reinforcement learning foundations**
calls a bandit, after slot machines: you pick one option and get one score back. In that kind of
learning, whatever makes the choices is called the **policy**. That is why the last stage in the strip
across the top of the lab is labeled **Policy + KL**, where KL is the leash you will meet in Stage 3.

Here are the six answers, with the short tags the lab gives them:

- **A** (tagged "another exam question") lists three more exam questions, starting with "Why is the
  sunset red?", so it never answers the prompt at all.
- **B** (tagged "short, names scattering") explains in 25 words that air scatters blue light far more
  than it scatters red light.
- **C** (tagged "praise, then scattering") opens with "Great question!" and then gives the same
  explanation in more detail, using 52 words.
- **D** (tagged "short, wrong") claims in 9 words that the sky reflects the blue of the oceans, which is
  not the reason.
- **E** (tagged "praise, long, no mechanism") opens with "Great question!" and spends 59 words on how
  fascinating the sky is without ever saying why it is blue.
- **F** (tagged "medium, names scattering") explains the scattering in 39 words and also says why
  sunsets look red.

The real reason the sky is blue is that the gas molecules in air scatter blue light much more strongly
than red light. Blue light travels in shorter waves, and shorter waves get scattered far more. An answer
that explains this "names the mechanism", which means it gives the how-it-works reason rather than only
describing the result. B, C and F name it, while A, D and E do not.

Each answer also carries a **gold score** from 0 to 1, written by the lesson's authors to stand for the
grade a careful human rater would give. F gets 1 and B gets 0.95, while E, the long empty answer, gets
only 0.1. Gold is never used to train anything, because its only job is to grade how well the training
turned out. The one exception is a button you will meet later, **Label like the gold rater**. It copies
gold's rankings into your picks, to show what perfectly careful picks would teach the scorer.

### A picture to keep in mind

Picture a trainee cook who is learning to run a kitchen and starts by copying a mentor's plates, dish by
dish. Next, tasters try pairs of dishes and pick the better dish in each pair, and a judge studies all
those picks until it can score any dish on its own. Finally the cook adjusts their cooking toward
whatever the judge scores highly. Meanwhile the head chef keeps them on a short leash, so they cannot
stray too far from how they cooked right after copying the mentor. Behind all of it sits the diner,
whose real appetite is what the kitchen is meant to serve, even though nobody in it can measure that
appetite directly.

Each of those steps matches one stage of post-training, and the picture also shows where things can go
wrong. If the bigger dish happened to win every tasting, the judge learns to score big portions highly,
whether or not the food is any better. Keep that in mind, because the lab's scorer is about to learn
the same taste for big portions from the picks it is given.

### Stage 1: copy good answers

The first stage is the cook copying the mentor. The model trains on examples of a prompt followed by a
good answer, and each round of training nudges its chances toward those answers and away from everything
else. The examples are called **demonstrations**, and the stage is called **supervised fine-tuning**, or
**SFT**. It uses the same kind of training as pretraining, which makes the right next piece of text more
likely. What has changed is the text itself, which is now a set of hand-picked answers instead of
ordinary pages from the web.

In the lab, B and F are the demonstrations, and their cards are marked **SFT demo**. Before any SFT, the
pretrained model gives about 50% of its chance to A, the exam question, while B gets about 8%. Move
**SFT steps** from 0 to 8 and A's share falls to about 9%. Meanwhile B climbs to 38% and F to 36%, so
the two demonstrations now hold nearly three quarters of the chance between them.

Notice what SFT never does: it never looks at a score. It only knows which answers were demonstrated, so
it can make the model more like its examples, but it cannot say what makes one answer better than
another. Even so, SFT does most of the visible work of turning a continuer into an assistant.

### Stage 2: let people compare

Writing a perfect answer by hand is slow, while picking the better of two answers is fast and gives more
consistent results. So the second stage collects many picks and uses them to train a separate program, a
scorer that gives any answer a single number. The scorer is called the **reward model**, and the number
it gives an answer is that answer's reward, shown as **r** on each answer card.

In the cook picture the people making picks are the tasters and the reward model is the judge. In the
lab you are the taster, and you supply the picks on the **Preference pairs** card. That card shows six
pairs of answers, and each click records which answer in a pair you prefer. Each recorded pick is
called a label. So that you have something to start from, the lab comes with five of the six pairs
already labeled, and these are the starting labels.

### What the scorer can see

A real reward model reads every word of an answer, while the lab's much cruder scorer sees each answer
as only three numbers, called its **features**:

- **Words ÷ 10** is the answer's word count divided by 10, so E's 59 words become 5.9.
- **Names mechanism** is 1 if the answer explains the scattering and 0 if it does not.
- **Opens with praise** is 1 if the answer starts with "Great question!" and 0 if it does not.

The scorer gives each feature a weight, multiplies each feature by its weight, and adds up the three
results to get the reward. The **Reward model fit** card shows the three weights as bars, and a weight
above zero means that feature raises the reward. With the starting labels, the weight for Words ÷ 10
comes out at +1.255, the largest of the three by far. The Names mechanism weight comes out at about
+0.47, and the Opens with praise weight at about +0.14.

Here is the arithmetic for E, the long answer that never explains anything:

1. Its 59 words become 5.9, and 5.9 times the words weight of 1.255 is about 7.4.
2. E does not name the mechanism, so the weight for that feature adds nothing.
3. E does open with praise, so the praise weight of 0.142 adds a little more.
4. The total is about 7.5, which matches the r of 7.54 on E's card once you allow for rounding.

Now run the same steps for F, the answer with a perfect gold score:

1. Its 39 words become 3.9, and 3.9 times 1.255 is about 4.9.
2. F names the mechanism, so the mechanism weight adds about 0.47.
3. F does not open with praise, so the praise weight adds nothing.
4. The total is about 5.4, which matches the r of 5.36 on F's card.

So the scorer rates E, which explains nothing, well above F, which a careful rater gives full
marks, and nearly all of the difference comes from word count.

### Turning picks into chances: Bradley-Terry

The scorer still needs a rule that links its numbers to your picks. The usual rule is called
**Bradley-Terry**: the chance that one answer beats another depends only on the gap between their two
rewards. If the rewards are equal, each answer has a 50% chance of being preferred, and as the gap grows
the chance climbs toward 100% without ever quite reaching it.

The lab writes this rule as `P(A ≻ B) = σ(r_A − r_B)`, where A and B stand for any two answers, not only
answers A and B. In that line, P means "the chance that", the symbol ≻ means "is preferred to", and
`r_A` and `r_B` are the two rewards. The symbol σ, the Greek letter sigma, stands for a curve that turns
any gap into a chance between 0 and 1. A gap of 1 gives about 73%, and a gap of 3 gives about 95%.

Pair 1 shows the rule at work: B has a reward of 3.61, while D has only 1.13. That makes the gap between
them about 2.5, and the σ curve turns that gap into 0.92. That is the `P(B ≻ D) = 0.92` printed under
Pair 1, which means the scorer predicts about a 92% chance that you would pick B over D.

Because only the gap matters, adding the same number to every reward changes nothing: if every reward
went up by 10, every gap and every chance would stay the same.

### How the scorer learns

The scorer starts with all three weights at zero, so every pair looks like a coin flip. It then adjusts
the weights 200 times, and each adjustment makes the picks you actually made a little more likely under
the Bradley-Terry rule. The line chart on Reward model fit tracks a number called the **training loss**,
which measures how badly the scorer's chances fit your picks, so lower is better. It starts at 0.693,
which is the value this measure always gives when every pair is a 50-50 guess. The exact number comes
from a piece of math called a logarithm, which you do not need here. The loss then falls as the scorer
learns to put your winners on top.

### Stage 3: tune toward the scorer, on a leash

The last stage is the cook adjusting toward the judge. The model is tuned so that answers with higher
rewards get more of its chance, while a leash stops it from wandering too far from where SFT left it.

### Measuring how far the model moved

To build a leash, you first need a way to measure the distance between two sets of chances. **KL
divergence** is a number for how far one set of chances has moved from another. For each answer, it
looks at how many times bigger or smaller the new chance is than the old one. Then it averages those
comparisons, giving more weight to the answers that the new model picks more often.

KL divergence has three properties worth knowing: it is never negative, it is zero only when the two
sets of chances are identical, and it is not symmetric. Not symmetric means that measuring from the new
chances back to the old ones gives a different number from measuring the other way. That is why the lab
always names the direction, measuring the tuned model against the SFT model.

The lab reports this distance as **KL from SFT**, in units called **nats**. A nat is the unit KL comes
in, the way a meter is a unit of length. Its name comes from a piece of math called the natural
logarithm, which you do not need here. What matters is that 0 nats means the tuned model matches the SFT
model exactly, and bigger numbers mean it has moved further away.

Here is a feel for the size of these numbers. After SFT, the model gives E about 11% of its chance. If
tuning pushes E to nearly 100%, E's chance has grown about 9 times, the new chances look nothing like
the old ones, and KL reaches about 2.2 nats. That is the biggest reading you can reach with the starting
labels. At the other end, if tuning leaves every chance where SFT put it, KL is 0. In between, at a
β of 3.5, the chances shift a fair amount but nothing piles up on one answer, and KL is about 0.2 nats.
For example, A's chance there is cut in half, from about 24% to about 12%.

### The leash itself

The leash is a **KL penalty**, which charges the tuned model for every nat of distance from the SFT
model. A setting called β, the Greek letter beta, sets the price per nat. The lab's slider for it is
called **KL coefficient β**, where a coefficient is a number that multiplies something else. A large β
makes distance expensive, so the model stays close to SFT, while a small β makes distance cheap, so the
model can chase the scorer much further.

Put together, the tuning tries to make the average reward as high as possible after subtracting β times
the distance. The lab prints this target at the top of its last card as `maximize E[r] − β·KL(π‖π_SFT)`.
In that line, `E[r]` means the average reward, where E stands for "expected" rather than for answer E.
It is the reward the model would earn on average if it answered the sky question many times, picking
each answer as often as its chances say.
The letter π stands for the tuned model's chances rather than the number 3.14, `π_SFT` stands for the
chances after SFT, and `KL(π‖π_SFT)` is the distance between them. The double bar ‖ only separates
the two sets of chances being compared.

### The recipe the lab uses

This target has an exact solution, so the lab can calculate the tuned model directly instead of training
it. It takes each answer's chance after SFT and multiplies it by a boost that grows very fast as the
reward goes up. Then it rescales all six results so that they add up to 100% again. For answer E, the
formula card writes this as `π(E) ∝ π_SFT(E) · exp(r / β)`. The card always works through whichever
answer the tuned model currently favors, so if you change β and a different answer takes the lead, the
letter in the card changes too. In that line, ∝ means "is in proportion to",
and `exp(x)` means a special number called e, about 2.72, raised to the power x. When x is a whole
number such as 2, that means e times e. When x is not a whole number, a calculator handles it, and all
you need to know is that a bigger x gives a much bigger result.

At the default settings, where β is 2.0 and E is the model's favorite, the card works through E like
this:

1. E's chance after SFT is 0.108, or about 11%, and its reward is 7.54.
2. Dividing the reward by β gives 3.77, and `exp(3.77)` is about 43, so E's chance gets multiplied by
   about 43.
3. That makes E's boosted value about 0.108 × 43, which comes to about 4.7.
4. F starts with a bigger chance, about 0.20, but its lower reward earns a boost of only about 15, which
   gives F about 3.
5. Once all six answers are boosted, their values add up to about 11.9, and E's share of that total is
   4.7 ÷ 11.9, or about 39%.

That 39% is the result shown on the formula card. E, an answer that explains nothing, is now the model's
favorite, because the scorer's taste for length has been turned into chance.

Making β smaller makes every boost bigger, and halving β squares each boost. At β 1, E's boost jumps
from about 43 to about 1,900. F's boost grows too, but E's lead widens from about 3 times F's boost to
about 9 times. That is why sliding β down hands more and more of the chance to whichever answer the
scorer likes best.

### Where RLHF fits

Stages 2 and 3 together are called **RLHF**, short for reinforcement learning from human feedback.
Reinforcement learning means learning from a score that arrives after acting, like a game that awards
points after each move instead of telling you the right move in advance. In RLHF, people's comparisons
train the scorer, and the scorer's rewards then steer the model. **What AI actually is** introduced this
reward-after-acting kind of learning, and **Reinforcement learning foundations** runs the basic version
on a small bandit.

A real model cannot list every possible answer the way this lab lists six, so real RLHF cannot use the
exact recipe above. Instead, it has the model write answers, scores them with the reward model, and
adjusts the model a little at a time. Step by step, that moves the model toward the same target by trial
and error.

### Where the cook picture stops

The analogy stops at intent, because a cook knows they are trying to please a judge, while the model has
no idea it is pleasing anyone. Its chances shift toward higher numbers only because the math moves them
there. The judge is different too, since in real systems it is a program built from many people's picks.
As the lab shows, that program can end up rewarding things that nobody meant to reward.

## Why it’s here

### Copying is not enough

SFT can only copy, so it shows the model what a good answer looks like but cannot rank two decent
answers against each other. Writing perfect example answers by hand for every kind of question would
also take an enormous amount of work. Comparisons fill that gap, because judging is much faster than
writing. A scorer trained on comparisons can then rate answers that nobody has ever judged, which lets
training reach far beyond the examples people wrote.

### Why the leash is needed

The reward model is fitted to a limited set of picks, so it is only an approximation, and it has
mistakes. Pushing hard on it rewards those mistakes along with the things it gets right. Without a
leash, the tuned model would pile all of its chance onto whichever answer the scorer rates highest,
however that answer earned its score. The leash keeps the model near the SFT model. SFT
never saw a score, so it has no special liking for the answers the scorer wrongly loves. It gives E,
the long empty answer, only about 11% of its chance. So the model can move away from the junk answers that the scorer rightly dislikes,
without running all the way to the scorer's mistakes.

### The trap of pushing on a measurement

Post-training is the clearest example in this course of a general trap, known as **Goodhart's law**.
Push hard on a measure of what you want, and it stops tracking what you wanted, so you start getting the
measurement instead of the thing.

Picture a class where the teacher, short on time, starts giving higher marks to longer essays, because
the longer ones have usually been the more careful ones. One student notices and begins padding every
essay with sentences that say nothing new. Their marks go up while their essays get worse, so the marks
have stopped measuring what the teacher cared about. In the lab, the scorer plays the teacher and the
tuned model plays the student. The model is not scheming, though, since the math pushes it toward
whatever scores higher.

Raising a score without raising the quality it was meant to measure is called **reward hacking**, and
the lab's last chart is a picture of it. That gap between a measurement and what you meant is also the
core of the **alignment** problem. This lab shows one small piece of it, and the subsection on alignment
under Where it breaks says which pieces it leaves out.

## How to play with it

The lab has four cards, and they follow the stages in order. At the top of the first card, a strip of
five stages runs Pretrained → SFT → Preferences → Reward model → Policy + KL. Each stage shows a short
live readout, and the highlighted stage follows the lesson step you are on.

### 1. Watch SFT move the chances

On the **Post-training pipeline** card, each of the six answer cards shows three bars. The **base** bar
shows the pretrained model's chance of giving that answer, the **SFT** bar shows the chance after
fine-tuning, and the **tuned** bar shows the final chance. Each card also shows **r**, the scorer's
reward, and **gold**, the careful rater's score. Set **SFT steps** to 0, and the base and SFT bars
match, with A out in front at about 50%.

Now raise SFT steps to 8, and watch A's SFT bar shrink while the bars for B and F, the two
demonstrations, grow. Then set it back to 3, the starting value, so the numbers you see match the ones
in this lesson.

### 2. Be the judge

On **Preference pairs**, each row is one pair, and the pressed button shows which answer is preferred.
The starting labels already pick a winner in five pairs and leave **Pair 5** unlabelled. Under each row,
the lab prints the scorer's predicted chance that the first answer beats the second, and it shows this
even for a pair that nobody has labeled.

Click an answer to pick it, and click a pressed button again to clear that label. Below the rows, three
buttons change every label at once: **Restore default labels**, **Label like the gold rater** and
**Clear all**.

### 3. Read what the scorer learned

On **Reward model fit**, read the three weight bars, each marked "raises reward" or "lowers reward".
Below the loss chart, three readouts sum up the fit. **Pairs used** counts your labels, and **Labels
fitted** counts how many of those pairs the scorer now ranks the way you did. **Agrees with gold** goes
further, because it checks all 15 ways of pairing up the six answers, most of which you never labeled,
against the gold scores.

With the starting labels, Labels fitted reads 5 of 5, while Agrees with gold reads only 9 of 15.

### 4. Loosen the leash

On **Optimize against the reward model**, the **KL coefficient β** slider runs from 20 down to 0.02.
Each notch multiplies or divides β by the same amount, so the stretch from 0.02 to 0.2 takes up as much
room as the stretch from 2 to 20. Under the slider, **Reward-model score** shows the tuned model's
average reward, the same `E[r]` as in the target, and **Gold score** shows the same kind of average for
gold. **KL from SFT** shows how far the tuned
model has moved from the SFT model, in nats.

The chart plots both scores against KL from SFT, and a marker shows where your current β sits. To fit
both lines on one chart, the reward-model line is rescaled so that the worst answer counts as 0 and the
best answer counts as 1.

**Try this:** Leave the default labels, push **KL coefficient β** up to 20, and then drag it all the way
down to 0.02. The Reward-model score keeps climbing the whole way. On the chart, the gold curve starts
at 0.49 at a KL of 0, where the model still equals the SFT model. Even β 20 lets the model move a
little, so there the Gold score readout already shows about 0.51. The curve rises to a peak of about
0.56 near β 3.5. At that peak, KL
from SFT reads about 0.20 nats. After that the gold score collapses to 0.10, because the tuned model has
piled onto E, the long answer that never explains anything. On the first card, an outline marks the
tuned model's favorite answer, and you can watch it move from B to F and then to E.

### 5. Break the length shortcut

Now give the scorer the one comparison it was missing. On **Preference pairs**, label **Pair 5** for B,
the short answer that names the mechanism, over E, the long one that does not. On Reward model fit,
Words ÷ 10 falls from +1.255 to +0.456. Meanwhile **Names mechanism** rises to about +1.86 and becomes
the biggest weight, and **Opens with praise** slips a little below zero.

Agrees with gold climbs to 12 of 15 at the same time. Sweep KL coefficient β down again, and this time
the gold score peaks at about 0.91. It ends at 0.85, on C, a correct answer padded with praise, instead
of collapsing onto E.

### 6. Compare with the gold rater

Finally, press **Label like the gold rater**, which labels every pair the way the gold scores would.
Agrees with gold reaches 13 of 15, and Opens with praise drops far below zero, to about −0.93. That
happens because Pair 4 now prefers the plainer B over C, which opens with "Great question!".

Even these perfect labels cannot reach 15 of 15. The scorer sees only three numbers per answer, and no
choice of three weights puts all six answers in exactly gold's order.

## What to notice

- In the default labels, the preferred answer is always the longer one. Pairs 1 to 3 also favor the
  answer that explains the scattering; in Pair 4 both answers explain it, and in Pair 6 neither does.
- Length is the only feature that sides with the winner in all five labeled pairs, while naming the
  mechanism explains only Pairs 1 to 3. So the scorer leans on length. A gap of 40 words also becomes a
  gap of 4 in Words ÷ 10, far bigger than the gap of 1 that naming the mechanism can make. That means a
  modest weight on length already buys a large difference in reward.
- **Labels fitted** can be perfect while **Agrees with gold** is poor, because matching your clicks is
  not the same as learning what you meant by them.
- Before Pair 5 is labeled, the scorer already puts `P(E ≻ B)` at 0.98, even though nobody compared
  those two answers. It has carried its taste for length over to a pair it never saw.
- The reward-model curve never bends down, and it cannot, because for each β the tuned model is built to
  push that score as high as the leash allows. Only an independent score, like gold, can reveal reward
  hacking.
- One extra comparison, Pair 5, does more for the final answers than any setting of β. With the starting
  labels no β gets gold above about 0.56, but with Pair 5 labeled the gold score peaks near 0.91. That
  is because β never changes what the scorer learned.
- The leash, β, decides how far along the curve the model goes, but it cannot change the curve itself.
  Without a gold score you could not see where the peak is. A larger β keeps the model close to SFT and
  gives up part of the gain, so choosing β is a trade-off with no setting that is right in general.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- Six fixed answers stand in for everything a model could write, while a real model builds its answers
  piece by piece from a huge range of possibilities.
- The starting chances, the "names mechanism" flags and the gold scores were all written by the lesson's
  authors. The word counts and the praise check, by contrast, are measured from the text.
- Gold is an authored score standing in for a careful rater, not the result of a study with real people.
- The scorer sees three numbers instead of the words, so it cannot notice anything else about an answer.
- The tuning is calculated exactly rather than learned by trial and error, so none of the randomness or
  extra machinery of real training appears here.
- Your labels are one person's six clicks on one prompt, while real preference data pools the picks of
  many people on many prompts.
- Because the lab changes six chances for a single prompt, it cannot show how training on some prompts
  changes a model's answers to others.
- Because the tuned model is calculated straight from the scorer, the lab cannot show a model that
  learned a goal of its own. The alignment subsection below covers that problem separately.

### Where the real thing fails

Real reward models read the whole answer and still pick up shortcuts, and they have been measured
favoring longer answers, confident wording and agreement with the user. That last habit drives
**sycophancy**, where a model drops a correct answer as soon as you push back.

Picture a friend in your group chat who always ends up agreeing with whoever spoke last. Ask whether a
film was good and they say yes, but tell them you hated it and suddenly they hated it too. Their replies
please you in the moment but tell you nothing, and a model trained to win people's approval can drift
toward the same habit.

Pushing hard against a scorer like that produces the same curve you saw in the lab. A 2022 study by Gao
and colleagues measured it by using a much bigger reward model to play the part of the careful rater. The stand-in score kept rising,
while the gold score rose, peaked and then fell. A KL penalty slows that slide, but it does not remove
it.

**A common mix-up:** people assume that if the reward number goes up, the model is getting better. Past
a point, the scorer's number keeps rising while a careful human's rating falls, because the model has
found a loophole in the scorer. That is reward hacking, and the lab's last chart shows it happening.

Post-training has other limits too, because it can teach a model not to *say* certain things without
removing the ability underneath. That is why a jailbreak, a cleverly worded prompt that gets around that
training, can work. Alignment training can also cost a model some performance on benchmarks, the shared
tests researchers use to compare models, and that cost is called the **alignment tax**.

Finally, preference data reflects whoever produced it. The instructions the raters were given, who the
raters are and how they are rewarded for the work all shape what "better" means. So no set of
preferences is free of someone's choices.

### What alignment is, and what RLHF does not fix

**Alignment** means getting a model to do what its makers and users actually want, but a model never
sees what you want directly. It only sees stand-ins, such as example answers, which of two answers
people picked, a written rule, or a score. In the cook picture, the cook only ever meets the judge's
scores, never the diner's real appetite, and the alignment problem is the gap between the two.

Researchers ask two separate questions about that gap. The first is whether the stand-in is the right
thing to aim at, which is called outer alignment. The second is whether the trained model really aims at
that stand-in, which is called inner alignment. The coin game described below, under goal
misgeneralization, is an example of the second question going wrong.

### What this lab shows about alignment

In this lab, the scorer is a stand-in built from your clicks, and the gold score stands in for what you
meant. When you drag **KL coefficient β** down, the scorer's number rises the whole way. Meanwhile the
gold score peaks, near 0.56 at 0.20 nats on the default labels, and then falls to 0.10. A measure pushed
hard stops tracking what it measured, which is Goodhart's law again. The leash only limits how far the
model goes, and it does not fix the stand-in.

Every failure in this lab sits in the stand-in itself. The tuned model does exactly what the scorer
says, so the lab shows the first question, outer alignment, and only that one.

### What this lab does not show

Four problems sit outside this lab, and because the evidence for each one is uneven, each comes with a
status that says how well it is established.

- **Goal misgeneralization** is when a model learns a goal that matches the training reward everywhere
  it was trained, yet differs from the intended goal in new situations. In a 2022 game study by Langosco
  and colleagues, a game-playing program was trained on levels where a coin always sat at the far right.
  When the coin was moved, the program kept running right, as if its goal had been "go right" rather
  than "get the coin". In training, both goals always led to the same place, so the reward never told
  them apart. *Status:* shown in small game worlds, while
  whether it explains chatbot behavior is still open. This lab cannot show it, because its model is
  defined directly by the scorer and has no goal of its own that could differ.
- **Scalable oversight** covers ways of supervising answers that are hard for people to check, such as a
  long program or a subtle argument. Every pick in RLHF needs a person who can tell which answer is
  better, and for answers like these they may not be able to. Picture judging two answers to a hard
  chess puzzle when you only know how the pieces move: you could pick one, but your pick would mean very
  little. Ideas for helping include a second model that points out flaws to the human judge, debates
  between models, and feedback against written principles. *Status:* these give some help in small
  studies, but none is proven enough for a model that does better than its judges.
- **Sycophancy**, agreeing with the user against the evidence, differs from the others because it has
  been measured. Studies by Perez and colleagues in 2022 and by Sharma and colleagues in 2023 looked at
  assistants trained on human feedback. They found that these assistants sometimes tailor their answers
  to a view the user has stated, or drop a correct answer after pushback. They also found that people
  and scorers sometimes rate agreeable answers above correct ones, so it is the length shortcut again,
  with agreement taking the place of length. *Status:* established as a behavior, while how much each
  training stage adds to it is still being worked out.
- **Deceptive behavior** is the worry that a model might behave well when it seems to be watched and
  differently when it seems not to be. It
  might also do badly on a test on purpose to hide a skill, which is called sandbagging. The evidence
  comes from set-ups built on purpose to study it. In a 2024 study by Hubinger and colleagues, models
  were trained to carry a hidden trigger, a secret cue that switches on a different behavior. The models
  kept that trigger through safety training. Another 2024 study, by van der Weij and colleagues, showed
  that models can be prompted or trained to do worse on tests than they are able to. *Status:* whether
  this arises on its own during ordinary training is open.

### How developers check before release

Before releasing a model, developers run **dangerous-capability evaluations**, which are tests of
whether it can do something seriously harmful. The worry is harm if someone misused the model or if it
acted on its own, such as giving real help toward weapons or carrying out cyber attacks. Published
frameworks, such as Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google
DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework, tie the results to safeguards each developer promises to apply.

A failed test shows that one attempt failed, not that the skill is missing. Picture a friend who cannot
solve a riddle on the first try: that tells you about one try only, and with a hint they might well get
it. For the same reason, testers also try extra training aimed at the skill, and extra tools, to draw it
out. The tests, and the scores that count as a warning sign, are each developer's own promises, and
they are still new and changing.

### Where this leaves RLHF

RLHF is the best-tested way to make a model behave as intended on the cases people can check. The
problems above are mostly about the cases people cannot check, or about what a stand-in leaves out. As
you read about any of them, keep three kinds of statement apart: what this lab computes, what has been
measured elsewhere, and what is still open.

## Going deeper

### Less human labeling

In **Constitutional AI**, a model critiques and rewrites its own answers against a list of written
principles, and those rewrites become fine-tuning data. The model then judges pairs of answers against
the same principles to make preference data, so people write the principles instead of clicking pairs.

### No scorer where a checker exists

Answers in math and code can be checked, because a program can be run against its tests and a math
answer can be compared with an answer key. Training against a real checker, called a verifiable reward,
removes the learned scorer and its loopholes, but it only works for tasks that can be checked.

### Simpler machinery

**DPO**, short for direct preference optimization, skips the separate scorer and trains the model
directly on the preference pairs. It does this by rearranging the same target that this lab computes.
Group-based methods such as GRPO, short for group relative policy optimization, have the model write
several answers to the same prompt and score those
answers against each other. **Preference optimization in practice** runs DPO on a tiny model you can
watch.

### How real RLHF reaches the target

Real systems approximate the lab's exact recipe with a method called PPO, short for proximal policy
optimization. PPO has the model write
answers, scores them and updates the model in small, limited steps. Because it learns from whatever
answers the model happens to write, its progress is noisy, and that noise is one of the things this lab
leaves out.

### More on KL divergence

The comparison inside KL divergence uses the logarithm of the new chance divided by the old chance,
which is a tool you may meet in later math classes. That is also where nats come from, since using the
natural logarithm gives distances in nats.

### More on SFT

Real SFT also teaches a **chat template**, a set of special markers showing where the instructions, the
user's message and the reply each begin. Those markers let the model tell instructions apart from the
text it is working on, and **Instruction tuning & templates** builds that stage on a real tiny model.
Small open models, whose trained numbers are published for anyone to download, are also often trained
on a larger model's answers, which is why they inherit its
habits and mistakes.

### Neighboring modules

asks who bears the costs and how rules and laws treat them. Both sit beside alignment rather than inside
it.
