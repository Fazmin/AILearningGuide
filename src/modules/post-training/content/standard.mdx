## The big picture

This module covers post-training, the training that follows pretraining and shapes a base model
into an assistant. Every stage of it faces one question: how do you move a model toward what people
intend, when intent only arrives through proxies such as demonstrations, comparisons, and scores?

The module runs all three stages on one prompt: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated
demonstrations, a reward model (a learned scorer) fitted to pairwise comparisons, and optimization
against that scorer under a KL penalty, a charge for drifting from the SFT model. In real systems,
SFT reuses the next-token cross-entropy loss from **Next-token prediction**, and optimization
usually treats each sampled response as one action that gets one score, the bandit setup from
**Reinforcement learning foundations**.

### Where it shows up

Post-training sits between a pretrained base model and the assistant people talk to. RLHF
(reinforcement learning from human feedback) optimizes against a reward model with PPO (proximal
policy optimization), a policy-gradient method that clips each update so the policy moves in small
steps. DPO (direct preference optimization) trains on preference pairs with no separate reward model.

### Why it matters

A base model predicts how a page continues, and the continuation of a question is often not an
answer. Demonstrations move it toward answering, but they are expensive to write and comparisons
are cheap, so the pipeline turns next to comparisons and a scorer learned from them.

That scorer is a proxy, and optimizing hard against it rewards its mistakes along with the intended
behavior. The proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls. This failure, reward hacking, shows
up only against an independent score, and a KL penalty slows it but does not stop it.

### What you will be able to explain

- How pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and KL-regularized policy optimization
  differ.
- How a Bradley–Terry reward model is fitted from pairwise preferences, and how to read what it
  actually learned.
- Why reward hacking shows up as a proxy score rising while true quality falls.
- Alignment as the gap between what can be measured and what is wanted, and the problems RLHF does

### What the lab shows

Everything runs on one prompt, "Explain why the sky is blue," and six fixed responses, A to F, so
every distribution in the lab, SFT's included, is over six whole responses rather than over tokens.
**Post-training pipeline** shows each response's base, SFT, and tuned probability; **SFT steps**
takes real cross-entropy gradient steps toward the demonstrations B and F.

**Preference pairs** holds six comparisons. Five come labelled by default, and in each one the
preferred response is also the longer one. **Pair 5**, a long answer that never names the mechanism
against a short one that does, starts unlabelled. **Reward model fit** refits a three-weight
Bradley–Terry scorer to the current labels, the defaults or yours, with 200 gradient steps.

The key experiment is on **Optimize against the reward model**. With the default labels, lower
**KL coefficient β** from 20 to 0.02 and watch **Reward-model score** rise the whole way while
**Gold score** peaks and falls. Then label **Pair 5** for B, the short answer that names the
mechanism, and sweep again.

The tuned policy is the exact closed-form optimum, with no sampling and no PPO. Word count and
praise are measured from the text; the base logits, the "names mechanism" flag, and the gold scores
are authored, and gold stands in for what you meant.

## What it is

Pretraining produces a text predictor, not an assistant. Given "Explain why the sky is blue," a
raw predictor's likely continuation might be more exam questions, because that is what follows
such a line in web text. It is behaving correctly and uselessly.

**Post-training** is the set of stages that turn that predictor into something that answers. This
lab runs all of them on one prompt with a deliberately tiny policy: a probability distribution over
six complete responses. Each response is one action, the bandit view from Reinforcement learning
foundations.

### Stage one: supervised fine-tuning

**SFT** is ordinary next-token cross-entropy on curated demonstrations, usually counted only on the
response tokens. The loss is the pretraining loss; the data changed. Here the authored base model
puts 50% on A, another exam question. **SFT steps** takes real gradient steps toward the
demonstrations B and F; eight steps move them to 38% and 36%.

Real SFT also teaches the **chat template**: special tokens that delimit system, user, and
assistant turns, so the model can tell instructions from content.

### Stage two: a reward model from comparisons

Writing an ideal response is slow; choosing the better of two is fast and more consistent. Those
choices train a **reward model** with the **Bradley-Terry** objective: `P(A ≻ B) = σ(r_A − r_B)`.

Here `r(y) = w·φ(y)`, where φ is three numbers: words ÷ 10, whether the response names the
scattering mechanism, and whether it opens with praise. **Reward model fit** runs 200 gradient steps
on the pairs you label.

### Stage three: optimize under a KL leash

The policy is then tuned to maximize `E[r] − β·KL(π‖π_SFT)`. The **KL penalty** charges it for
drifting from the SFT model. **KL divergence** is that drift, measured:

```text
KL(π‖π_SFT) = Σ π(y) · ln( π(y) / π_SFT(y) )
```

It is the expected log-ratio of the tuned probabilities to the SFT probabilities, averaged over the
tuned policy's own responses. It is never negative, it is zero only when the two distributions are
identical, and it is not symmetric: swapping the arguments gives a different number. The lab's
**KL from SFT** readout is this sum in nats; with the default labels the gold score peaks near 0.20 nats.

The full objective has an exact optimum, `π(y) ∝ π_SFT(y)·exp(r(y)/β)`,
which this lab computes directly.

**RLHF** approximates the same optimum by sampling responses and updating with PPO. That
reward-after-acting signal is the reinforcement learning that **What AI actually is** sorted from labels
and raw text, and **Reinforcement learning foundations** runs the basic policy-gradient update on a
bandit. **DPO**
rearranges the optimum so the reward is `β·log(π/π_SFT)` plus a constant, and trains the policy on
pairs directly.

## Why it’s here

Most traits people call a model's personality are set here: helpfulness, verbosity, hedging,
formatting habits, refusals, and willingness to say "I don't know." Two products built on the same
base model can feel completely different.

It is also where a model is optimized directly against an imperfect measurement, so the reward
hacking previewed above is post-training's case of Goodhart's law: push hard on a measure, and it
stops tracking what you wanted. That gap between a measure and what you meant is the **alignment**
problem. This lab shows one small piece of it, and the subsection under Where it breaks says which
pieces it leaves out.

## How to play with it

1. Move **SFT steps** from 0 to 8 and watch A's base-model lead collapse as B and F grow.
2. Read the default labels on **Preference pairs**. Pairs 1 to 3 prefer the answer that names the
   mechanism; pair 4 prefers the longer of two answers that both name it, and pair 6 the longer of
   two that neither names. Pair 5 starts unlabelled, and every preferred answer is also the longer
   one. **Reward model fit** reports
   **Words ÷ 10** at +1.255 and **Agrees with gold** at 9 of 15.
3. Move **KL coefficient β** from 20 to 0.02. **Gold score** starts at 0.49, peaks at 0.56 near
   KL 0.20 nats, then falls to 0.10 as the policy moves onto E. **Reward-model score** rises the whole
   way.
4. Label **Pair 5** for B and sweep β again. **Words ÷ 10** falls to +0.456, and gold now peaks at
   0.91.

## What to notice

- **Labels fitted** reads 5 of 5 while **Agrees with gold** reads 9 of 15. Fitting your data is not
  the same as learning your criterion.
- When every preferred answer is longer, the scorer cannot tell whether you liked length or
  correctness. It picks the feature with the larger differences.
- The reward-model curve never bends down. It cannot; the tilt maximizes it. Only an independent
  score can show **reward hacking**.
- A larger β keeps the policy close to SFT and gives up part of the gain. There is no β that is
  correct in general.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Six fixed responses stand in for everything a model could write. The base logits,
the "names mechanism" flag, and the gold scores are authored; word count and praise are measured
from the text. The reward model sees only three features. The tuned policy is the exact optimum,
so there is no sampling noise, no PPO clipping, and no value function.

**In real systems.** Reward models read the whole response and still learn shortcuts. They have been
measured favoring longer answers, confident phrasing, and agreement with the user. The last is a
driver of **sycophancy**: a model abandoning a correct answer after mild pushback.

Optimizing hard against such a scorer produces the curve in this lab. Gao et al. (2022) measured it
against a larger "gold" reward model: the proxy kept rising while the gold score peaked and fell. A
KL penalty slows that; it does not remove it.

Two further limits. Post-training can suppress a behavior without removing the capability, which
is why jailbreaks work. And alignment can cost some benchmark performance, an **alignment tax**.

Finally, preference data encodes whoever produced it. Annotator instructions, demographics, and
incentives shape what "better" means, and there is no neutral setting.

### What alignment is, and what RLHF does not fix

Alignment is getting a model's behavior to match what its developers and users actually intend.
Intent is never handed to a model directly. It arrives through proxies: demonstrations, comparisons,
a written rule, a score. The alignment problem is the gap between those proxies and what we want.

Researchers split it in two. *Outer alignment* asks whether the specified objective is the right
one. *Inner alignment* asks whether the trained model actually pursues that objective.

**What this lab shows.** The reward model is a proxy fitted to your labels, and the gold score stands
in for what you meant. As **KL coefficient β** falls, the reward-model score rises the whole way
while the gold score peaks, near 0.56 at 0.20 nats on the default labels, and then falls to 0.10.
That is Goodhart's law in miniature, and the KL leash only slows it.

Every failure here sits in the objective. The tuned policy is the exact optimum of the reward model,
so it does precisely what the proxy says. That is the outer half of the problem, and only that half.

**What it does not show.** Four problems sit outside this lab. Each carries its status, because the
evidence is uneven.

- **Goal misgeneralization.** A policy can learn a goal that matches the training reward wherever it
  was trained and differs from the intended goal elsewhere. In one reinforcement-learning
  demonstration (Langosco et al., 2022), an agent trained on levels where a coin always sat at the far
  right kept running right after the coin was moved. *Status:* shown in small environments; how far
  it explains language-model behavior is open. This lab cannot show it, because its policy is defined
  by the reward.
- **Scalable oversight.** Both reward-model steps need a person who can say which answer is better.
  For a long program or a subtle argument, they may not be able to. Proposed remedies include
  model-written critiques that help raters find flaws, debate between models, and feedback against
  written principles, as in Constitutional AI. *Status:* some help in controlled studies; none is
  established as enough for a model that outperforms its overseers.
- **Sycophancy.** This one is measured. Studies (Perez et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2023) found that
  assistants trained with human feedback sometimes tailor answers to a user's stated view or drop a
  correct answer after pushback, and that preference data sometimes rates agreeable answers above
  correct ones. It is the length shortcut again, with a different feature. *Status:* established as a
  behavior; each training stage's share of it is still being worked out.
- **Deceptive behavior.** The worry is a model that behaves well when it seems to be observed, or
  underperforms on purpose, called sandbagging, to hide a capability. The evidence comes from
  constructed settings: models trained to carry a hidden backdoor kept it through standard safety
  training (Hubinger et al., 2024), and models can be prompted or fine-tuned to underperform on
  evaluations (van der Weij et al., 2024). *Status:* whether such behavior arises unprompted in
  ordinary training is open.

**How developers check before release.** Developers run **dangerous-capability evaluations**: tests of
whether a model can do things that would be seriously harmful if misused or if it acted on its own,
such as giving real uplift toward weapons or carrying out cyber operations. Published frameworks, such
as Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, and Google DeepMind's
Frontier Safety Framework, tie results to safeguards the developer commits to.

A failed test shows that one attempt failed, not that the capability is absent, so evaluators also try
fine-tuning and scaffolding to draw it out. The tests and thresholds are each developer's own
commitments, and they are still young.

**Where this leaves RLHF.** It is the best-tested way to make a model's behavior match intent on the
cases people can check. The problems above are mostly about the cases they cannot, or about what a
proxy leaves out. Keep three kinds of statement apart: what this lab computes, what has been measured
elsewhere, and what is still open.

## Going deeper

**Less human labeling.** In **Constitutional AI**, a model critiques and revises its own drafts
against written principles to make fine-tuning data, then judges pairs of outputs against those
principles to make preference data. Human effort moves from labeling to writing the principles.

**No learned scorer where a checker exists.** For math and code, a **verifiable reward** comes from
tests or an answer key, which removes the exploitable reward model. It only applies where checking
is possible.

**Simpler optimizers.** DPO removed the separate reward model. Group-based methods such as GRPO score
several sampled responses to the same prompt against each other instead of training a value
function.

**Instruction tuning & templates** builds the SFT stage on a real tiny model, and **Preference
optimization in practice** runs DPO on it. Small open models are also often distilled from a larger
model's answers, which is why they inherit its habits and mistakes.

**Failure modes, safety, and security** covers attacks on deployed systems, and **Ethics and impact**
asks who bears the costs and how regulation treats them. Both sit beside alignment rather than inside it.
