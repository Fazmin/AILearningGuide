## The big picture

This module collects the arithmetic later labs lean on: vectors and dot products, matrices and the
linear layer Wx + b, derivatives and gradient descent, probability, and logarithms. It answers a
practical question: when a later lab says score, layer, slope, distribution or loss, which
calculation does it mean?

It follows **What AI actually is**. Each idea is shown four ways that must agree, ending in the
exact formula the page evaluates.

### Where it shows up

- **Dot products.** **Attention** scores each query against each key with one, and **Tokens &
  embeddings** compares embeddings by cosine, a dot product divided by both lengths. **Data,
  features, and representation** shows where the feature vectors come from.
- **Linear layers.** Layers apply Wx + b. A production layer is the same map in thousands of
  dimensions.
- **Training.** Training follows a slope downhill. **Loss & gradient descent** applies the same
  update to both parameters, w and b, of a fitted line instead of to one isolated w.
- **Sampling and losses.** Sampling reads a distribution, and a loss adds log-probabilities.
- **Classical models.** In **Classical machine learning**, naive Bayes multiplies a prior by
  Gaussian likelihoods, and logistic regression reports σ(w·x + b) as P(y=1|x). **Stacking
  neurons** uses the same sigmoid curve.

### Why it matters

Each calculation guards against a specific misreading:

- A large dot product can come from length rather than alignment.
- On the convex bowl, a step size η above 0.5 overshoots the minimum on every step.
- A likelihood stays a product of tiny fractions until a logarithm turns it into a sum.
- Reading the likelihood alone misjudges a rare class; Bayes' rule keeps the prior in the answer.
- A Gaussian's height is a density, not a probability, so it can exceed 1.
- Descent can settle in a shallower valley rather than the global minimum.

## What it is

A vector is an ordered list treated as a point or an arrow. The dot product is the sum of paired coordinates, equal to |a||b|cos θ, and it is also the linear score w·x. A matrix is a linear map: each output coordinate is a row dotted with the input. A linear layer is y = Wx + b, affine unless b = 0.

A derivative is the local slope. The gradient of a loss is the direction of steepest increase; descent steps the other way. A probability is a non-negative share in a set that sums to one. Conditional probability P(y|x) restricts that set. Likelihood is the probability the model assigned to the data you saw. A logarithm turns a product into a sum. Cross-entropy is expected surprise of p under q.

`topic` selects which identity this page focuses.

### Four representations, one number

Each widget shows the same calculation as words, a picture, a tiny table or walk-through, then a closed form. If those four disagree, the page is wrong.

### A derivative by hand

On f(x) = x² at the default x = 1.2, f = 1.44 and the tangent slope is 2x = 2.4. Nudge by the default ε = 0.4: f(1.6) = 2.56, so the secant slope is (2.56 − 1.44) / 0.4 = 2.8. That is exactly 2x + ε = 2.4 + 0.4.

Shrink the nudge to ε = 0.05 and the secant reads 2.45. The gap to the tangent is the nudge itself, so the derivative is the number the ratio settles on as ε goes to zero. In training the thing you nudge is a parameter, and the slope it returns is the gradient.

### A matrix by hand

Take the default W = [[1, 0.4], [0, 1]], x = (1, 0.2), and b = (0.2, 0.1). Row one dotted with x is 1·1 + 0.4·0.2 = 1.08, and adding 0.2 gives 1.28. Row two is 0·1 + 1·0.2 = 0.2, and adding 0.1 gives 0.30. So Wx + b = (1.28, 0.30).

The determinant is 1·1 − 0.4·0 = 1, so areas keep their size: this W shears the square sideways rather than stretching it. Each output coordinate is one row of W dotted with x, which is why a linear layer is a bank of dot products.

### A logarithm by hand

Set True p = 0.25 and Model q = 0.60. Under q the two outcomes surprise by −ln 0.60 = 0.511 and −ln 0.40 = 0.916 nats. Weighted by the truth, H(p,q) = 0.25·0.511 + 0.75·0.916 = 0.815.

The truth's own entropy is H(p) = 0.25·1.386 + 0.75·0.288 = 0.562. The gap is KL = 0.815 − 0.562 = 0.253, the price of believing q when the world is p. Move q onto p and the gap closes to 0. Because a log turns a product into a sum, a loss adds terms like these instead of multiplying tiny fractions.

### Bayes' rule: prior, likelihood, posterior

Bayes' rule updates a belief: P(y|x) = P(y) P(x|y) / P(x). The prior P(y) is what you believed before seeing x. The likelihood P(x|y) is how probable x is if y holds. The evidence P(x) rescales the result so the posterior sums to one.

On the count table, P(x=1|y=1) = 3/4 and P(x=1|y=0) = 1/4. At the table's own prior of 0.50 the posterior is 0.75. Lower the prior to 0.10 and the same evidence only reaches 0.25. In odds form, posterior odds = prior odds × 3, so a rare class needs strong evidence.

### Mean and variance: the two numbers of a Gaussian

The mean is the average, the center of the bell. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and its square root σ is back in the units of x. For the sample −1, 0, 1, 2 the mean is 0.50 and the variance is 1.25.

A Gaussian N(μ, σ²) is fixed by those two numbers. About 68% of its area lies within one σ of μ, and its height is a density, not a probability. Naive Bayes in Classical machine learning fits one such pair per class and per feature.

### From logit to probability

A logit is any real number z. The sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^−z) maps it to a probability between 0 and 1, with σ(0) = 0.5, and the logit is the log-odds ln(p/(1−p)). With two classes, softmax is the sigmoid of the logit gap: P(A) = σ(A − B), so shifting both logits changes nothing.

Logistic regression computes z = w·x + b and reports σ(z) as P(y=1|x). The curve is drawn here before Stacking neurons uses it.

In logit to probability, raise Logit A and Logit B together. On logarithms, move True p and Model q until KL is near 0.

## What to notice

- Cosine isolates angle; the raw dot product still mixes length. The foot of the dotted drop is the projection onto b.
- Distance |a − b| is the straight-line gap between the two arrow tips, the length of a − b. It is 0 only when a = b, and unlike cosine it changes when you stretch an arrow without turning it. Nearest-neighbor methods rank by it.
- The columns of W are where (1, 0) and (0, 1) land, det W scales area, and b translates the whole parallelogram. A stack needs a nonlinearity before it can bend.
- For f(x)=x² the gap between secant and 2x is exactly ε.
- The tilted well has two valleys of different depth, near w = −1.04 (L ≈ 0.24) and w = 0.95 (L ≈ 0.94). Settling in the shallower one is a real local minimum. On the bowl, L'' = 2, so η above 0.5 overshoots each step. That 1-D picture does not describe a high-dimensional network.
- Softmax is bookkeeping. Likelihood of A,A,B is P(A)²P(B). Cross-entropy equals entropy only when q = p.
- A rare class stays rare. At a prior of 0.10 the same 3-to-1 evidence lifts the posterior only to 0.25, because posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.
- Raising the variance widens the bell and lowers its peak, since the area is fixed at 1. A density can exceed 1 when the variance is small. A probability cannot.
- Two-class softmax is the sigmoid of the logit gap. Raising both logits by the same amount leaves P(A) unchanged.

## Where it breaks

In this lab the widgets are 1-D or 2-D closed forms. The parabola is y = x² sampled at 81 points. The two loss surfaces are the formulas printed on the card. Counts in the probability table, its likelihoods, and the four-number Gaussian sample are authored.

In the real mechanism, a Bayes update multiplies many estimated likelihoods rather than reading one from a table, and the posterior still depends on a prior you must justify. A Gaussian is one shape among many, and real features are often not bell-shaped.

Automatic differentiation traces a much larger graph. Discrete samples have no derivative until you choose a surrogate. A production layer is the same Wx+b in thousands of dimensions. High-dimensional loss geometry is not a double well, and “all local minima are terrible” is the mythology this page refuses.
