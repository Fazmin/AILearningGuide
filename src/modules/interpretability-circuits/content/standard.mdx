## The big picture

This module is about **circuits**: claims that specific components of a network, connected in a
specific way, implement a specific behavior. It asks which components a behavior actually depends
on, and by which route the information it needs travels, which attention maps and correlations
alone cannot settle.

Where **Interpretability I: features** found candidate features by correlation, this module
intervenes, in two ways. **Ablation** takes a component out to ask whether the behavior needs it.
**Activation patching** asks where the information an answer depends on sits, using a clean run and
a corrupted run: two nearly identical prompts that call for different answers.

Both are aimed at one behavior, copying a repeated string, and at its textbook explanation, the
induction circuit, in which a **previous-token head** in layer 1 feeds an **induction head** in
layer 2 so that the model can copy whatever followed the current letter last time.

### Where it shows up

- **Induction heads in language models.** Elhage et al. (2021) showed they need at least two
  layers, a previous-token head feeding an induction head, and Olsson et al. (2022) argued they may
  be the source of much in-context learning.
- **Head-level circuits.** Indirect object identification in GPT-2 small (Wang et al., 2022) was
  mapped to 26 heads in 7 classes and scored on faithfulness, completeness and minimality: whether
  the circuit reproduces the behavior alone, captures everything relevant, and needs every part.
- **Feature-level circuits.** Anthropic's circuit tracing (Ameisen et al., 2025) builds attribution
  graphs over interpretable features in a replacement model, rather than over raw heads.

### Why it matters

Intervention is what lets a circuit claim fail. Ablation can show that a behavior survives without a
component, and patching can show that the information an answer needs sits somewhere other than
where the story put it.

The interventions have failure modes of their own. Zero-ablation feeds later layers a value they
never saw, so it can overstate a role. When one component is removed, others can compensate
(**self-repair**), so a small effect can hide a real role. And a patch's **recovery**, the share of
the clean-minus-corrupted gap on a chosen metric that it restores, means nothing when that gap is
tiny, because the gap is the divisor.

### What you will be able to explain

- Read the two halves of an induction circuit in attention, and say why changing the repeat period,
  including to one the model never saw, separates a head that follows content from one that reads a
  fixed distance back.
- Use ablation to test whether a head is necessary and activation patching to locate the
  information an answer depends on, and say what causal claim each supports.
- State the limits of a circuit claim: the ablation's replacement value, self-repair, a gap too
  small to divide by, and results from one prompt, one metric and one small model.

### What the lab shows

The model is the **Attention** lab's two-layer, four-head character transformer, run in your
browser and checked against ONNX Runtime, so every live readout is measured from its forward
passes. The live prompts are synthetic: capitals from one fixed pool, 24 letters long, repeating with
the **Repeat period** you set, from 6 to 13.

In **Copy task and attention**, the head buttons, `L1 H1` to `L2 H4`, choose which head's attention
grid to show: solid green outlines mark the key an induction head would read, dashed outlines the
previous letter. The decisive test sets **Repeat period** to 11 or 13, which the repeated-string
training never used, and checks whether the induction heads' attention moves with the period.

In **Head ablation**, toggle heads off individually or with **Ablate layer 1** and
**Ablate layer 2**, and set **Ablation** to Zero or Mean; Mean substitutes each head's average over
eight random repeats of the same period. Read **Change** and
**Attention on the induction key, L2 H2 · L2 H3**.

In **Activation patching**, **Corrupt** replaces one letter with `Y`: The letter to copy gives the
main sweep, and An unrelated letter is a control whose **Gap** is too small for its recoveries to
mean anything. Each grid cell pastes one residual-stream vector, **After block 1** or
**After block 2** at one position, from the clean run into the corrupted one.

The live sweep's metric is a **logit difference**, the right letter's logit minus that of the foil
`Y`. **Sweep** also offers Cached · speaker and Cached · letters, two sweeps precomputed offline
with the target logit alone as the metric. Cached · speaker uses a short
ROMEO/JULIET prompt pair rather than the letter pool.

## What it is

The previous lab found candidate features by looking at what correlates with what. This lab asks the
harder question: does a component actually *cause* a behavior?

A **circuit** is a claim of the form "these components, connected this way, implement this
behavior." Supporting one needs intervention, because observation alone cannot separate a component
that computes the answer from one that merely correlates with it.

### The model and the behavior

Everything here runs on the course's real two-layer, four-head character transformer, the same file
the attention lab uses: 1.4 million parameters, trained on Shakespeare plus synthetic repeated strings.
Its weights are read in your browser and checked against ONNX Runtime.

The behavior is copying: given `QXZRKWMP` repeated three times, predict the next letter. The textbook
mechanism has two parts. A **previous-token head** in layer 1 writes into each position a note of
the letter just before it. An **induction head** in layer 2 attends from the current letter to the
letter after its earlier occurrence, by matching against those notes, and copies it.

This model draws that picture: L1 H3 and L1 H4 look at the previous letter, L2 H2 and L2 H3 at
the letter after the earlier match. The lab asks whether the picture is causal, and how far it goes.

### Three instruments

- **Attention** shows where each head looks. It is observation, not intervention.
- **Ablation** removes a component and measures the damage: is it *necessary*? You can replace the
  head's output with zero, or with its average over a reference set (**mean ablation**).
- **Activation patching** runs a **clean and corrupted run**, two minimally different prompts, pastes
  one activation from the clean one into the corrupted one, and measures how much of a chosen metric
  returns: *where is the information?*

## Why it’s here

"Attention was high here" and "the answer depended on this" are different statements, and only the
second is a finding.

The same tools support practical safety work: localizing where a refusal is decided, and checking
whether a fine-tune removed a capability or only its expression.

## How to play with it

1. In **Copy task and attention**, choose `L2 H3` and read the solid green outlines, the key an
   induction head would read. Then choose `L1 H4` and read the dashed outlines, the previous letter.
   Compare the table's two columns for all eight heads.
2. In **Head ablation**, switch off `L1 H4`, then `L2 H3`, then `L2 H1`, reading **Change** and the
   attention on the induction key each time. Then set **Ablation** to Mean and compare what `L2 H3`
   costs.
3. In **Activation patching**, click `After block 1` at the amber position, the letter being copied,
   and then at the last position. Then set **Corrupt** to An unrelated letter and read the Gap.
4. Set **Repeat period** to 11 or 13, which the model's repeated-string training never used, and
   repeat step 1.

## What to notice

### The attention picture matches the textbook

Copy accuracy is 94.5% at period 8 and between 88.0% and 97.2% at every period from 6 to 13,
including the unseen 11 and 13. The weak spot is the first letter of the second repeat; from the
second letter on, the mean is at least 95% at every period.

Only L2 H2 and L2 H3 put much attention on the induction key: 0.66 and 0.67 at period 8, and 0.55 to
0.84 across periods. No other head puts more than 0.10 there. L1 H3 and L1 H4 put 0.98 or more of their
attention on the previous letter at every period.

At short periods the prompt holds several earlier copies, and the induction heads spread across
them. At period 6, L2 H2 and L2 H3 put 0.55 and 0.56 on the nearest copy and 0.89 and 0.93 on the
letter after any earlier copy.

### The period test passes

At period 8 the key after the previous occurrence is 7 back, so a head that always looked 7 back
would draw the same stripe and copy as well. Changing the period moves the previous occurrence but
not a fixed distance.

At every period from 6 to 13, the strongest offset of L2 H2 and L2 H3 is the period minus one, and
the outlines and the weights move together. That includes 11 and 13, which the training windows
never used.

This is the test the induction hypothesis had to survive. Surviving it is evidence for content-based
heads, not proof that no positional signal also helps.

### Single heads are necessary

At period 8 the intact model scores 94.5%. Zero-ablating one head leaves L1 H2 5.1%, L2 H3 6.0% and
L1 H4 9.9%; L2 H2 50.2% and L1 H3 56.6%; and L1 H1, L2 H1 and L2 H4 89.2%, 95.8% and 99.6%. All of
layer 1 leaves 1.9% and all of layer 2 leaves 0.4%.

Removing L2 H4 raises accuracy. Necessary and useful are different words, and an ablation reports an
effect on one metric, not what a head is for.

### Layer 1 feeds layer 2, as far as ablation can say

Switch off L1 H4 and the induction heads' attention on the green key falls from 0.66 and 0.67 to
0.19 and 0.24. Switch off L1 H3 and L1 H4 together and it falls to 0.01 and 0.03. The layer-2 heads'
attention depends on what the previous-token heads write, as the induction story predicts.

The story is not complete. Switching off L1 H2 cuts the same attention to 0.30 and 0.21, though
L1 H2 puts 0.98 or more of its attention exactly two letters back, not on the previous letter.
What it contributes is not established here.

### Zero overstates, and the layer-1 result survives

Zero is a value the next layer never saw. With mean ablation, each head's output is replaced by its
average over eight random repeats of the same period. L2 H3 then leaves 47.9% rather than 6.0%, and
L2 H2 75.9% rather than 50.2%. L1 H2, L1 H4 and L1 H3 still leave 27.4%, 24.3% and 48.8%.

Under mean ablation the two induction heads partly overlap: each alone leaves a good share of the
copying, and both off leaves 2.4% (1.3% under zero). The same layer-1 heads matter either way; the
induction heads' solo roles are smaller than zero suggested. Tests on random strings at periods 9 and
13, which the lab does not show, find the same split.

### Patching finds one route, with a large gap

The metric is **logit difference**, `logit(Q) − logit(Y)`, where `Y` replaces the letter to copy.
At period 8 the gap between the runs is 11.70 (clean 10.69, corrupted −1.01). Restoring the corrupted
letter's own position after block 1 recovers 98.8%; every other position in that row stays within 0.6
points of zero, including the last position (−0.5%).

The corrupted run copies the planted letter: its top prediction is `Y`, at 48% for period 8 and 99.5%
for period 13. The gap runs from 6.05 to 22.35 logits across periods 6 to 13, and on 16 random
period-13 strings it averages 20.7 and never falls below 15.9.

### A control shows what a bad measurement looks like

Set **Corrupt** to An unrelated letter. The model still copies correctly, the gap falls to 0.16 at
period 8 and stays under 0.35 at every period, and the warning appears. The grid still prints
percentages, up to 43% in one cell after block 1, because recovery divides by a tiny number. Check
the gap before reading any cell.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

- Prompts are synthetic capitals from one fixed pool, 24 letters long, one prompt per period. Tests
  repeat the main contrasts on seeded random strings the lab does not show.
- Ablation is per whole head. Patching is at block outputs in the residual stream, not per head or per
  path, and the metric is one logit difference against one foil letter.
- No path patching was run. "Layer 1 feeds layer 2" rests on the induction heads' attention falling
  when layer-1 heads are off, and L1 H2 shows other layer-1 heads matter too.
- Mean ablation here is one constant vector per head from eight random repeats. A position-by-position
  mean or a resample ablation could give different sizes.
- One trained model. A second training seed also passed the induction check offline, but only this
  model is in the lab, and a 1.4-million-parameter character model says little about large ones.
- The cached `speaker` pair differs in its final character, so its last position recovering 96% is
  close to built in. It shows a pattern, not a discovery.

### In real systems

Zero-ablation is confounded by distribution shift. Wang et al. (2022) used mean ablation because
zero is an arbitrary value and later components may rely on average activations as an implicit bias.
Mean and resample ablation reduce the problem and do not remove it, as the L2 H3 result above shows.

**Self-repair** complicates every ablation. McGrath et al. (2023) found that ablating one attention
layer can make another compensate, calling it the Hydra effect, and Wang et al. found backup name
mover heads that take over when the main ones are knocked out. A small measured effect is not proof
of a small role.

A circuit found for one prompt template may not transfer to a paraphrase, another model, or another
training seed, and patching says where information passes, not which head wrote it.

## Going deeper

### More specific interventions

**Path patching** patches a single connection between two components rather than a location, which
separates "head A matters" from "head A matters because it feeds head B." It is the missing test for
the layer-1 to layer-2 claim here. **Attribution patching** estimates a whole sweep from two forward
passes and one backward pass, instead of one forward pass per cell.

### Making claims falsifiable

**Causal scrubbing** states a hypothesis as the set of computations that may be swapped without
changing behavior, then performs every permitted swap. The period change in this lab is a small
version of that idea: the induction hypothesis permits changing the period, and the stripe moved with it.

Three criteria are now standard for a circuit: **faithfulness** (does the subgraph reproduce the
behavior alone?), completeness (does it capture everything relevant?), and minimality (is every part
necessary?).

### The field's worked examples

Elhage et al. (2021) showed that induction heads need at least two layers, with a previous-token head
feeding an induction head, and Olsson et al. (2022) argued that induction heads may be the source of
much in-context learning. Indirect object identification in GPT-2 small (Wang et al., 2022) mapped 26
heads in 7 classes, scored by faithfulness, completeness and minimality; the authors say the
criteria also point to remaining gaps in their understanding.

Current work, such as Anthropic's circuit tracing (Ameisen et al., 2025), builds attribution graphs over
interpretable features in a replacement model, in the spirit of the previous lab, rather than over raw
heads. **Steering** along a feature's direction tests whether it has the causal role its label claims.
The Attention and The transformer block labs show the heads and residual stream this lab
intervenes on.
