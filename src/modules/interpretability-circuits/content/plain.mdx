## The big picture

When you ask a chatbot a question, the reply comes from a **language model**, a program that writes
by predicting text one small piece at a time. Inside it are many small working parts, and nobody
gave those parts their jobs by hand, because its internal numbers were set automatically while it
learned from example text. So even the people who build a model cannot point to the part that
handles a particular task.

This module is about finding out which of those parts a behavior really depends on. Looking inside
a model shows you which parts are busy while it works, which is useful but not enough. A part can be
busy without the answer depending on it at all. To learn what the answer really depends on, you
have to change something inside the model on purpose and see whether the output changes too.

Researchers run these tests on real models, and one well-known study traced how a model picks which
of two names should come next in a sentence. It found a whole team of parts doing the job,
including backup parts that step in when the main ones are switched off. The same tests help with
safety work, such as finding where inside a model the decision to refuse a request gets made. They
can also check whether extra training really removed an ability from a model or only stopped it
from showing.

This matters because, without these tests, any explanation of a model's insides would be a
convincing story with no way to catch it when it is wrong. With them, a story has to predict what
will happen when you change something, and a prediction can fail, which is what makes a story worth
trusting once it passes.

The parts this module studies are called **attention heads**, small units that each decide, at
every letter, which earlier letters to look back at. By the end, you will be able to explain:

- how two kinds of attention heads, one in each of a small model's two stages, called layers, team
  up so that the model can copy a repeating pattern;
- how to spot those heads in a picture of attention, and how to test the idea on a repeat length the
  model never saw in training;
- how switching parts off, called **ablation**, and swapping values between two runs of the model,
  called **activation patching**, test what an answer truly depends on;
- why every claim about the inside of a model comes with limits, and which limits apply to the
  tests in this lab.

The lab runs a real, small language model, the same one the Attention lab uses, on a copying puzzle.
The model sees a block of capital letters repeated several times, such as `QXZRKWMP` three times in
a row, and has to guess the letter that comes next. The lab has three cards, which you will work
through in order:

- **Copy task and attention** shows how well the model copies and where each of its eight attention
  heads looks.
- **Head ablation** lets you switch heads off, one at a time or a whole layer at once, and measures
  how much worse the copying gets.
- **Activation patching** runs the model on a normal prompt and on a broken copy, swaps one piece
  between them, and measures how much of the right answer comes back.

Every number in the lab is measured live from the model as you change the settings, using one fixed
puzzle for each repeat length from 6 to 13. There are two exceptions. The third card offers two
saved patching results, worked out ahead of time, that you can switch to. And if the model's
learned numbers, called its weights, cannot load, the first card's attention grid falls back to a
saved example.

## What it is

### A small model with eight heads

The model in this lab reads text one character at a time, and at each character it gives a guess
for the character that comes next. It holds about 1.4 million learned numbers, called
**parameters**, which makes it tiny next to the models behind chatbots. It learned from the plays of
Shakespeare, mixed with made-up strings of letters that repeat.

The model is built in two layers, which the lab also calls **blocks**, and the first one finishes
its work before the second begins. Each layer holds four **attention heads**, and at every letter a
head decides how much to look back at each earlier letter. It then carries information forward from
the letters it looks at most.

The lab names the eight heads by layer and number, so `L1 H4` is layer 1, head 4, and `L2 H3` is
layer 2, head 3. A head shares out its attention among the letters it can see: each letter gets a
share, called an **attention weight**, and the shares always add up to 1. A weight of 0.67 means
about two-thirds of the head's attention goes to that letter, while 0.05 means it barely looks
there.

### The copying puzzle

The prompt is always 24 capital letters, made by repeating a short block taken from a fixed list of
letters. The length of that block is the **Repeat period**, the lab's main control. At period 8 the
block is `QXZRKWMP`, so the prompt is that block three times. The model's job is to guess the 25th
letter, which should be `Q` if the model copies the pattern.

The lab counts positions from 0, the way many computer tools do, so the first letter sits at
position 0 and the last one at position 23. At period 8, each copy of the block fills 8 positions,
which puts the third copy at positions 16 to 23. The last letter, at position 23, is a `P`, and the
`Q` that should come next last appeared at position 16.

### Two halves of the copying trick

How can a model copy a pattern of letters in an order it has never seen before? The standard
explanation uses two heads, one in each layer, working one after the other.

The first half happens in layer 1, where at every letter a head looks at the letter immediately
before it and carries a trace of that letter forward. As a result, each position ends up holding a
note that says "the letter before me was …". The piece of text a model reads at each step is called
a **token**, which here is one character. A head that does this job is therefore called a
**previous-token head**.

The second half happens in layer 2, where a head at the last letter, `P`, searches the earlier
positions for a note saying "the letter before me was `P`". Position 16 carries that note, because
it comes right after the `P` at position 15, and the letter stored there is a `Q`. The head puts
much of its attention on position 16, reads the `Q`, and pushes the model's guess toward `Q`.

A head that works this way is called an **induction head**, and its rule, in short, is "A was
followed by B before, so after A comes B again". Position 8 carries the same note, because it
follows the `P` at position 7, so the head can spread its attention across both earlier copies. The
lab outlines the nearer one, position 16, because it is the previous occurrence of the letter to
copy.

The layer-2 head can find position 16 only because a layer-1 head has already written the note
there, which is why the trick needs two layers working in order. Together, the two halves form a
**circuit**: a claim that particular parts, connected in a particular way, produce a particular
behavior.

In this model, the attention picture points to two previous-token heads, `L1 H3` and `L1 H4`, and
two induction heads, `L2 H2` and `L2 H3`. Whether that picture is true, and how far it goes, is
what the rest of the lab tests.

### Watching is not testing

A picture of where heads look is a good start, but it is only a picture. To see why, imagine a
kitchen where eight helpers work in two shifts to bake a cake, passing ingredients between bowls
lined up along a counter. You want to know which helpers and which bowls the cake's sweetness
really depends on, and you have three ways to find out.

- **Look:** you watch which bowls each helper reaches into, which shows who handles what. But a
  helper can stir a bowl all afternoon without the cake depending on it.
- **Send a helper home:** you bake again without one helper and taste the result. If the cake comes
  out wrong, that helper was needed, at least for this cake.
- **Swap a bowl:** you set up a second kitchen where the sugar jar was filled with salt by mistake,
  so its cake tastes wrong. Partway through, you move one bowl from the good kitchen into the same
  spot in the salty kitchen and let the baking finish. If the cake now tastes sweet, the sweetness
  was traveling in that bowl at that moment.

In the model, the helpers are the eight heads and the cake is the model's guess for the next letter.
The bowls are the lists of numbers the model keeps at each letter, which you will meet properly
below. Looking means reading the attention, sending a helper home is called **ablation**, and
swapping a bowl is called **activation patching**.

The analogy stops matching in one important way, because in a real kitchen you can peek into any
bowl and ask any helper what their job is. In the model, every bowl is a long list of unlabeled
numbers, and no head was ever given a job title. One job can be split across several heads, and one
head can help with several jobs, which is exactly why you need tests and not only a look.

### Ablation: switching a head off

When you switch a head off, you have to decide what to put in its place, and that choice changes
the result. The plainest choice, called **zero-ablation**, replaces the head's output with zeros,
as if the head had added nothing at all. The trouble is that the parts of the model that come after
the head never received a zero from it during training. Some of the damage may therefore come from
that strange input, not from the head's missing work.

In the kitchen, zero-ablation is like the missing helper's ingredient never landing in any bowl at
all, a gap the later work never met during practice. That could throw off what comes next for
reasons unrelated to the helper's real job.

A gentler choice, called **mean ablation**, puts in the head's typical output instead: its average
over eight other random letter strings that repeat with the same period. That one average is used at
every letter, whatever the prompt is. It is like a stand-in who pours the same average scoop into
every bowl, whatever this cake needs. The later work gets something familiar, but none of the
information the real helper would have added for this cake.

The **Ablation** control in the **Head ablation** card switches between these two choices, which it
labels `Zero` and `Mean`.

### Activation patching: swapping one value

At every letter, the model keeps a running list of numbers that sums up the text so far, and each
block adds its own results to that list. A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**, and
this particular running one is called the **residual**, or residual stream. In the kitchen picture,
the residual at each letter is one of the bowls on the counter.

Patching needs two runs of the model that differ in one small way. The **clean** run uses the normal
prompt, while the **corrupted** run uses the same prompt with one letter changed to `Y`, a letter
the prompt never uses. Together they are called a **clean and corrupted run**, and the contrast
between them is what gives a patching result its meaning.

With the **Corrupt** control set to `The letter to copy`, the lab changes the `Q` at position 16 into
a `Y`. The model then copies what it finds there, so the corrupted run's top guess becomes `Y`
instead of `Q`.

To patch, the lab takes the clean run's residual at one position, right after one block, and pastes
it into the corrupted run at the same place. It then lets the corrupted run finish and checks how
much of the right answer came back. Each square in the patching grid stands for one experiment of
this kind, at one position and after one block.

### Keeping score: logit difference and recovery

Before a model turns its guesses into percentages, it gives every possible next character a raw
score, called a **logit**. A higher logit means the model ranks that character higher, and a logit
can be negative.

The lab scores each run with a **logit difference**: the logit of the right letter minus the logit
of the decoy `Y`, read at the last position. At period 8 the card writes it as
`logit(Q) − logit(Y)`, so a large positive value means `Q` is far ahead of `Y`. A negative value
means that `Y` has pulled ahead instead.

At period 8, the clean run scores 10.69, so `Q` is far ahead, while the corrupted run scores −1.01,
so `Y` is slightly ahead. The distance between those two scores, 11.70, is called the **gap**, and
it is the whole amount a patch could possibly win back.

**Recovery** says what share of the gap a patch wins back, using the rule
`recovery = (patched − corrupted) ÷ (clean − corrupted)`. Suppose a patch lifts the corrupted score
from −1.01 up to about 10.55. Because the score started below zero, the climb is 10.55 plus 1.01,
which comes to about 11.56. Dividing that climb by the full gap of 11.70 gives about 0.988, or
98.8%, which is the recovery you will find in one square of the lab's grid.

A recovery near 100% means that one pasted vector brought back almost all of the answer, while a
recovery near 0% means it changed almost nothing. A negative recovery means the patch pushed the
score even further in the wrong direction.

## Why it’s here

The previous lab found **features**, patterns inside a model that tend to show up alongside an idea
such as "this is a name", by noticing what goes along with what. This lab is where the course stops
describing and starts testing.

Picture a friend who wore the same pair of socks to every game their team won and now believes the
socks cause the wins. Watching more games can never settle it, because the socks and the wins keep
turning up together. The only fair test is to change one thing on purpose, play a game without the
socks, and see what happens.

Reading an attention map is like watching the games, so in this lab it runs into three problems
that only an **intervention** can get past. An intervention means changing something inside the
model on purpose and measuring what follows.

### Two stories can draw the same picture

At period 8, the letter an induction head should read is always exactly 7 positions back from the
letter doing the looking. From the last letter at position 23, for example, the letter to read is
the `Q` at position 16. A head that had learned to always look 7 back, whatever the letters are,
would draw the same picture and copy equally well.

Attention at period 8 cannot tell these two stories apart, but changing the period can. A new period
moves the right letter to a new distance, while a head stuck at 7 back would keep looking at the old
distance.

### Needed is not the same as doing the job

A head can be needed for copying without doing what the textbook story says, and a head can show a
neat pattern without the answer depending on it. Ablation measures whether the answer gets worse
without a part, which tells you the part is needed in this setting, but not what it calculates.

### Where the information actually travels

Attention shows where a head could pull information from, not where the information the answer
needs actually travels. Patching answers that second question, because it shows which single
position, after which block, carries the letter to copy.

## How to play with it

Work through the cards from top to bottom, because each one tests the picture that the one before it
draws. Start with **Repeat period** at 8, the setting the lab opens on, and keep it there until the
last step.

### Step 1: find an induction pattern

In **Copy task and attention**, the prompt appears as a row of letter chips. The `Q` at position 16
is outlined in green, because it is the previous occurrence of the next letter. Beside the row, a
`next` box shows the letter the pattern calls for, `Q?`, along with the model's top guess and how
much probability it gives that guess.

Of the three readouts underneath, **Copy accuracy** is the average probability the model gives the
right next letter, from the start of the second repeat to the end. **P(Q) at the end** is that
probability at the last letter only, and **Top 3 next letters** lists the model's three
highest-scoring guesses there.

The lab starts on head `L2 H3`, and the eight head buttons above the grid let you switch to any
other head. The grid shows the chosen head's attention across the whole prompt, with one row and one
column for each of the 24 letters. Each row is a letter doing the looking, each column is a letter
it can see, and darker purple means more attention.

In the Attention lab's words, the letter doing the looking is the **query** and a letter being
looked at is a **key**. Each row stops at its own letter, because a letter can look at itself and
at earlier letters but never at later ones.

Solid green outlines mark, in every row from the second repeat onward, the key an induction head
would read. An induction head does this lookup at every letter, not only the last one, which is why
the outlines run down the grid in a diagonal stripe.

The legend under the grid gives the rule as `i − 8 + 1`, where `i` is the row's position. Stepping
back 8, one whole block, lands on the same letter in the copy before, and stepping 1 forward lands
on the letter that came after it. For the last row, 23 − 8 + 1 = 16, which is the `Q` that follows
the earlier `P`.

Dashed amber outlines mark the previous letter, `i − 1`, which for the last row is position 22.
With `L2 H3` chosen, the dark cells should sit on the green outlines, and when you choose `L1 H4`,
they should jump to the dashed outlines instead.

The line under the grid names the single distance that takes the biggest share of the chosen head's
attention, so you can check the picture in words as well. Try `L1 H2` too and read where its
attention goes, because that head will matter later in the lesson.

The table beside the grid scores all eight heads at once. Its **previous letter** column is each
head's average attention on the letter immediately before, and its **after previous occurrence**
column is the average on the green key. Clicking a row in the table also picks that head for the
grid, which is a quick way to move between heads.

A chip in the corner checks the lab's own arithmetic against a standard program, ONNX Runtime,
running the same model file on the same prompt. It first reads `Weights loaded`, then `Matches ONNX`
followed by a tiny number, the largest difference the check found between the two.

The model's learned numbers are also called its weights. If they cannot load, the chip reads
`Weights unavailable · cached view`, and the grid falls back to a saved example at period 13.

### Step 2: ablate a head

In **Head ablation**, click `L1 H4` to switch it off, and its button changes from `on` to `off`. The
**Change** readout gives the difference in copy accuracy in percentage points, shown as `pts`, so a
fall from 90% to 10% would read as −80.0 pts.

Next to it, the readout **Attention on the induction key, L2 H2 · L2 H3** shows how much attention
the two induction heads put on the induction key. That key is the green-outlined letter from Step 1.
The readout gives
the values with every head on, then an arrow, then the values with your chosen heads off. That lets
you see whether switching off a layer-1 head disturbs the layer-2 heads.

Head switches add up, so click **Restore all** before each new head to keep only one head off at a
time. Restore, then switch off `L2 H3`, then restore again and switch off `L2 H1`. Each head's
button also shows an `alone` figure, the change in points when only that head is off, so you can
compare all eight heads at a glance. The **Ablate layer 1** and **Ablate layer 2** buttons switch off
a whole layer in one click.

With a layer-2 head off, the induction-key readout does not move at all, even when the head you
switched off is `L2 H3` itself. Switching a head off removes what it writes, not where it looks, and
heads in the same layer cannot affect each other. Only switching off a layer-1 head changes where the
layer-2 heads look.

The bars at the bottom show the probability of the right letter at every scored position, outlined
blue for the intact model and solid red with your chosen heads off. The scored positions are every
letter from the start of the second repeat on, the same letters that **Copy accuracy** averages. With
`L2 H3` off, the red bars collapse almost everywhere. With only `L2 H2` off, the losses are partial
and uneven, deep at some letters and small at others.

Finally, click **Restore all**, set **Ablation** to `Mean`, switch off `L2 H3` again, and compare what
the copying loses under `Mean` with what it lost under `Zero`.

### Step 3: patch activations

In **Activation patching**, the **Sweep** control picks where the grid's results come from. Keep it
on `Live · copy task`, which runs the patching on the copy puzzle in front of you, and keep
**Corrupt** on `The letter to copy`. The clean and corrupted prompts appear as two rows of chips, with the changed position
outlined in amber. The readouts give the logit difference for **Clean** and **Corrupted**, the
**Gap** between them, and each run's top letter.

The grid has two rows, `After block 1` and `After block 2`, and one column for each position. Green
squares bring the score back toward the clean run, red squares push it the other way, and the color
stops getting darker once it reaches 100%. The exact recovery for whichever square you pick appears
in the line under the grid.

Above the grid, the letter of the changed column, position 16, is highlighted in amber. Click the
square in the `After block 1` row under that column, which the lab opens with already selected, and
read its recovery. Then click the last square in the same row, position 23, and compare the two.

Then set **Corrupt** to `An unrelated letter`, which makes the lab change the `K` at position 4, a
letter the answer does not depend on. Read the **Gap** before anything else, and notice the
warning that appears whenever the gap is smaller than 1.

The **Sweep** control also offers two saved sweeps, `Cached · speaker` and `Cached · letters`, which
were worked out ahead of time and stored with the lab.

- `Cached · speaker` uses a short script in the style of the plays: `ROMEO: hello`, then
  `JULIET: hello`, then `ROMEO:`. In its corrupted prompt the last speaker is JULIET instead, cut to
  the clean prompt's length, so it ends `JULIET` with no colon.
- `Cached · letters` uses the same period-13 letter puzzle that the live sweep builds at period 13.

Both saved sweeps score each run by the right character's logit alone, so their **Clean**,
**Corrupted** and **Gap** numbers differ from the live sweep's. The recoveries in the
`Cached · letters` grid still come out almost the same as the live sweep's at period 13.

### Step 4: try an unseen period

The made-up repeating strings in the model's training covered almost every period from 4 to 31. A
few were skipped on purpose, including 11 and 13, so the lab marks those two as `unseen`.

**Try this:** Set **Repeat period** to `13 · unseen`, a length the model's repeated-string training
never used. Look again at **Copy accuracy**, the green outlines, and the `L2 H3` row of the table,
then go back to 8 and compare what moved with what stayed the same.

## What to notice

### The attention picture matches the textbook

At period 8, copy accuracy is 94.5%, which means that on average the model gives the right next
letter about a 19-in-20 chance. Across every period the lab offers, copy accuracy stays between
88.0% and 97.2%. That range includes the unseen periods, so the model still copies well at lengths
its training skipped.

The one weak spot is the guess made at the first letter of the second repeat. That is the very first
point where copying is possible, when the pattern has only just started to repeat. From the second
letter of that repeat onward, the average is at least 95% at every period.

The unseen periods still cost a little. Period 13 gives the lowest copy accuracy of any period,
88.0%, and period 11 gives the next lowest, while every other period scores 93.4% or more. Almost all
of that drop comes from the weak first letter, where the model gives the right letter under a 5%
chance at period 13. From the next letter on, its average is at least 95% there too.

Only two heads put much attention on the green key: `L2 H2` and `L2 H3`, with 0.66 and 0.67 at
period 8. No other head puts more than 0.10 there, which is a tenth of its attention. Meanwhile
`L1 H3` and `L1 H4` put 0.98 or more of their attention on the previous letter at every period.
Those four heads are the two halves of the textbook picture, found by looking alone.

### The heads follow the pattern, not a fixed distance

Picture a classmate who aces every practice quiz, and you start to wonder whether they learned the
topic or only noticed that the answer is always C. Watching them retake the same quiz cannot settle
it, because both habits give the same score. You need a new quiz where the right answers sit in
different places.

Changing the period gives the heads that kind of new quiz. At period 13 the green key moves to 12
back, so a head stuck at 7 back would keep its dark cells at 7 back, off the green outlines.
Instead, the distance that takes the largest share of attention for `L2 H2` and `L2 H3` is always
the period minus 1. So at every period, the outlines and the dark cells move together. At period 13,
`L2 H2` and `L2 H3` put 0.82 and 0.80 of their attention on the green key, 12 back.

Any period other than 8 already rules out a head stuck at 7 back. The unseen periods 11 and 13 go
further, because they rule out a head that only memorized one look-back distance for each period it
trained on. Passing both tests is strong evidence that these heads follow the letters, though it
does not prove that position plays no part at all.

At short periods the prompt holds several earlier copies, and the induction heads spread their
attention across them. Take period 6, where the two heads put 0.55 and 0.56 on the green key, the
letter after the nearest earlier copy. Counting the matching letter after every earlier copy, their
totals rise to 0.89 and 0.93.

### Single heads are needed

At period 8 the intact model scores 94.5%, and switching off one head at a time with Zero sorts the
eight heads into three groups:

- **Badly needed:** without `L1 H2` the score falls to 5.1%, without `L2 H3` to 6.0%, and without
  `L1 H4` to 9.9%.
- **Partly needed:** without `L2 H2` the score falls to 50.2%, and without `L1 H3` to 56.6%.
- **Hardly needed:** without `L1 H1` the score is still 89.2%, without `L2 H1` it is 95.8%, and
  without `L2 H4` it is 99.6%.

Switching off all of layer 1 leaves 1.9%, and switching off all of layer 2 leaves 0.4%, so the model
needs both layers to copy.

Notice that switching off `L2 H4`, and to a lesser degree `L2 H1`, raises the score above the intact
94.5%. So `L2 H4` is not needed for copying here, and it even gets slightly in the way. That does not
make it useless, because it may do a different job, such as helping with the Shakespeare text the
model also learned from, which this score never checks. An ablation only reports what happened to
one score on one prompt, not what a head is for.

### Layer 1 feeds layer 2, as far as ablation can tell

Switch off `L1 H4` and watch **Attention on the induction key, L2 H2 · L2 H3**. With every head on,
the two induction heads put 0.66 and 0.67 on the green key. With `L1 H4` off, those values fall to
0.19 and 0.24. With `L1 H3` and `L1 H4` both off, they fall to almost nothing, 0.01 and 0.03.

That is what the story predicts, because the layer-2 heads lose their aim when the layer-1 heads
stop writing their notes, but the story is not complete. Switching off `L1 H2` cuts the same
attention to 0.30 and 0.21, even though `L1 H2` puts nearly all its attention two letters back.
What `L1 H2` adds to the copying is not known from this lab.

### Zero exaggerates, and the layer-1 result holds up

Zero is a value the parts after a head never received from it during training, so it can make a head
look more important than it is. Each layer has one more step that runs after all four of its heads
have finished. So for a layer-2 head, the parts after it are that later step inside its own layer
and the final step that turns the model's numbers into a guess. Switch to `Mean` and switch off `L2 H3` alone, and the model keeps 47.9% instead of 6.0%. In the
same way, switching off `L2 H2` alone keeps 75.9% instead of 50.2%.

The layer-1 heads still matter under Mean: without `L1 H4` the score drops to 24.3%, and without
`L1 H2` it drops to 27.4%. Losing `L1 H3`, the least harmful of the three, still drops it to 48.8%,
about half the intact score.

So the two ways of switching off agree on which layer-1 heads matter, while they disagree about how
much each induction head matters alone. Under `Mean`, the two induction heads overlap: each does
part of the same copying on its own, so losing one leaves the other's share. With both of them off,
the score is 2.4%, close to the 1.3% that `Zero` gives.

Because the two heads sit in the same layer, neither can react to the other being switched off, so
this is overlap, not one head stepping in for the other.

Checks built into the lab's code repeat this comparison on random letter strings at periods 9 and
13, and they find the same split. You do not see those checks on screen.

### Patching finds one route

With **Corrupt** on `The letter to copy`, the `After block 1` square at position 16 recovers 98.8% of
the gap. Every other square in that row stays within 0.6 points of zero, including the last
position, which recovers −0.5%.

So after the first block, the letter to copy still sits at its own earlier position, where the
layer-2 heads can read it, not at the last position.

The corrupted run shows that the model copies whatever it finds there, because with `Y` planted at
position 16, its top guess becomes `Y`. At period 8 the planted `Y` gets 48% of the probability,
about half, and at period 13 it gets 99.5%, nearly all of it.

At period 8 an untouched `Q` still sits at position 8 for the heads to read, while at period 13 the
planted `Y` is the only earlier copy. That fits the idea that the heads read from every copy they
can find.

The gap is large at every period, between 6.05 and 22.35, so the patching results rest on a big,
clear difference between the two runs.

In the `After block 2` row, every square except the last reads exactly 0%, and the last reads 100%.
That pattern is built into the design, because after the last block no layer is left to move
information between positions. Only the last position's list can affect the guess at that point.

So pasting the clean list at any earlier position changes nothing, which reads 0%. Pasting it at the
last position hands the model the clean run's finished answer, which reads 100%. This row is a check
that the tool works, not a discovery.

### A control shows what a bad measurement looks like

A **control** is a run set up so that you already know what should happen, which lets you check the
measuring tool itself. Set **Corrupt** to `An unrelated letter`, and the model still copies
correctly, because the changed letter is not one it needs.

Because both runs now give nearly the same answer, the gap between their scores falls to 0.16 at
period 8, and the warning appears. Yet the grid still prints percentages, up to 43% in one square,
because recovery divides by that tiny gap. A shift of less than a tenth in the score, divided by
0.16, turns into a large-looking percentage like that.

Picture two runners who cross the finish line two hundredths of a second apart. If new shoes make
the slower one a hundredth of a second faster, someone could say the shoes "closed 50% of the gap".
That sounds impressive, yet a hundredth of a second is far too small to matter in a race.

The shift is far too small to tell you anything about how the model copies, and dividing by a tiny
gap is what makes it look big.

The unrelated letter's gap stays under 0.35 at every period, so this control never gives a usable
measurement. The lesson for any patching experiment is to check the gap before you read a single
square.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- The prompts are made-up capital letters from one fixed list, 24 letters long, with one prompt for
  each period. Checks built into the lab's code repeat the main results on random letter strings,
  but you do not see those on screen.
- Repeated capital letters are not a far-out test for this model, because made-up repeating strings
  were part of its training, even though two of the lab's periods were left out.
- Ablation switches off a whole head at a time, and patching swaps the whole residual at the end of
  a block. Neither tool here tests a single connection between two heads, and patching never swaps
  the output of one head on its own.
- The score is one logit difference against one decoy letter, `Y`, which was chosen before the
  experiment.
- Mean ablation uses one typical value per head, averaged over eight random repeats. Other ways of
  choosing the stand-in, such as a separate average for each position, could give different sizes.
- Nobody here has checked the wiring between the layers directly, which is the job of **path
  patching**. That method, described in Going deeper, swaps the signal along one single connection
  between two heads. The claim that layer 1 feeds layer 2 rests on the induction heads' attention falling
  when layer-1 heads are off, and `L1 H2` shows that other layer-1 heads matter too.
- This is one small model from one training run, and while a second training run also passed the
  same induction check offline, only this one is in the lab.
- A model with 1.4 million parameters says little about the huge models behind chatbots, which have
  far more layers and heads.
- The saved `Cached · speaker` sweep recovers 96% at the last position, but that result is close to
  built in. Its two prompts differ only in the last speaker's name, right at the end. The final six
  characters are `ROMEO:` in one and `JULIET` in the other, and the lab outlines all six in amber.
  The last of those six is the very character the guess is made from. Pasting the clean list back at
  that position restores what the model knows about that character, so a big recovery there could
  hardly fail to appear. It shows a pattern, not a discovery.

### Where the real method fails

**Switching a part off puts the model in a state it never met.** Zero is an arbitrary value, and
later parts may rely on a head's usual output as a steady background level. That is why, in their 2022
study of names, Wang and colleagues used mean ablation rather than zero. Research papers write this
team as Wang et al., where "et al." is Latin shorthand for "and others". A typical value reduces this
problem without removing it, as the big change in `L2 H3`'s result from Zero to Mean shows.

**Other parts can quietly take over:** picture a group project where one member is off sick and the
others cover their part without saying anything. A teacher who compares the final grade with and
without that member might decide the member contributed nothing.

Models can do something similar, called **self-repair**, where other parts make up for a part that
has been switched off. In 2023, McGrath and colleagues saw one layer make up for another. They called
this the Hydra effect, after a monster in Greek myth that grows new heads when one is cut off. Wang
and colleagues found backup heads that took over when the main ones were knocked out, so a small
effect does not prove a small role.

**A circuit may not travel:** one found for a single kind of prompt may not hold for a reworded
prompt, another model, or another training run.

**Patching says where, not who:** it shows where the needed information passes through, not which
head wrote it there.

**A score can be useless:** as the control showed, a score that barely changes between the two runs
turns every recovery into a meaningless number. So choose a score that clearly changes between the runs before you
start the experiment.

**A common mix-up:** "Every part that matters belongs to the circuit." Switching off `L1 H2` hurts
copying about as much as switching off `L1 H4`, yet `L1 H2` looks two letters back, not at the
previous letter. Being needed shows that the answer depends on a part, not that the part does the
job the story describes.

## Going deeper

### Sharper interventions

Ablation and patching each ask about one location at a time, while **path patching** swaps a single
connection between two parts. That separates "this head matters" from "this head matters because it
feeds that one". It is the missing test for this lab's claim that layer 1 feeds layer 2.

A full patching grid costs one complete run of the model for every square, which gets expensive in
large models. **Attribution patching** estimates the whole grid from two ordinary runs plus one extra
calculation that works backward through the model, trading some accuracy for speed.

### Making claims testable

**Causal scrubbing** turns an explanation into a list of changes that should not matter if the
explanation is right, and then tries every one of them. Changing the period in this lab is a small
version of that idea. The induction story says changing the period should move where the heads look
without breaking the copying, and the outlines and dark cells moved with the period as predicted.

Researchers now judge a circuit by three standards, and the first, **faithfulness**, asks whether the
claimed parts can produce the behavior on their own, without the rest of the model. Completeness asks
whether the circuit includes everything that matters, and minimality asks whether every part in it
is truly needed.

### Where the idea came from

In 2021, Elhage and colleagues showed that induction heads need at least two layers, with a
previous-token head feeding an induction head, as in this lab. The following year, Olsson and
colleagues argued that
induction heads may be behind much of **in-context learning**, a model's ability to pick up a
pattern from the prompt itself.

The best-known worked example of a circuit is the 2022 study of names by Wang and colleagues, on a model
called GPT-2 small. The researchers mapped 26 heads sorted into 7 groups and scored their circuit on
faithfulness, completeness and minimality. They said themselves that those scores also pointed to
gaps in their understanding.

More recent work, such as Anthropic's circuit tracing by Ameisen and colleagues in 2025, maps how
readable features connect rather than mapping raw heads. It builds those maps inside a stand-in
model that imitates the original but is built from features like the previous lab's, which makes it
easier to read.

**Steering** means turning one feature up or down by hand while the model writes. If turning up a
feature labeled "talks about the ocean" makes the model start talking about the ocean, the label has
passed a real test of cause. Checking that turning it down has the opposite effect makes the test
stronger still.

switches off and swaps.
