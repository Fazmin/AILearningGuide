## The big picture

Picture a friend reading a sentence to you one letter at a time. They pause before each new letter so you can guess it. If they stop at `the harbo`, you would bet on `r`. After a `q`, you would bet on `u`. You can do this because you have seen those letters follow each other many times.

In this module you build a tiny program that plays a simpler version of this guessing game. You build it starting from nothing at all. A program that guesses the next piece of text is called a **language model**. The big models behind chatbots do the same job at a huge size. Yours works one character at a time on a few hundred characters of text.

Your model starts **from scratch**. That means its numbers begin as meaningless starting values that carry no information about any text. Everything it can predict by the end has to come from the text you chose, and from nothing else.

The lab lays out a training run in four stages. You make the first three decisions, and the fourth is a report on them:

- **The text** is what the model learns from. You pick one of three short choices in the lab.
- **The size** is how many adjustable numbers the model gets to tune while it learns. Those numbers are called **weights**.
- **The recipe** covers how long training lasts and how big each learning step is. It also sets how many examples the model studies before each step.
- **The report** comes at the end, on a page called a **model card**. The lab fills it in to record what you built and how it scored.

### Where you have met it

The chatbots you may have used rest on language models trained through these same kinds of decisions. They use far more text and far more weights. The team behind Llama 2, a family of large language models, trained one version with 7 billion weights. It learned from 2 trillion tokens, and a token is a word or a piece of a word. That is far more text than a person could read in many lifetimes.

The Llama 2 recipe used ideas you will try in this lab. One is a step size that shrinks during training, and another is a limit on oversized steps. Real model cards exist too. They list much more than yours does. Examples are where the data came from and what the model is meant for.

### Why it matters

Without a training run there is no model at all. But a run can also go wrong in ways that are hard to spot. To see how a model is doing, you measure its error. That number is called the **loss**, and lower means better guessing. The loss on the model's own practice text flatters it, because it has already studied that text. So you need a second loss, measured on text it has never seen.

A recipe whose steps are too large can wreck the whole run. A recipe with too few steps stops well short of where it could get. This module also holds a surprise. With the lab's starting recipe, a smaller model beats a bigger one. Working out why will show you something. The number of weights a model has does not, on its own, decide how well it learns.

Finally, unless you write down what you did and how it scored, nobody else can read your numbers. They cannot tell what the numbers mean or whether they apply to anything they care about.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each idea below in your own words:

- How the text, the size and the recipe define a training run, and how each one changes the result.
- Why more weights do not automatically make a model fit its text better, and what decides the fit instead.
- What a model card promises about a model, and the much longer list of things it does not promise.
- How four recipe settings work as a team, and why text the model never saw judges the recipe.

Those four settings are:

- The **learning rate** is the size of each learning step.
- The **schedule** is the plan for changing that step size during the run.
- The **batch size** is how many examples the model studies before each step.
- The **gradient clip** is a limit on oversized steps.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab trains two real models from scratch every time you move a control. One is yours. The other is a plainer comparison model that stores its numbers in the simplest way. It is called the full table. Both are really trained, so every curve, loss and sample you see is computed, not drawn by hand.

You pick the text and slide the model's size up and down. You also choose how many times it reads through the text. Then you watch two losses fall as the model trains. One is measured on its practice text, and the other on text it never saw.

Along the way, you can read three samples of the model's writing, from before training to the very end. You watch random letters turn into rough word shapes. The **Training recipe** card lets you change how the model learns. You can even break a run on purpose to see how a safety limit contains the damage. The **Model card** then sums up your whole run, with both losses side by side.

A few things are fixed rather than computed. The three texts are fixed. So are the random choices that training makes, so the same settings always give the same result. The lab builds the messy "raw scrape" version from that text's own sentences plus made-up junk. It is not collected from the web.

## What it is

### The guessing game, as numbers

Your model reads one character and guesses the next one. That single character is all the context it ever gets. So at `the harbo` it bets using the `o` alone, not the whole word. That makes its game much narrower than the one you played above.

Its alphabet has 30 symbols: a space, the letters `a` to `z`, a full stop, and two angle brackets. Those two brackets are kept back for a later lab, which uses them as special markers. None of the three texts here contains them.

Every character in the practice text, together with the character right after it, makes one practice pair. One of the lab's three texts is Harbor weather notes. It has a sentence that begins `the morning fog`. That gives the pairs `t` then `h`, `h` then `e`, and `e` then a space. The pairs go on like this through the whole text. A model that learns from pairs of characters like this is called a **character bigram**, because "bi" means two. The **Model card** uses that name under Architecture.

In this lesson, the character the model is looking at is called the current character. For each of the 30 characters that could be current, the model keeps a row of 30 scores. There is one score for each character that could come next. A step called **softmax** turns each row of scores into chances that add up to 100 percent. The highest scores get the biggest chances.

For example, say a row had only three scores: 2, 1 and 0. Softmax would turn them into chances of about 67, 24 and 9 percent. A score can even be negative, and it still gets a chance, but only a small one.

After training, you would expect the row for `t` to give `h` a large share of the chances. That is because `th` turns up again and again in the practice text.

### Measuring surprise: the loss

To improve, the model needs a single number that says how badly it is doing. That number is the **loss** from the big picture. For each pair, the lab looks at the chance the model gave to the character that really came next. A big chance means little surprise, and a small chance means a lot. The loss is the average surprise over every pair, so lower is better.

The surprise for a chance of 1 in N is written ln N. Here ln is the natural logarithm key on a scientific calculator. You do not need to know how ln works inside. You only need to know how it behaves for a few chances:

- A sure thing, a chance of 1 in 1, gives a surprise of 0. There is nothing to be surprised by.
- A chance of 1 in 8 gives a surprise of about 2.08, which is a moderate amount.
- A chance of 1 in 30 gives a surprise of 3.401. A completely blind guess gets this here.

A chance that is not a whole "1 in N" works the same way. A 40 percent chance is 1 in 2.5, so its surprise is ln 2.5, which is about 0.92.

So the surprise climbs as the chance shrinks, but it climbs slowly. Going from 8 choices to 30 adds only about 1.3. The unit of this surprise is called the **nat**. That is why the chart measures loss in nats per token. A **token** is the piece of text a model reads at a time. Large models use words and pieces of words, but in this lab every token is one character.

Now picture a model that has learned nothing and spreads its bets evenly. It gives each of the 30 symbols a chance of 1 in 30. Its surprise is ln 30 = 3.401 on every character, so its loss is 3.401 as well. The chart draws this as a dotted line labelled `knows nothing`. That is the number to beat, because any model that has learned a pattern should land below it.

When a model's loss on a text is low, people say the model fits that text well. The lower the loss, the closer the fit.

### How training works

Training from scratch begins with those meaningless starting values. Then it repeats one small routine hundreds of times. First, the model takes a handful of practice pairs, called a **batch**, and measures its loss on them. At the default settings a batch holds 16 pairs. Then it works out how steeply the loss would rise or fall if each weight moved a little. That steepness is called the weight's **slope**, like the slope of a hill on a graph.

The full list of those slopes is called the **gradient**. It points the way that would raise the loss, so each weight steps the opposite way, downhill. This lesson calls that downhill direction the push. The **learning rate** sets how big each step is. Repeating this routine is called **gradient descent**. Each step heads downhill on the loss, the way a ball rolls toward the lowest point of a valley.

To work one push through, picture the weights for now as the 900 scores in the rows. The Model size section below shows a cheaper way to store them. Suppose a batch holds the pair `t` then `h`. The model gives `h` only a 10 percent chance after `t`.

The push then says to raise the score for `h` in `t`'s row by a lot. It also says to lower the other 29 scores in that row a little. Then `h` gets a bigger share next time. Each weight's part of the push says which way that weight should move, and how strongly.

One full pass through all the practice pairs is called an **epoch**. The default run makes 20 of them. The pairs are shuffled into batches, and the pushes are really calculated, not made up. The whole run starts again from the beginning each time you move a control. The model is so small that this takes only milliseconds, which are thousandths of a second.

### The four choices

The first card, **Build a model in four choices**, lays the run out as a strip of four stages. Each stage stands for one part of the run:

- **Corpus** is the text the model reads. A corpus is the usual name for a body of text used for training.
- **Size** is how many weights the model gets. You set it with the **Model size** slider.
- **Recipe** is how the model trains. You set it with **Epochs** and the **Training recipe** card.
- **Model card** is the report the lab fills in on what you built. It is a summary of the other three, not a fourth choice.

Clicking a stage only moves the `Choice N of 4` marker at the top of the card. No choice is locked behind another. Every control stays live the whole time, so you can change any choice in any order you like.

### What Model size does

The most direct way to store the scores is a full grid. It has a row for each current character and a column for each next character. That grid holds 30 × 30 = 900 numbers, and this lab calls it the **full table**. Each of its rows is completely separate, so learning something about `t` changes nothing in the row for `s`.

Picture a coach who rates how well each of 30 players suits each of 30 positions on a team. One way is to write all 900 ratings by hand, one per pairing. The ratings have no link between them. That is the full table.

A cheaper way is to give each player a short stats card. It holds one number for speed and one for strength. Each position then gets a matching card that says how much speed and strength it needs.

To rate a player for a position, the coach multiplies the matching numbers on the two cards. Then the coach adds up the results. **Model size** swaps the full table for this cheaper version. Each character, as the current one, gets a short list of r numbers. Each possible next character gets a matching list of r numbers. The score for a pair is the two lists multiplied number by number, then added up.

Storing a big grid as two thin sets of lists that multiply together like this is called a **factorization**. The length of each list, r, is called the **rank**. It is the number that the **Model size** slider sets.

Here is one score worked through at rank 2, using made-up numbers. Say the list for `t`, as the current character, is 2 and −1. The list for `h`, as a possible next character, is 1.5 and 0.5. Multiply the matching numbers and add them up. That gives 2 × 1.5 plus −1 × 0.5, which comes to 3 − 0.5 = 2.5. So 2.5 is the score for `h` after `t`.

Counting the weights uses the same kind of arithmetic. Each of the 30 characters gets r numbers on the current side and r more on the next side. So the model stores 2 × r × 30 weights in all. At the default rank of 8, that is 2 × 8 × 30 = 480.

The slider shows this as `rank 8 · 480 weights`. The other sizes you will try in this lesson follow the same rule:

- Rank 1 stores 60 weights, which makes it the smallest model the slider allows.
- Rank 4 stores 240 weights, which is about a quarter of the full table's size.
- Rank 12 stores 720 weights, which makes it the largest model the slider allows.

The lab trains your factored model and the full table side by side. Both models use the same text and the same recipe. So the only difference between them is how the weights are stored. On the chart, the thick curves are your model and the thin curves are the full table.

The coach picture stops matching in one important way. A coach chooses what each stat means, but nobody chooses what the model's r numbers mean. They end up wherever training happens to leave them. So you usually could not put a name such as "speed" on any one of them.

### Capacity: the ceiling that rank sets

How much pattern a model can represent at all, however long it trains, is called its **capacity**. The rank puts a hard ceiling on it. Rank 1 shows that ceiling most clearly. Each list then holds a single number, so every score is one number multiplied by another.

Take a small case with only three possible next characters. The next-side numbers are 3 for `h`, 2 for `e` and 1 for `u`. A current character whose number is 2 gets scores of 6, 4 and 2, so `h` comes first. One whose number is 0.5 gets 1.5, 1 and 0.5, which is the same order but flatter. One whose number is −1 gets −3, −2 and −1, so the order flips and `u` comes first.

So at rank 1, every row follows one shared order of next characters, or its exact reverse. Each row's favourite must be either the top or the bottom of that order. That means there can only ever be two different favourites. A rank 1 model can never expect `u` after `q`, `h` after `t` and `t` after a space together. That needs three favourites. Real text needs far more than three.

In the coach picture, that is like rating everyone on speed alone. Every player gets the same order of positions, only more or less extreme. A player whose number is negative gets that order flipped end to end. That limit is built into the model's shape, so extra training cannot remove it.

### What the recipe changes

The size decides what the model could represent. The recipe decides how training gets there, much like a set of cooking instructions. Say you have the same ingredients and the same pan. How hot you cook, for how long and how much at a time can change the dish completely.

The **Training recipe** card holds the settings that the earlier labs kept fixed, now turned into controls. The cooking picture stops matching at one point. A cook can taste the dish, but a model's loss on its own practice text is a poor guide. You judge a recipe mainly by the held-out loss, which the Why it’s here section explains.

The **training recipe**, then, is everything about the run apart from the model's shape. It covers which version of the text the model reads and how many epochs it runs. It also covers the learning rate and how it changes. The batch size, the gradient clip and a fixed seed belong to it too. Each of these gets its own part below.

### The training text

A program that copies text off web pages is called a scraper. The pile of text it collects is called a scrape. A scrape picks up junk along with the writing you wanted. One example is the repeated notices at the bottom of every page, which are called **boilerplate**. The **Training text** switch chooses between a `Raw scrape`, junk included, and a `Cleaned scrape`, which is the default.

The lab builds the raw scrape from the corpus's own training sentences. It slots extra lines in at fixed places:

- **Footer lines** are notices from page bottoms, such as `click here to subscribe to our newsletter.`. Harbor gets three of these, and Proverbs gets four because it has one more training sentence.
- **Exact repeats** are two sentences that already appeared earlier in the scrape.
- **A misspelled copy** is one sentence copy with a spelling mistake, such as `cllouds` in place of `clouds`.
- **Tiny fragments** are two short lines, `ok.` and `yes.`, which carry almost no useful text at all.

Cleaning uses the stages from *Building a dataset*, in order. It drops lines with a boilerplate phrase and lines under 12 characters. Then it drops exact repeats and near-repeats such as the misspelled copy. For Harbor weather notes, the raw scrape has 14 lines and 696 characters. Cleaning removes 8 of those lines:

- **Boilerplate:** The 3 footer lines go first.
- **Too short:** The 2 fragments go next.
- **Exact repeats:** The 2 lines that repeat an earlier line exactly go after that.
- **Near-repeat:** The 1 misspelled copy is the last to go.

That leaves 6 lines and 379 characters, and those 6 lines are exactly the original training sentences. So the `Cleaned scrape` is the very same text that this lab has always trained on.

### Learning rate and schedule

**Learning rate** sets how far each weight moves for a given push. Its slider has eight notches, and each notch doubles the one before. The notches run from 0.125 up to 16, and the default is 0.5. For the same push, a rate of 4 moves every weight eight times as far as the default.

**Schedule** changes the step size over the course of the run. Its four buttons give four different shapes:

- `Constant` keeps the same step size from the first batch of the run to the last.
- `Cosine decay` starts at full size and eases down along the smooth curve that gives it its name. It ends at 3 percent of where it began.
- `Warmup + decay` starts near zero and ramps up to full size over the first 15 percent of the steps. Then it shrinks in a straight line to 5 percent of full size.
- `One cycle` also warms up to full size. Then it eases down along a smooth curve until it ends below where it began.

Every schedule works by multiplying the learning rate. The rate you set is the peak, and the schedule decides how much of that peak each step uses. Say the rate is 4 and the schedule is `Cosine decay`. The first step uses 4, and the last uses about 0.12, which is 3 percent of 4. A plan like this for the step size across a run is called a **learning-rate schedule**.

### Batch size

**Batch size** is how many pairs the model studies before each update. It runs from 2 up to 64, and the default is 16. The card shows the number of updates as **Steps**. To find it, divide the number of pairs by the batch size, round up, and then multiply by **Epochs**.

Harbor's training text gives 378 pairs, shown at the top of the **Training progress and samples** card. The default run turns them into steps like this:

- Divide 378 pairs by 16 pairs per batch. That gives 23.6, which rounds up to 24 batches in each epoch.
- Multiply 24 batches by 20 epochs. That gives 480 steps, which is the number the **Steps** readout shows.

### Gradient clip

The push has an overall size. That is one number for how strong it is across all the weights together. To find it, square each weight's part of the push, add up the squares, and take the square root. That size is called the **gradient norm**. The **Peak gradient norm** readout shows the largest one in the whole run, measured before any clipping happens.

Putting a limit on that size is called **gradient clipping**, and the **Gradient clip** control sets the limit. Whenever a push is bigger than the limit, the lab shrinks every part of it by the same fraction. Its size then drops to exactly the limit.

Here is a small example with only two weights. Say a push is 0.6 on one weight and 0.8 on the other. Its size is the square root of 0.36 + 0.64, which is 1. Now the clip is at 0.50. The lab halves both parts to 0.3 and 0.4, so the size drops to 0.5.

The two weights still move in the same proportion to each other. That is what people mean when they say clipping keeps the push's direction. A push whose size is already under the limit, such as 0.3, passes through untouched.

The clip is off by default and can go up to 1.50. The **Clipped steps** readout counts how many steps it had to shrink.

### The seed, and sharing your recipe

Training your factored model uses random numbers twice. It uses them once for its starting values and once for shuffling the pairs into batches. The full table needs them only for shuffling, because it starts with every number at zero. A **seed** is a number that fixes those random choices, so the same recipe always gives the same result. This lab always uses seed 9.

Your recipe is saved in the lab's state. The **Copy a link to this state** button in the header shares it with the rest of your work. **Copy my recipe** writes every setting out as one line of text that starts with `tiny-lm-recipe v1`. **Load this recipe** reads a line like that back in.

### The model card

The last stage is a report rather than a choice. The **Model card** writes down the run that the other three stages defined. It lists the corpus and how it was split, the architecture and the recipe. Then come the **Training loss** and the **Held-out loss**, along with a number called perplexity. The What to notice section explains that number.

Its last row gives the full table's two losses for comparison. Under the rows sit three readouts, **Train**, **Held out** and **Gap**, which the next section explains.

## Why it’s here

### The flattering loss and the honest one

Picture a friend who gets ready for a test by memorising the answer key to the practice test. They score 100 percent every time they retake the practice test. Yet that says little about how they will do on the real test, which asks new questions.

Training loss has the same weakness. It measures the model on text it has already studied many times over. So the lab holds back about the last quarter of each corpus's sentences. These never enter a single training step, and the lab measures the model's loss on them as well. That second loss is the **held-out loss**. It is the honest one, because it shows how well the model predicts text it has never seen.

For Harbor weather notes, the held-out text is the final two sentences of the corpus. They come to 119 characters, against 379 for training:

- `in summer the harbor fills with sails and the water stays warm.` is the first held-out sentence.
- `the fog returns in the morning and the pattern repeats.` is the second held-out sentence.

How well a pattern learned from one text carries over to new text is called generalization. The chart draws both losses after every epoch. A solid line shows training and a dashed line shows held out. It shades the space between them. The size of that space is the **generalization gap**, which is the held-out loss minus the training loss.

The **Gap** readout on the **Model card** shows that difference at the end of the run. The chart prints it beside the curves too. The readout subtracts the losses before they are rounded to three decimals. So your own subtraction can differ from it by 0.001.

Extra capacity usually lowers the training loss. The gap shows how much of that gain fails to carry over to new text. Training loss alone never shows that.

### Two ideas that usually travel together

The number of weights, often called the parameter count, usually rises together with capacity. So people tend to treat them as one idea. They are not the same thing. A rank 15 model would store 900 weights, the same as the full table. Yet every row of its scores would still be a mix of only 15 shared patterns. So it could build only some of the grids the full table can.

This lab adds a second surprise, which What to notice unpacks. Even the model with more weights and more capacity can end up fitting worse after a short run.

### The run that is yours

From here on, every lab in the track starts from weights that somebody else trained, and you adapt them. This is the one run you own from start to finish. The model card is the claim you have to stand behind afterwards.

A model card works a bit like the label on a food packet. It lists what went in and a few measured numbers. But it does not tell you whether the food tastes good or suits your plans. The comparison stops there. A food label's numbers hold for every packet. A model card's losses hold only for the text and recipe it names.

### Where the first half of the track meets

*Building a dataset* asked which text is worth training on. *Hyperparameters & schedules* asked how to spend each step. In this lab both questions land in one run. The **Held out** readout judges the recipe, but it barely tells raw text from cleaned text. The difference cleaning makes shows up somewhere else. It shows up in **Footer lines**. This readout on the **Training recipe** card gives the model's loss on the footer junk.

## How to play with it

Start with every control at its default. That means Harbor weather notes, **Model size** at rank 8, **Epochs** at 20, and the **Training recipe** card untouched.

### Compare the three texts

Click each of the three corpus buttons and read the character count on it. That count is the size of the whole corpus. Harbor weather notes has 499 characters, roughly one short paragraph of a school book. The other two are shorter still.

The **Model card** then shows how the text splits. For Harbor that is 379 characters for training and 119 held out. The one character left over is the space where the two parts meet.

Notice how little text that is. Every pattern the model ends up with has to come from those few sentences. Each button also describes its corpus. Harbor is repetitive description. Recipe steps is a list of cooking instructions. It has nothing to do with the training recipe apart from the word.

The Proverbs button calls it short unrelated sentences that are hard to fit. You will see below what that means here. The text is small enough to memorise. So its training loss can fall low while its held-out loss stays high.

### Walk Model size up

Leave **Epochs** at 20 and move **Model size** from 1 up to 12. Stop at these ranks to read the **Model card** each time:

- At rank 1, the training loss ends at 2.925 and the held-out loss ends at 3.025.
- Rank 4 brings the training loss down to 2.208 and the held-out loss down to 2.246.
- Rank 8 brings them lower again, to 2.039 for training and 2.117 for held out.
- Rank 12 barely changes the training loss, at 2.038, but the held-out loss rises to 2.165.

Now read the thin lines. They belong to the 900-weight full table and end at 2.283 for training and 2.302 held out. With this recipe, the rank 4 model has 240 weights. It already beats a table almost four times bigger. The boxed note under the two sliders on the **Size and epochs** card explains why. This lesson calls that note the callout.

### Hit the wall at rank 1

**Try this:** put **Model size** back to 1 and drag **Epochs** all the way to 30. The training loss ends at 2.852, and the callout reports that the full table is ahead by 0.688 nats. Look at the thick solid curve, too. From about epoch 10 to epoch 22 it hardly moves, staying between about 2.92 and 2.96.

Now set **Model size** to 8. Its training curve drops below 2.852 by epoch 4. Even the slow full table passes that mark by epoch 5. Changing the learning rate or the schedule on the **Training recipe** card does not rescue rank 1 either. With or without the clip, it never gets within a third of a nat of the full table. That is a wall, not a slow start.

### Read the samples

Below the chart on the **Training progress and samples** card sit three samples, labelled A, B and C. Each one is also marked on the solid curve at the point where it was written. A **sample** is text that the model generates one character at a time, starting after `the` and a space.

The model only uses that last space, and the sample shows only the characters it adds. Each next character is picked at random, and the likelier characters are picked more often. The samples use a fixed seed of their own. They come out the same each time for the same weights.

Sample A comes from before any training, B from halfway through, and C from the end. Each one shows the loss the model had at that moment. At the defaults, sample A is letter soup. The untrained model sits at a loss of 3.40, right on the `knows nothing` line. By sample C, at a loss of 2.04, you can see word-shaped pieces such as `the`. They sit in a stream that is still not real English.

Moving **Model size** changes all three samples. They come from your factored model, not the full table.

### Watch overfitting happen

Pick **Proverbs**, set **Model size** to 12 and **Epochs** to 30. Then watch the dashed held-out line. It falls for a while, bottoms out, and then turns back up. The solid training line keeps going down the whole time. The What to notice section explains what that turn means.

### Set the recipe

Go back to Harbor weather notes, rank 8 and 20 epochs. Then open the **Training recipe** card with its defaults. There, **Held out** reads 2.117 and **Peak gradient norm** reads 0.95. From that starting point you can work through these comparisons one at a time:

1. Raise **Learning rate** to 4 with **Schedule** on `Constant`. **Held out** jumps to 3.016, which is much worse than before.
2. Switch **Schedule** to `Cosine decay` at the same rate. **Held out** falls to 2.054, which is better than the default.
3. Return the rate to 0.5 and compare the two schedules again. The order flips, with `Constant` at 2.117 and `Cosine decay` at 2.251.

### Break the run, then rescue it

Set **Learning rate** to 8 with **Schedule** on `Constant`. The run halts at step 154 with a warning that it diverged. Diverging means the weights grew so large that the computer could not store the loss. Training could not go on.

Now turn **Gradient clip** up to 0.50, and the same run finishes, with 459 of its 480 steps clipped. It ends with a held-out loss of 3.089. That is far worse than the default's 2.117, but it is at least a real number.

### Change the batch size

Return the rate to 0.5 and the clip to off. Then set **Batch size** to 8, 16 and 64 in turn while you watch **Steps** and **Held out**:

- Batch 8 gives 960 steps, and its held-out loss ends at 2.089.
- Batch 16 gives 480 steps, and its held-out loss ends at 2.117.
- Batch 64 gives only 120 steps, and its held-out loss ends clearly highest, at 2.618.

### Feed it junk

Last, note **Held out** and **Footer lines** with `Cleaned scrape`. Then switch **Training text** to `Raw scrape` and see how much each one changes. The **Footer lines** readout is the model's loss on the three footer lines. A low number there means the model has learned to predict the junk.

### Save your recipe

When you find a recipe you like, press **Copy my recipe**. Paste the line somewhere safe, or send it to a friend. They can paste it into the box labelled `Load a recipe someone sent you` and press **Load this recipe**. That sets up exactly the same run.

## What to notice

### The smaller model wins, and sharing is the reason

At the default recipe, the full table only updates the row for the current character. So each practice pair moves 30 weights. In the factored model, the same pair moves that character's own list of r numbers. It also moves the whole next side, which every character shares. At rank 8 that is 8 + 240 = 248 weights moved by a single pair.

The letter `q` shows how uneven this gets. It appears only once in Harbor's training text, in the word `quiet`. So the full table's `q` row learns from that one pair in each pass. Over 20 epochs that row gets only 20 updates. Every weight on the shared side of the factored model is updated on every one of the 480 steps.

In the coach picture, one game shows that player `t` suits position `h`. In the full table, that changes only `t`'s own 30 ratings. With stats cards, the coach updates `t`'s card and also every position's card. Those position cards are used to rate every other player too. With only 378 pairs to learn from, that sharing gets far more out of each example.

### More weights is not more fitting power

The full table has more weights than any rank 8 model, and more capacity too. Every grid that a rank 8 model can build is also a possible 900-number grid. Yet after 20 epochs at the default recipe, the full table fits worse.

Capacity is only a ceiling. It is the most a model could ever fit with unlimited training. The full table has the higher ceiling. But after 20 epochs it is still far below it, because each of its rows gets so little practice. So a parameter count tells you about the ceiling at best. It does not say how close a run gets to it.

You can check that the full table was short of practice rather than capacity. At a rate of 4 on `Constant`, its training loss in the Model card's comparison row reaches 1.872. That is far below the 2.039 your rank 8 model reaches at the default.

### Read the Gap as a trend

The **Gap** tells the other half of the story. The full table has the smallest gap, only 0.019. The next part explains why that is not the win it looks like. Among the factored models, the gap tends to grow with rank past rank 4. But it grows in a bumpy way:

- At rank 4, the gap is 0.038, so the two curves almost touch.
- At rank 6, the gap jumps to 0.095, which is more than twice as wide.
- At rank 8, the gap dips back down to 0.077.
- At rank 12, the gap reaches 0.128, the widest of these four.

The held-out text is only 119 characters, which is two sentences. So the held-out loss can shift by a few hundredths through small accidents. One example is which letters happen to land in those sentences. Random ups and downs like these are called **noise**.

Comparing rank 6 with rank 8 here is like judging two basketball players by two free throws each. A single step such as rank 6 to rank 8 is mostly noise. So it is the overall trend that counts.

### A small gap can still be bad news

A gap only means something when you read it next to the loss itself. At the very start of training, the two curves begin at the same point, a gap of about 0.001. Yet the model knows nothing.

The full table's gap of 0.019 is small for a similar reason. Its training loss of 2.283 is worse than any rank from 4 up. So it has fit its practice text too loosely to have much to lose on new text. Train it at a rate of 8 on `Constant`. The Model card's comparison row then reads 1.853 for training and 2.066 held out, a gap of 0.213.

A loss that stays high is called **underfitting**. It happens when the model cannot represent the pattern, or has not trained long enough. The full table at the default recipe shows the second kind, because bigger steps let it fit much better.

Rank 1 shows the first kind. Its gap of 0.100 is no comfort. Its training loss of 2.925 is not far below the 3.401 of a model that learned nothing. However you train it, it stays well behind the full table. When a model is stuck like this, more capacity would help and more steps would not.

### Overfitting on Proverbs

On **Proverbs** at rank 12 and 30 epochs, you can watch the opposite failure happen. The dashed held-out line bottoms out at 2.668 around epoch 18, then climbs back to 2.818. Meanwhile the solid training line keeps dropping, all the way down to 1.865.

Past epoch 18, extra practice made the model worse at new text. It was fitting quirks of its training sentences that new sentences do not share. Fitting the training text more closely than new text of the same kind is called **overfitting**.

Picture the friend with the answer key again. This time they have memorised it so firmly that they write the practice answers down on new questions too. The model does the same, making confident bets on patterns from its training sentences. Each confident wrong bet costs a lot of surprise on new text, so the held-out loss rises.

### Enough, then too much

Rank 8 and rank 12 train to almost the same place, 2.039 against 2.038. Rank 12 does no better on the held-out text. Its 2.165 against rank 8's 2.117 is a difference of 0.048, which is inside the noise. At 30 epochs the two swap places.

So the extra 240 weights bought nothing here. That is the shape to look for when you size a model. More capacity helps only until the model has enough. For a clear case of too much capacity costing you on new text, look back at Proverbs.

### Perplexity turns the loss into a count

The **Model card** shows **perplexity** beside the held-out loss. It turns the loss back into a number of choices. At the defaults, the held-out loss of 2.117 becomes a perplexity of 8.31. That means the model is about as unsure as if it were picking among 8 equally likely characters. That is a big drop from the 30 it started with.

On a scientific calculator, the eˣ key undoes ln, and e to the power 2.117 is about 8.31. Check this against the surprise scale from earlier. There a 1 in 8 chance gave a surprise of about 2.08. By the same logic, a model that learned nothing would have a perplexity of exactly 30.

### Samples follow the loss

The samples improve as the loss falls. They go from letter soup before training to word-shaped pieces such as `the`. Even then the stream is not fluent. A sample shows you what a loss number looks like as text. But it measures nothing, so never judge a run by its samples alone.

### Rank 15 is where the saving ends

Past rank 15, the factored model saves nothing at all. Then 2 × 15 × 30 is already 900, the size of the full table. **Model size** stops at 12, so you cannot reach that point in this lab.

### The best learning rate depends on the schedule

Picture adjusting the hot tap in a shower that is slow to respond. Say you make big turns the whole time. You keep overshooting, too hot and then too cold, and you never settle. Now say you start with big turns and switch to tiny ones near the end. You get close quickly and then settle on the right temperature.

A learning rate of 4 behaves like those big turns. On `Constant`, every step stays large right to the end. The run keeps overshooting and finishes at 3.016 held out. Under `Cosine decay`, the same peak reaches 2.054, which is better than the default's 2.117. The decay hands back small steps for the end of the run.

At a rate of 0.5, though, no step is too big to begin with. Shrinking the steps only wastes the later part of the run. That is why `Cosine decay` ends worse there, at 2.251 against 2.117 for `Constant`. A schedule only makes sense when you judge it together with the rate it multiplies.

### Why only the factored model can blow up

The full table cannot explode, because of how its push is built. For one pair, the push lowers each character's score by the chance the model gave it. The one exception is the character that really came next. Its score is raised by 1 minus its chance.

The worst case is a model that is 100 percent sure of a wrong character. Then the wrong character drops by 1 and the right one rises by 1. The rest do not move. The size is the square root of 1 + 1, which is about 1.41. Every other case is smaller, and averaging over a batch cannot make it bigger.

So at a learning rate of 8 on `Constant`, the full table trains normally to 2.066 held out. Its biggest push has a size of only 0.34.

The factored model is different, because it multiplies two sets of numbers together. The push on each side grows with the size of the other side. A big step can make one side larger, which makes the next push on the other side larger. That makes the next step larger still.

On `Constant` at a rate of 8, the factored run halts before the end. So this comparison uses `Cosine decay`, where the run does finish. With no clip, its **Peak gradient norm** reaches about `1.2e+12`, which is calculator shorthand for 1.2 trillion.

The run ends at a held-out loss of 22.245. That is far worse than the 3.401 of a model that learned nothing. With a clip of 0.50, the same run ends at 2.065. That even beats the default, because `Cosine decay` shrinks the steps late in the run. The clip held the early, oversized steps in check, and the schedule did the rest.

### Clipping contains a bad rate without fixing it

On `Constant` the clip gets no such help. At a rate of 8 it lets the run finish. But the run ends at 3.089 held out, against the default's 2.117. The steps are shorter, but they are not better aimed. The rate is still too large for every step of the run. Clipping is a safety limit that stops one oversized step from wrecking the weights. It is not a cure for a learning rate that is wrong.

### Batch size trades steps against noise

Picture two teachers marking the same pile of homework over the same number of weeks. One gives feedback after every 8 questions. The other waits until 64 questions are done. The second teacher's feedback is based on more work each time, so it is steadier. But the student hears it far less often.

Batch size works the same way when **Epochs** is fixed. A bigger batch averages over more pairs and smooths out the random wobble in each push. The catch is that bigger batches leave fewer updates. Batch 64 makes only 120 of them and ends clearly highest, at 2.618.

Batch 8 makes 960 updates and ends at 2.089, which is within noise of batch 16. So the clear lesson is at the large end. On this text, with the epochs fixed, too few updates cost more than steadier pushes gain.

### Junk text: the footers notice it, the held-out loss does not

The raw scrape adds steps, 880 against 480, because it has more pairs in each pass. It also lowers **Footer lines** from 3.090 to 2.509. This shows that the model has learned to predict the footer junk. Yet **Held out** barely moves, 2.105 against 2.117. With other seeds the difference sometimes goes the other way, although this lab keeps the seed fixed at 9.

So the held-out loss alone would never tell you to clean the text. The **Footer lines** loss does. In this lab, cleaning keeps junk out of the model and saves training steps. It does not lower the held-out loss.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Nothing in the training is faked. The pushes, all four curves, the held-out losses and the samples are all worked out live. The one made-up part is the raw scrape, described below. Each point on a curve is the loss of the weights saved at that epoch. It is measured on the whole split, not on one noisy batch.

What is missing is size and context. The model sees exactly one character back, so `t` predicts the same thing in `the` as in `it`. A real model has many layers, which are stages that each rework every token's list of numbers. This model has none. It has no attention either. Attention is the part of a real model that lets it look back across many earlier tokens.

Nothing sits between the two halves of the factored model, the current-character lists and the next-character lists. A real model does its main work in the layers between them.

The held-out text is only 119 characters, so treat any difference under about 0.05 nats as noise. It also comes from the end of the document, not from random picks. So it may not be typical of the rest.

The three samples come from the factored model you sized, so they change when you move **Model size**. The thin curves belong to the full table, which has no samples of its own. **Epochs** stops at 30 and **Model size** stops at 12. The three corpora are fixed, so you cannot paste in your own text.

The scrape is synthetic. The lab made it up from the corpus's own sentences, with fixed junk slotted in. It did not collect it from real web pages.

The step method is the plainest kind, called stochastic gradient descent or SGD. Here "stochastic" means random, because each batch is a shuffled handful of pairs. Real models use step methods with momentum, which carries part of each step into the next. They also give each weight its own step size. So a rate of 0.5 here and a rate of 0.0003 in a real model are on different scales. You cannot compare them, as Toy versus real explains.

The clip shrinks the push on both halves of the factored model together. Only the factored model can explode. Every recipe result comes from one seed on about 380 characters. So differences under about 0.05 nats are noise there too. Weight decay, a real-world extra described below, is not offered. The warmup always takes 15 percent of the steps.

The callout on the **Size and epochs** card is written with the default recipe in mind. Raise the rate to 2 or 4 on `Constant` and the full table pulls ahead. The callout then blames capacity. There the factored model is really overshooting. The same rank 8 reaches a lower loss at the default rate.

### Where the real mechanism fails

"Smaller fits better" is a real result, but it depends on this setup. The setup uses plain SGD, 379 characters of training text, and a full table whose rows share nothing. It also depends on the default learning rate and a short run. On `Constant` at a rate of 2 or more, the full table pulls ahead. Bigger steps let its rarely updated rows catch up, while the factored model starts to overshoot.

Step methods that give each weight its own step size also shrink that advantage. They give weights that rarely get practice, such as the `q` row, bigger steps to make up for it. This lab has no switch for them.

Scaling laws are patterns that describe how loss falls as models and data grow. With real amounts of data, bigger models usually do better. Scaling laws say to grow the weights and the text together, rather than pushing either one alone.

A held-out loss is only honest while the split is honest. Suppose near-copies of one sentence land in both the training text and the test text. Then the model has partly seen the test already, so its loss looks better than it deserves. That is one more reason the near-repeat stage in cleaning matters.

A real recipe also leans on tools that this lab leaves out. One is a step method that gives each weight its own step size. Another is weight decay, which gently shrinks every weight a little at each step. This stops any weight from growing too large. Real runs also use mixed precision. It does some of the arithmetic with shorter, less exact numbers to save time and memory.

**A common mix-up:** a low loss is not a capable model. Loss only says how well the model predicts the next character on this kind of text. It promises nothing about following instructions, refusing what it should refuse, citing sources or getting facts right.

A model card records which model was trained on which text, with which recipe, and how it scored. It is a statement of scope, not a quality rating. Its job is to let someone else judge whether your number applies to their problem.

## Going deeper

### The same two halves sit inside real models

The two thin halves of your factored model are not a toy shortcut. Every real language model has these same two pieces at its two ends. One turns each token into a list of numbers. The other scores every possible next token against that list. These two pieces are usually called the embedding and the unembedding.

In a real model each list is thousands of numbers long. Much of the interesting work happens in the many layers stacked between the two ends. Your model has no layers there, and no attention to look further back. It only ever sees one character, which is why it can only learn which character tends to follow which.

### Two reasons the small model wins

The first reason is uneven practice, which the letter `q` showed in What to notice. A rare character's row in the full table barely gets updated. The shared side of the factored model learns from every step. What matters is how much practice each weight gets, not how many weights there are.

The second reason is that the factorization is a constraint. It forces characters to share structure whether the text calls for it or not. That keeps the factored model from bending each row to fit its few training pairs exactly. This helps it on new text.

The full table's tiny gap at the default recipe has a different cause, as you saw earlier. It learns so slowly that it fits its training text only loosely. So it has little to lose on new text. Once it trains harder, its gap grows.

### What real model cards add

Real model cards go much further than this one. They record where the data came from, under what licence, and the exact recipe. They also list which tests ran on which version. They name the failure modes people already know about and what the model is meant for.

None of that is a quality rating either. A card describes what a model covers, not how good it is. Your card does the smallest version of the same job. It reports two losses that disagree instead of only the one that looks good.

### Toy versus real

**Learning rates do not carry over** from this lab to real models. This lab uses plain SGD with rates from 0.125 to 16. The Llama 2 paper trained its 7 billion and 13 billion weight models with a method called AdamW. It rescales each weight's own step.

The peak learning rate for those Llama 2 models was 0.0003. The paper writes it in scientific notation as 3.0 × 10⁻⁴. The Llama 3 paper lists the same rate for its 8 billion weight model. AdamW gives every weight its own step size, so its rates and this lab's rates cannot be compared.

**The same three ideas show up at full size** in Llama 2. It used a schedule that warmed up over 2,000 steps. Then it followed a cosine curve down to 10 percent of the peak. It also used weight decay of 0.1 and clipped its gradients at 1.0. Cosine decay, warmup and a clip are three of this lab's recipe settings. The lab's cosine ends at 3 percent of the peak rather than 10.

**The scale is wildly different.** The Llama 2 paper reports 2.0 trillion training tokens for its 7 billion weight model. Each of its batches held 4 million tokens, while this lab works through 16 pairs at a time.

The same paper reports 184,320 hours of work on GPUs. These are chips built to do huge amounts of arithmetic at once. That adds up to about 21 years if a single chip had done all the work alone. This lab trains on about 380 characters in milliseconds.

**Cleaning is a chain of steps** in real data work. It uses the same kinds of stages as this lab's cleaning step. These filter junk and remove near and exact repeats. It runs those stages over collections of text vastly larger than this one. It judges the results on large held-out tests rather than on one small split.

- Touvron and co-authors, *Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models*. Read section 2.2 and Tables 1 and 2 (arXiv 2307.09288).
- Llama Team, AI @ Meta, wrote *The Llama 3 Herd of Models* (arXiv 2407.21783), Table 3.
