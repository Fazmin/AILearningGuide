## The big picture

This module puts a whole training run in your hands. You choose the text, the model's size, and
the training recipe, train a character-level language model from initialized weights, and read a
model card that records what you built and how it scored.

The question is which of those decisions helped, judged on text the model never trained on.
Along the way, with plain SGD on a few hundred characters of text, a model with fewer parameters
can fit better, and the best learning rate depends on the schedule.

This module brings the first half of the track together, building on two prerequisites.
*Building a dataset* asked what text is worth training on, and *Hyperparameters & schedules* asked
how to spend a step. Here both land in one run.

The model card's **Held out** readout judges the recipe, but it barely separates training on the
raw scrape (the corpus with footer lines and repeats mixed in) from training on the cleaned one. The difference cleaning makes shows up in
**Footer lines** on the Training recipe card, the model's loss on the three boilerplate lines the
raw scrape contains.

### Where it shows up

- **Pretraining recipes.** Llama 2 used three of the Training recipe card's ideas (cosine decay,
  warmup, and gradient clipping), with AdamW instead of plain SGD.
- **Data pipelines.** The lab's **Cleaned scrape** comes from a small version of the stages that
  production pipelines run over web crawls many orders of magnitude larger: filtering, exact
  deduplication, and near deduplication.
- **Model cards.** Real cards add data provenance and licence, versioned evaluation suites, known
  failure modes, and intended use.
- **The ends of a language model.** The lab's factored model stores its 30 × 30 table of
  next-character scores as a 30 × r matrix times an r × 30 one, where r is the rank. The first
  factor is an embedding matrix, mapping each character to r numbers, and the second is an
  unembedding, scoring every candidate next character against them. Real language models have
  these two matrices at their ends.

### Why it matters

Training loss can keep falling while a model gets worse on new text, so without a held-out score
you cannot tell useful capacity from overfitting. Without the data and recipe written down on a
card, nobody can reproduce the run or judge whether its number applies to their problem. The
settings also interact: a learning rate that ruins one schedule can beat the default under another.

### What you will be able to explain

- Which decisions define a training run (text, size, and recipe) and what each one changes.
- How parameter count relates to fit, and why a smaller factored model can beat the full table here.
- What a model card promises (scope and measured scores) and what it does not.
- How learning rate, schedule, batch size, and gradient clip interact, and why held-out loss judges
  the recipe.

### What the lab shows

The lab trains two real character bigrams with minibatch SGD on the same text and recipe,
retraining whenever you move a control: the factored model you configure and the full 900-weight
table for comparison. Every curve, loss, perplexity, gradient readout, and step count comes from
those runs, and the samples are drawn from the factored model's checkpoints. The weight and
character counts are exact arithmetic, and the dotted "knows nothing" line at `ln(30)` is
closed-form.

The inputs are fixed: three short built-in corpora (Harbor weather notes, Recipe steps, and
Proverbs), a held-out split taken from the end of each, and seed 9. The **Raw scrape** option is
synthetic, the corpus's own sentences with footer lines, repeats, and fragments slotted in.

The corpus buttons sit on **Build a model in four choices**, and **Size and epochs** holds **Model
size** (the factored model's rank) and **Epochs**. **Training recipe** holds **Training text**,
**Learning rate**, **Schedule**, **Batch size**, and **Gradient clip**.

That card's readouts are **Held out**, **Footer lines**, **Steps**, **Peak gradient norm**, and
**Clipped steps**, and **Copy my recipe** saves the settings as one line of text that **Load this
recipe** reads back. **Training progress and samples** plots the curves, and **Model card**
reports **Train**, **Held out**, and **Gap**.

The key experiment is the **Model size** sweep on Harbor weather notes at 20 epochs and the default
recipe: from rank 4 up the factored model beats the full table on both losses, and held-out loss
bottoms out at rank 8 (2.117), with ranks 7 to 11 all within about 0.03 of it. Rank 1 stalls well
above the table.

On **Proverbs** at rank 12 and 30 epochs the factored model's dashed held-out curve turns upward.
On **Training recipe**, a rate of 8 on `Constant` makes the factored run diverge until
**Gradient clip** contains it, while the full table trains normally at the same rate.

## What it is

Training **from scratch** means starting from initialized weights, so everything the model
knows has to come from the corpus you picked. Four decisions define the run — which text, how
many weights, which **training recipe**, and what you report — and the wizard stages are those
decisions. The recipe is everything about the run except its architecture: epochs, peak learning
rate, schedule, batch size, gradient clip, and a fixed seed.

The model is a character bigram. The vocabulary is 30 symbols: a space, `a`–`z`, a full stop,
and two reserved angle brackets that occur in no corpus. For each possible preceding character
it keeps a row of 30 logits, and a softmax over that row is the next-character distribution.

Loss is mean cross-entropy in nats per token. A model that has learned nothing spreads
probability evenly across 30 symbols and scores `ln(30) = 3.401`, the dotted "knows nothing"
line. Training is minibatch SGD on that loss, recomputed whenever you move a control.

### What Model size changes

The full logit table holds 30 × 30 = 900 numbers. **Model size** replaces it with a
**factorization**: a 30 × r matrix times an r × 30 matrix, whose product is the logit table.
That trains 2 × r × 30 = 60r weights, and r is the **rank** — 60 at rank 1, 240 at rank 4, 480
at rank 8, 720 at rank 12.

The lab trains the factored model and the full 900-weight table on the same text with the same
recipe — by default batch 16, learning rate 0.5, a constant schedule, no clipping, and seed 9 —
so the only difference between the thick factored curves and the thin full-table curves is how the
weights are stored.

Rank is a hard ceiling on **capacity**, and rank 1 makes that concrete. With r = 1 every logit
is `u[row] · v[next]`, so every context row produces the *same* shaped distribution scaled by a
single number. Sharper, flatter, or reversed — never a different ordering of characters.

### What the recipe changes

The **Training recipe** card carries the settings the earlier labs fixed. **Training text** chooses
between a raw scrape and a cleaned one. The lab builds the scrape from the corpus's own training
sentences by slotting in footer lines, two exact repeats, a misspelled copy, and two fragments.

Cleaning uses the stages from *Building a dataset*: a boilerplate phrase, a length floor, an exact
duplicate, a near-duplicate. Cleaned is the default, and it is exactly the text this lab always
trained on.

Four settings come from *Hyperparameters & schedules*:

- **Learning rate** is the peak step size, in eight notches that double from 0.125 to 16.
- **Schedule** multiplies that peak over the run: Constant, Cosine decay, Warmup + decay, or One
  cycle. This is the **learning-rate schedule**.
- **Batch size** is the number of pairs per update, from 2 to 64. Steps equal the pairs divided by
  the batch, rounded up, times Epochs.
- **Gradient clip** scales any batch gradient whose norm exceeds it down to that norm, which is
  **gradient clipping**. It is off by default.

The recipe is saved in this lab's state, so the *Copy a link to this state* button in the header
shares it with the rest of your work. **Copy my recipe** writes only the recipe, as one line, and
**Load this recipe** reads such a line back.

## Why it’s here

Every later lab adapts weights someone else trained. This is the run you own end to end, and
the **model card** is what you have to defend afterwards. It also pulls apart two ideas that
usually travel together: **capacity** is what a model can represent at all, parameter count is
how many numbers it stores. Here they come apart on screen.

And it puts the honest number beside the flattering one. Training loss covers text the model
has already seen; **held-out loss** is scored on the last 25% of sentences, which never enter a
gradient step. The chart plots both at every epoch — solid for training, dashed for held out,
with the band between them shaded — and the **Gap** between them is what extra capacity buys
and what it costs.

## How to play with it

Click each of the three corpora and read the character count on the button, which is the whole
corpus. The **Model card** reports the split: Harbor weather notes gives 379 training characters
and 119 held out.

Leave **Epochs** at 20 and walk **Model size** upward. Rank 1 finishes at 2.925 training and
3.025 held out; rank 4 reaches 2.208 and 2.246; rank 8 reaches 2.039 and 2.117; rank 12 reaches
2.038 and 2.165. Now read the thin full-table curves: 2.283 training, 2.302 held out. The
240-weight model has beaten the 900-weight one, and the callout under the sliders says why.

Then set **Model size** back to 1 and push **Epochs** to 30. Training loss creeps from 2.925 to
2.852 and stops, and the callout reports the full table ahead by 0.688 nats (0.642 at 20
epochs). Finally read the three samples A, B, and C, which are marked on the solid curve: the
untrained checkpoint, the halfway epoch, and the end of training, each with the loss it was
drawn at.

Next, choose **Proverbs**, set **Model size** to 12 and **Epochs** to 30, and watch the dashed
held-out curve.

### Set the recipe

Go back to Harbor weather notes, rank 8, 20 epochs, and open **Training recipe** with its defaults:
**Held out** reads 2.117 and **Peak gradient norm** 0.95. Raise **Learning rate** to 4 on
`Constant` and Held out jumps to 3.016. Switch **Schedule** to `Cosine decay` at the same rate and
it falls to 2.054, below the default. Return to rate 0.5 and the order flips: Constant 2.117,
Cosine decay 2.251.

Now set **Learning rate** to 8 on `Constant`. The run halts at step 154 and the page says it
diverged. Turn **Gradient clip** up to 0.50 and it finishes, with 459 of its 480 steps clipped, at
3.089.

Set **Batch size** to 8, 16, then 64. **Steps** reads 960, 480, and 120, and Held out reads 2.089,
2.117, and 2.618. Last, switch **Training text** to `Raw scrape` and compare Held out with **Footer
lines**.

## What to notice

- The smaller model fits better, and the reason is the update rule. The full table only touches
  the row for the character it just saw, so each example moves 30 weights. In the factored model
  it moves that character's r weights in the input factor plus the whole r × 30 output factor,
  which every character shares: 31r weights, 248 at rank 8. At 379 characters, shared structure
  beats independent rows.
- So parameter count is not capacity. The full table can express strictly more matrices than any
  rank-8 product and still loses on this data at this budget.
- The **Gap** readout tells the other half. The full table has the smallest gap, 0.019, but
  because it fits its training text loosely (2.283), not because it generalizes better: at rate 8
  on Constant it trains to 1.853 and its gap grows to 0.213. Rank 12 sits at 0.128. Among the factored runs the gap trends up past rank 4 but not
  smoothly: 0.038 at rank 4, 0.095 at 6, 0.077 at 8, 0.128 at 12. With 119 held-out characters,
  single steps like 6 to 8 are noise; the trend is the signal.
- A gap is only readable next to a loss. Rank 1's gap is 0.100, larger than rank 4's, and means
  nothing good: 2.925 against 3.401 for knowing nothing. That is **underfitting**.
- Rank 1 is an underfitting plateau, not a slow start — thirty epochs buy 0.073 nats. Rank 8 and
  rank 12 train to almost the same place (2.039 against 2.038) but rank 8 holds out better,
  which is the shape of "enough capacity, then too much".
- **Overfitting** is visible on the harder corpora. On **Proverbs** at rank 12 and 30 epochs the
  dashed held-out curve bottoms out at 2.668 around epoch 18 and climbs back to 2.818 while
  training keeps falling to 1.865. More epochs made the model worse on new text.
- **Perplexity** on the model card is the held-out loss on a friendlier scale: 8.31 at the
  defaults, so 30 equally likely options narrowed to about 8.
- Samples track loss. The untrained checkpoint is letter soup; by the end there are word-shaped
  fragments like `the` and `and` in a stream that is still not fluent. A **sample** illustrates
  the loss and measures nothing.
- Past rank 15 the factorization saves nothing, since 2 × 15 × 30 is already 900. The lab notes
  this, and **Model size** stops at 12 so you cannot reach the crossover.
- The best learning rate depends on the schedule. Rate 4 ruins a constant run at 3.016 held out,
  yet under cosine decay the same peak reaches 2.054, below the default's 2.117, because the decay
  hands back small steps for the end. At rate 0.5 nothing overshoots, so decay only wastes steps.
- The full table cannot explode. Its batch gradient norm cannot pass the square root of 2, so at
  rate 8 on Constant it trains normally to 2.066 held out with a peak norm of 0.34.
- The factored model can. It multiplies two matrices, so each factor's gradient grows with the
  other. At rate 8 on Cosine decay with no clip its **Peak gradient norm** reaches about 1.2e12
  and it ends at 22.245; with a 0.50 clip it ends at 2.065.
- Clipping contains a bad rate without fixing it. At rate 8 on Constant the clip lets the run
  finish, but at 3.089 against 2.117 for the default: the steps are shorter, not better chosen.
- Batch size trades step count against noise when Epochs is fixed. Batch 64 makes only 120 updates
  and ends highest at 2.618; batch 8 makes 960 and ends lowest at 2.089.
- The raw scrape adds steps, 880 against 480, and moves **Footer lines** from 3.090 to 2.509, so
  the model has learned the page furniture. **Held out** barely moves, 2.105 against 2.117, and its
  sign flips across seeds. The score would not have told you to clean; the footers do.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Every number is computed: the gradients, all four curves, the held-out scores,
and the samples. Each curve point is the loss of the weights saved at that epoch boundary,
measured on the whole split, not a noisy batch average. What is missing is scale and context.
The model conditions on exactly one preceding character, so `t` predicts the same distribution
in `the` as in `it`.

There are no layers, no attention, and no nonlinearity between the two factored matrices. The
held-out set is 119 characters from the *end* of the document rather than a random sample, so
treat differences under about 0.05 nats as unresolved. The three samples are drawn from the
factored model's own checkpoints, so they follow **Model size**, while the thin comparison
curves belong to the full table. **Epochs** stops at 30 and **Model size** at 12.

The scrape is synthetic: the corpus's own sentences with fixed furniture slotted in, not a crawl.
The optimizer is plain SGD with no momentum, so the learning rates are not in real units. The clip
rescales the norm of both factors together, and only the factored model can explode. Every recipe
result is one seed on about 380 characters, so differences under about 0.05 nats are unresolved.

**In real systems.** The smaller-fits-better result is real but conditional: it depends on plain
SGD, on 379 training characters, and on the full table being a pure lookup with no sharing
between rows. Optimizers that normalize the step size per parameter reduce that asymmetry, and
this lab has no such option.

At realistic data scale the usual finding runs the other way, which is why scaling laws pair
parameters with tokens instead of maximizing either alone. Loss is also not capability — a good
cross-entropy says nothing about whether a model refuses or cites — and held-out loss is honest
only while the split is, since near-duplicates across a real train/test boundary inflate scores
for free.

A real recipe also leans on an adaptive optimizer, warmup, weight decay, and mixed precision, none
of which this lab offers.

## Going deeper

The factorization is not specific to this lab. A rank-r logit table *is* an embedding matrix
followed by an unembedding: 30 × r maps a character to r numbers, r × 30 scores every candidate
against that vector. Real language models have exactly these two matrices at the ends, with d in
the thousands, and everything beyond bigram statistics happens in the layers between them —
layers this model does not have.

Two mechanisms sit behind the counterintuitive result. First, gradient sparsity: in the full
table a character appearing ten times receives ten updates for its row, while every weight in
the shared output factor receives an update on every batch.

Effective sample size per parameter, not parameter count, is what the optimizer sees. Second,
the factorization is a regularizer — it forces rows to share structure whether the data wants it
or not. The full table's smaller **Gap** at the default recipe is not evidence against this: it comes
from fitting the training text loosely, and the gap widens once the table trains harder.

Real model cards go further than this one: data provenance and licence, the exact recipe,
evaluation suites with versions, known failure modes, intended use. None of it is a quality
claim. A card documents scope so someone else can judge whether your number applies to their
problem, and this card does the minimum version of that by reporting two losses that disagree.

### Toy versus real

- **Rates are not comparable.** This lab uses plain SGD at rates from 0.125 to 16. The Llama 2 paper
  trained its 7B and 13B models with AdamW at a peak learning rate of 3.0 × 10⁻⁴, and the Llama 3 paper
  lists 3 × 10⁻⁴ for the 8B model. Adam rescales each parameter's step, so the two scales do not
  convert.
- **The same three ideas, different sizes.** Llama 2 used a cosine schedule with 2,000 warmup steps,
  decayed to 10% of the peak, with weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 1.0. Cosine decay,
  warmup, and a clip are the Recipe card's three settings; here the cosine ends at 3% of the peak.
- **Scale.** The Llama 2 paper reports 2.0 trillion training tokens and 184,320 GPU hours on A100-80GB
  GPUs for the 7B model, with a global batch of 4 million tokens. This lab reads about 380 characters
  in milliseconds, in batches of 16 pairs.
- **Cleaning is a pipeline.** Real data work runs the same kinds of stages as the card's cleaning step
  — filtering, exact and near deduplication — over crawls many orders of magnitude larger, and its
  output is judged on held-out evaluations, not on one small split.
