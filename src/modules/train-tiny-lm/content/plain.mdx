## The big picture

Picture a friend reading a sentence to you one letter at a time, pausing before each new letter so
that you can guess it. If they stop at `the harbo`, you would bet on `r`, and after a `q` you would
bet on `u`. You can do this because you have seen those letters follow each other many times before.

In this module you build a tiny program that plays a simpler version of this guessing game, and you
build it starting from nothing at all. A program that guesses the next piece of text is called a
**language model**. The large language models behind chatbots do this same job at an enormous size,
while yours works one character at a time on a few hundred characters of text.

Your model starts **from scratch**, which means its numbers begin as meaningless starting values
that carry no information about any text. Everything it can predict by the end has to come from the
text you chose for it, and from nothing else.

The lab lays out a training run in four stages, where the first three are decisions you make and
the fourth is a report on them:

- **The text** the model learns from, which you pick from three short choices in the lab.
- **The size** of the model, meaning how many adjustable numbers, called **weights**, it gets to
  tune while it learns.
- **The recipe**, which covers how long training lasts, how big each learning step is, and how many
  examples the model studies before each step.
- **The report** at the end, on a page called a **model card**, which the lab fills in to record
  what you built and how it scored.

### Where you have met it

The chatbots you may have used rest on language models trained through these same kinds of
decisions, only with vastly more text and weights. The team behind Llama 2, a family of large
language models, trained one version with 7 billion weights. It learned from 2 trillion tokens,
where a token is a word or a piece of a word. That is far more text than a person could read in many
lifetimes.

The Llama 2 recipe used ideas you will try in this lab, such as a step size that shrinks during
training and a limit on oversized steps. Real model cards exist too, and they list much more than
yours will, such as where the data came from and what the model is meant for.

### Why it matters

Without a training run there is no model at all, but a run can also go wrong in ways that are hard
to spot. To see how a model is doing, you measure its error with a number called the **loss**,
where lower means better guessing. The loss on the model's own practice text flatters it, since it
has already studied that text, so you need a second loss measured on text it has never seen.

A recipe whose steps are too large can wreck the whole run, while one with too few steps stops well
short of where it could get. This module also holds a result you might not expect: with the lab's
starting recipe, a smaller model beats a bigger one. Working out why will show you that the number
of weights a model has does not, on its own, decide how well it learns.

And unless you write down what you did and how it scored, nobody else can tell what your numbers
mean or whether they apply to anything they care about.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these ideas in your own words:

- how the text, the size and the recipe together define a training run, and how each of them changes
  the result you get;
- why giving a model more weights does not automatically make it fit its text better, and what
  decides how well it fits instead;
- what a model card promises about a model, and the much longer list of things it does not promise;
- how four recipe settings work together, and why you judge the whole recipe by how well the model
  does on text it never trained on.

Those four settings are:

- the **learning rate**, which is the size of each learning step;
- the **schedule**, which is the plan for changing that step size during the run;
- the **batch size**, which is how many examples the model studies before each step;
- the **gradient clip**, which is a limit on oversized steps.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab trains two real models from scratch every time you move a control. One is yours, and the
other is a plainer comparison model, called the full table, that stores its numbers in the simplest
possible way. Because both are really trained, every curve, loss and sample you see is computed, not
drawn by hand.

You pick the text, slide the model's size up and down, and choose how many times it reads through
the text. Then you watch two losses fall as the model trains, one on its practice text and one on
text it never saw.

Along the way, you can read three samples of the model's writing, from before training to the very
end, and watch random letters turn into rough word shapes. The **Training recipe** card lets you
change how the model learns, and even break a run on purpose to see how a safety limit contains the
damage. The **Model card** then sums up your whole run, with both losses side by side.

A few things are fixed rather than computed: the three texts, and the random choices training makes,
so the same settings always give the same result. The messy "raw scrape"
version of a text is built by the lab from that text's own sentences plus made-up junk, rather than
collected from the web.

## What it is

### The guessing game, as numbers

Your model reads one character and guesses the next one, and that single character is all the
context it ever gets. So at `the harbo` it bets using the `o` alone, not the whole word, which makes
its game much narrower than the one you played above.

Its alphabet has 30 symbols: a space, the letters `a` to `z`, and a full stop, plus two angle
brackets. Those two are kept back for a later lab, which uses them as special markers, and none of
the three texts here contains them.

Every character in the practice text, together with the character right after it, makes one practice
pair. One of the lab's three texts, Harbor weather notes, has a sentence that begins
`the morning fog`. That gives the pairs `t` then `h`, `h` then `e`, and `e` then a space, and so on
through the whole text. A model that learns from pairs of characters like this is called a
**character bigram**, since "bi" means two, and the **Model card** uses that name under Architecture.

In this lesson, the character the model is looking at is called the current character. For each of
the 30 characters that could be current, the model keeps a row of 30 scores, one for each character
that could come next. A step called **softmax** turns each row of scores into chances that add up to
100 percent, and the highest scores get the biggest chances.

For example, if a row had only three scores, 2, 1 and 0, softmax would turn them into chances of
about 67, 24 and 9 percent. A score can even be negative, and it still gets a chance, only a small
one.

After training, you would expect the row for `t` to give `h` a large share of the chances, because
`th` turns up again and again in the practice text.

### Measuring surprise: the loss

To improve, the model needs a single number that says how badly it is doing, and that number is the
**loss** from the big picture. For each pair, the lab looks at the chance the model gave to the
character that really came next. A big chance means little surprise while a small chance means a
lot, and the loss is the average surprise over every pair, so lower is better.

The surprise for a chance of 1 in N is written ln N, where ln is the natural logarithm key on a
scientific calculator. You do not need to know how ln works inside, only how it behaves for a few
chances:

- A sure thing, which is a chance of 1 in 1, gives a surprise of 0, because there is nothing to be
  surprised by.
- A chance of 1 in 8 gives a surprise of about 2.08, which is a moderate amount.
- A chance of 1 in 30 gives a surprise of 3.401, which is what a completely blind guess gets here.

A chance that is not a whole "1 in N" works the same way. A 40 percent chance is 1 in 2.5, so its
surprise is ln 2.5, which is about 0.92.

So the surprise climbs as the chance shrinks, but it climbs slowly, since going from 8 choices to 30
adds only about 1.3. The unit of this surprise is called the **nat**, which is why the chart measures
loss in nats per token. A **token** is the piece of text a model reads at a time. Large models use
words and pieces of words, while in this lab every token is one character.

Now picture a model that has learned nothing and spreads its bets evenly, giving each of the 30
symbols a chance of 1 in 30. Its surprise is ln 30 = 3.401 on every character, so its loss is 3.401
as well. The chart draws this as a dotted line labelled `knows nothing`, which is the number to
beat, since any model that has learned a pattern should land below it.

When a model's loss on a text is low, people say the model fits that text well, and the lower the
loss, the closer the fit.

### How training works

Training from scratch begins with those meaningless starting values and then repeats one small
routine hundreds of times. First, the model takes a handful of practice pairs, called a **batch**,
and measures its loss on them; at the default settings a batch holds 16 pairs. Then it works out,
for every weight, how steeply the loss would rise or fall if that weight moved a little. That
steepness is called the weight's **slope**, just like the slope of a hill on a graph.

The full list of those slopes is called the **gradient**. It points the way that would raise the
loss, so each weight steps the opposite way, downhill, and this lesson calls that downhill direction
the push. The **learning rate** sets how big each step is. Repeating this routine is called
**gradient descent**, because each step heads downhill on the loss, the way a ball rolls toward the
lowest point of a valley.

To work one push through, picture the weights for now as the 900 scores in the rows, since the
Model size section below shows a cheaper way to store them. Suppose a batch holds the pair `t`
then `h`, and the model gives `h` only a 10 percent chance after `t`.

The push then says to raise the score for `h` in `t`'s row by a lot. It also says to lower the
other 29 scores in that row by a little, so that `h` gets a bigger share next time. Each weight's part of the
push says which way that weight should move, and how strongly.

One full pass through all the practice pairs is called an **epoch**, and the default run makes 20 of
them. The pairs are shuffled into batches, the pushes are actually calculated rather than made up,
and the whole run starts again from the beginning each time you move a control. Because the model is
so small, that takes only milliseconds, which are thousandths of a second.

The first card, **Build a model in four choices**, lays the run out as a strip of four stages, and

- **Corpus** is the text the model reads, since a corpus is the usual name for a body of text used

Clicking a stage only moves the `Choice N of 4` marker at the top of the card, since no choice is

### What Model size does

The most direct way to store the scores is a full grid, with a row for each current character and a
column for each next character. That grid holds 30 × 30 = 900 numbers, and this lab calls it the
**full table**. Each of its rows is completely separate, so learning something about `t` changes
nothing in the row for `s`.

Picture a coach who has to rate how well each of 30 players suits each of 30 positions on a team.
One way is to write all 900 ratings by hand, one for each player and position, with no link between
them, and that is the full table.

A cheaper way is to give each player a short stats card, with one number for speed and one for
strength. Each position then gets a matching card that says how much speed and strength it needs.

To rate a player for a position, the coach multiplies the matching numbers on the two cards and adds
up the results. **Model size** swaps the full table for this cheaper version, so each character, as
the current one, gets a short list of r numbers. Each possible next character gets a matching list
of r numbers, and the score for a pair is the two lists multiplied number by number, then added up.

Storing a big grid as two thin sets of lists that multiply together like this is called a
**factorization**. The length of each list, r, is called the **rank**, and it is the number that the
**Model size** slider sets.

Here is one score worked through at rank 2, using made-up numbers. Suppose the list for `t`, as the
current character, is 2 and −1, while the list for `h`, as a possible next character, is 1.5 and
0.5. Multiplying the matching numbers and adding gives 2 × 1.5 plus −1 × 0.5, which comes to
3 − 0.5 = 2.5, the score for `h` after `t`.

Counting the weights uses the same kind of arithmetic, because each of the 30 characters gets r
numbers on the current side and r more on the next side. So the model stores 2 × r × 30 weights in
all, and at the default rank of 8 that is 2 × 8 × 30 = 480.

The slider shows this as `rank 8 · 480 weights`, and the other sizes you will try in this lesson
follow the same rule:

- rank 1 stores 60 weights, which makes it the smallest model the slider allows;
- rank 4 stores 240 weights, which is about a quarter of the full table's size;
- rank 12 stores 720 weights, which makes it the largest model the slider allows.

The lab trains your factored model and the full table side by side. Because both models use the same
text and the same recipe, the only difference between them is how the weights are stored. On the
chart, the thick curves are your model and the thin curves are the full table.

The coach picture stops matching in one important way, because a coach chooses what each stat means,
while nobody chooses what the model's r numbers mean. They end up wherever training happens to leave
them, so you usually could not put a name such as "speed" on any one of them.

### Capacity: the ceiling that rank sets

How much pattern a model can represent at all, however long it trains, is called its **capacity**,
and the rank puts a hard ceiling on it. Rank 1 shows that ceiling most clearly, because each list
then holds a single number, so every score is one number multiplied by another.

Take a small case with only three possible next characters, where the next-side numbers are 3 for
`h`, 2 for `e` and 1 for `u`. A current character whose number is 2 gets scores of 6, 4 and 2,
so `h` comes first. One whose number is 0.5 gets 1.5, 1 and 0.5, which is the same order but
flatter. One whose number is −1 gets −3, −2 and −1, so the order flips and `u` comes first.

So at rank 1, every row follows one shared order of next characters or its exact reverse. Each row's
favourite must be either the top or the bottom of that order, so there can only ever be two
different favourites. A rank 1 model can never expect `u` after `q`, `h` after `t` and `t` after a space all at once,
because that needs three. Real text needs far more than three.

In the coach picture, that is like rating everyone on speed alone. Every player gets the same order
of positions, only more or less extreme, or flipped end to end for a player whose number is negative.
That limit is built into the model's shape, so extra training cannot remove it.

### What the recipe changes

The size decides what the model could represent, while the recipe decides how training gets there,
much like a set of cooking instructions. With the same ingredients and the same pan, how hot you
cook, for how long and how much at a time can change the dish completely.

The **Training recipe** card holds the settings that the earlier labs kept fixed, now turned into
controls. The cooking picture stops matching at one point: a cook can taste the dish, but a model's
loss on its own practice text is a poor guide. You judge a recipe mainly by the held-out loss, which
the Why it’s here section explains.

The **training recipe**, then, is everything about the run apart from the model's shape. It covers
which version of the text the model reads and how many epochs it runs, along with the learning rate
and how it changes. It also covers the batch size, the gradient clip and a fixed seed, and each of
these gets its own part below.

### The training text

A program that copies text off web pages is called a scraper, and the pile of text it collects is
called a scrape. A scrape picks up junk along with the writing you wanted, such as the repeated
notices at the bottom of every page, which are called **boilerplate**. The **Training text** switch
chooses between a `Raw scrape`, junk included, and a `Cleaned scrape`, which is the default.

The lab builds the raw scrape from the corpus's own training sentences by slotting extra lines in at
fixed places:

- footer lines of the kind found at the bottom of web pages, such as
  `click here to subscribe to our newsletter.`. Harbor gets three of these, and Proverbs gets four
  because it has one more training sentence;
- two exact repeats of sentences that already appeared earlier in the scrape;
- one copy of a sentence with a spelling mistake in it, such as `cllouds` in place of `clouds`;
- two tiny fragments, `ok.` and `yes.`, which carry almost no useful text at all.

Cleaning uses the stages from *Building a dataset*, in order, dropping lines with a boilerplate
phrase, lines under 12 characters, exact repeats, and near-repeats such as the misspelled copy. For
Harbor weather notes, the raw scrape has 14 lines and 696 characters, and cleaning removes 8 of
those lines:

- 3 boilerplate lines, which are the three footers;
- 2 lines that are too short, which are the two fragments;
- 2 lines that repeat an earlier line exactly;
- 1 near-repeated line, which is the misspelled copy.

That leaves 6 lines and 379 characters, and those 6 lines are exactly the original training
sentences. So the `Cleaned scrape` is the very same text that this lab has always trained on.

### Learning rate and schedule

**Learning rate** sets how far each weight moves for a given push, and its slider has eight notches
that each double the one before. The notches run from 0.125 up to 16, and the default is 0.5. For
the same push, a rate of 4 moves every weight eight times as far as the default rate does.

**Schedule** changes the step size over the course of the run, and its four buttons give four
different shapes:

- `Constant` keeps the same step size from the first batch of the run to the last.
- `Cosine decay` starts at full size and eases down along the smooth curve that gives it its name,
  ending at 3 percent of where it began.
- `Warmup + decay` starts near zero and ramps up to full size over the first 15 percent of the steps.
  Then it shrinks in a straight line to 5 percent of full size.
- `One cycle` also warms up to full size, then eases down along a smooth curve until it ends below
  where it began.

Every schedule works by multiplying the learning rate, so the rate you set is the peak, and the
schedule decides how much of that peak each step uses. With the rate at 4 and `Cosine decay`, for
example, the first step uses 4 and the last uses about 0.12, which is 3 percent of 4. A plan like
this for the step size across a run is called a **learning-rate schedule**.

### Batch size

**Batch size** is how many pairs the model studies before each update, from 2 up to 64, and the
default is 16. The number of updates, which the card shows as **Steps**, is the number of pairs
divided by the batch size, rounded up, and then multiplied by **Epochs**.

Harbor's training text gives 378 pairs, shown at the top of the **Training progress and samples**
card, and the default run turns them into steps like this:

- 378 pairs ÷ 16 pairs per batch = 23.6, which rounds up to 24 batches in each epoch;
- 24 batches × 20 epochs = 480 steps, which is the number the **Steps** readout shows.

### Gradient clip

The push has an overall size, which is one number for how strong it is across all the weights
together. To find it, square each weight's part of the push, add up the squares, and take the square
root. That size is called the **gradient norm**, and the **Peak gradient norm** readout shows the
largest one in the whole run, measured before any clipping happens.

Putting a limit on that size is called **gradient clipping**, and the **Gradient clip** control sets
the limit. Whenever a push is bigger than the limit, the lab shrinks every part of it by the same
fraction, so that its size drops to exactly the limit.

Here is a small example with only two weights. A push of 0.6 on one weight and 0.8 on the other has
a size of the square root of 0.36 + 0.64, which is 1. With the clip at 0.50, the lab halves both
parts to 0.3 and 0.4, so the size drops to 0.5.

The two weights still move in the same proportion to each other, which is what people mean when they
say clipping keeps the push's direction. A push whose size is already under the limit, such as 0.3,
passes through untouched.

The clip is off by default and can go up to 1.50, while the **Clipped steps** readout counts how
many steps it had to shrink.

### The seed, and sharing your recipe

Training your factored model uses random numbers twice, once for its starting values and once for
shuffling the pairs into batches. The full table needs them only for shuffling, since it starts with
every number at zero. A **seed** is a number that fixes those random choices, so the same recipe
always gives the same result, and this lab always uses seed 9.

Your recipe is saved in the lab's state, so the **Copy a link to this state** button in the header
shares it with the rest of your work. **Copy my recipe** writes every setting out as one line of
text that starts with `tiny-lm-recipe v1`, and **Load this recipe** reads a line like that back in.

### The model card

The last stage is a report rather than a choice, because the **Model card** writes down the run that
the other three stages defined. It lists the corpus and how it was split, the architecture and the
recipe. Then come the **Training loss** and the **Held-out loss**, with a number called perplexity
that the What to notice section explains.

Its last row gives the full table's two losses for comparison, and under the rows sit three
readouts, **Train**, **Held out** and **Gap**, which the next section explains.

## Why it’s here

### The flattering loss and the honest one

Picture a friend who gets ready for a test by memorising the answer key to the practice test. They
score 100 percent every time they retake the practice test, yet that says little about how they will
do on the real test, which asks new questions.

Training loss has the same weakness, because it measures the model on text it has already studied
many times over. So the lab holds back about the last quarter of each corpus's sentences, which never
enter a single training step, and measures the model's loss on those sentences as well. That second
loss is the **held-out loss**, and it is the honest one, because it shows how well the model predicts
text it has never seen.

For Harbor weather notes, the held-out text is the final two sentences of the corpus, which come to
119 characters, against 379 for training:

- `in summer the harbor fills with sails and the water stays warm.`
- `the fog returns in the morning and the pattern repeats.`

How well a pattern learned from one text carries over to new text is called generalization. The
chart draws both losses after every epoch, with a solid line for training and a dashed line for held
out, and it shades the space between them. The size of that space is the **generalization gap**,
which is the held-out loss minus the training loss.

The **Gap** readout on the **Model card** shows that difference at the end of the run, and the chart
prints it beside the curves as well. The readout subtracts the losses before they are rounded to
three decimals, so your own subtraction can differ from it by 0.001.

Extra capacity usually lowers the training loss, and the gap shows how much of that gain fails to
carry over to new text, which training loss alone never shows.

### Two ideas that usually travel together

The number of weights, often called the parameter count, and capacity usually rise together, so
people tend to treat them as one idea. They are not the same thing. A rank 15 model would store 900 weights, the same as the full table.
Yet every row of its scores would still be a mix of only 15 shared patterns, so it could build only
some of the grids the full table can.

This lab adds a second surprise, which What to notice unpacks: even the model with more of both can
end up fitting worse after a short run.

### The run that is yours

From here on, every lab in the track starts from weights that somebody else trained, and you adapt
them. This is the one run you own from start to finish, and the model card is the claim you have to
stand behind afterwards.

A model card works a bit like the label on a food packet. It lists what went in and a few measured
numbers, but it does not tell you whether the food tastes good or suits your plans. The comparison
stops there, because a food label's numbers hold for every packet, while a model card's losses hold
only for the text and recipe it names.

### Where the first half of the track meets

*Building a dataset* asked which text is worth training on, and *Hyperparameters & schedules* asked
how to spend each step. In this lab both questions land in one run. The **Held out** readout judges the recipe, but it barely tells raw text from cleaned
text. The difference cleaning makes shows up instead in **Footer lines**, a readout on the **Training
recipe** card that gives the model's loss on the footer junk.

## How to play with it

Start with every control at its default, which is Harbor weather notes, **Model size** at rank 8,
**Epochs** at 20, and the **Training recipe** card untouched.

### Compare the three texts

Click each of the three corpus buttons and read the character count on it, which is the size of the
whole corpus. Harbor weather notes has 499 characters, roughly one short paragraph of a school book,
and the other two are shorter still.

The **Model card** then shows how the text splits, and for Harbor that is 379 characters for
training and 119 held out. The one character left over is the space where the two parts meet.

Notice how little text that is, since every pattern the model ends up with has to come from those
few sentences. Each button also describes its corpus, and Harbor is repetitive description. Recipe
steps is a list of cooking instructions, which has nothing to do with the training recipe apart from
the word.

The Proverbs button calls it short unrelated sentences that are hard to fit. You will see below what
that means here: the text is small enough to memorise, so its training loss can fall low, while its
held-out loss stays high.

### Walk Model size up

Leave **Epochs** at 20 and move **Model size** from 1 up to 12, stopping at these ranks to read the
**Model card** each time:

- At rank 1, the training loss ends at 2.925 and the held-out loss ends at 3.025.
- Rank 4 brings the training loss down to 2.208 and the held-out loss down to 2.246.
- Rank 8 brings them lower again, to 2.039 for training and 2.117 for held out.
- Rank 12 barely changes the training loss, at 2.038, but the held-out loss rises to 2.165.

Now read the thin lines, which belong to the 900-weight full table and end at 2.283 for training and
2.302 held out. So with this recipe, the 240-weight model at rank 4 has already beaten a table almost
four times its size. The boxed note under the two sliders on the **Size and epochs** card, which
this lesson calls the callout, explains why.

### Hit the wall at rank 1

**Try this:** put **Model size** back to 1 and drag **Epochs** all the way to 30. The training loss
ends at 2.852, and the callout reports that the full table is ahead by 0.688 nats. Look at the thick
solid curve, too: from about epoch 10 to epoch 22 it hardly moves, staying between about 2.92 and 2.96.

Now set **Model size** to 8, and its training curve drops below 2.852 by epoch 4, while even the
slow full table passes that mark by epoch 5. Changing the learning rate or the schedule on the
**Training recipe** card does not rescue rank 1 either. With or without the clip, it never gets
within a third of a nat of the full table. That is a wall, not a slow start.

### Read the samples

Below the chart on the **Training progress and samples** card sit three samples, labelled A, B and
C. Each one is also marked on the solid curve at the point where it was written. A **sample** is
text that the model generates one character at a time, starting after `the` and a space.

The model only uses that last space, and the sample shows only the characters it adds. Each next
character is picked at random, with the likelier characters picked more often. The samples use a
fixed seed of their own, so they come out the same each time for the same weights.

Sample A comes from before any training, B from halfway through, and C from the end, and each one
shows the loss the model had at that moment. At the defaults, sample A is letter soup, because the
untrained model sits at a loss of 3.40, right on the `knows nothing` line. By sample C, at a loss of
2.04, you can see word-shaped pieces such as `the` in a stream that is still not real English.

Moving **Model size** changes all three samples, because they come from your factored model rather
than from the full table.

### Watch overfitting happen

Pick **Proverbs**, set **Model size** to 12 and **Epochs** to 30, and keep your eyes on the dashed
held-out line. It falls for a while, bottoms out, and then turns back up, while the solid training
line keeps going down the whole time. The What to notice section explains what that turn means.

### Set the recipe

Go back to Harbor weather notes, rank 8 and 20 epochs, and open the **Training recipe** card with
its defaults. There, **Held out** reads 2.117 and **Peak gradient norm** reads 0.95, and from that
starting point you can work through these comparisons one at a time:

1. Raise **Learning rate** to 4 with **Schedule** on `Constant`, and **Held out** jumps to 3.016,
   which is much worse than before.
2. Switch **Schedule** to `Cosine decay` at the same rate, and **Held out** falls to 2.054, which is
   better than the default.
3. Return the rate to 0.5 and compare the two schedules again, and the order flips, with `Constant`
   at 2.117 and `Cosine decay` at 2.251.

### Break the run, then rescue it

Set **Learning rate** to 8 with **Schedule** on `Constant`, and the run halts at step 154 with a
warning that it diverged. Diverging means the weights grew so large that the computer could no
longer store the loss as an ordinary number, so training could not go on.

Now turn **Gradient clip** up to 0.50, and the same run finishes, with 459 of its 480 steps clipped.
It ends with a held-out loss of 3.089, which is far worse than the default's 2.117 but is at least a
real number.

### Change the batch size

Return the rate to 0.5 and the clip to off, then set **Batch size** to 8, 16 and 64 in turn while
you watch **Steps** and **Held out**:

- batch 8 gives 960 steps, and its held-out loss ends at 2.089;
- batch 16 gives 480 steps, and its held-out loss ends at 2.117;
- batch 64 gives only 120 steps, and its held-out loss ends clearly highest, at 2.618.

### Feed it junk

Last, note **Held out** and **Footer lines** with `Cleaned scrape`, then switch **Training text** to

## What to notice

### The smaller model wins, and sharing is the reason

At the default recipe, the full table only updates the row for the current character, so each
practice pair moves 30 weights. In the factored model, the same pair moves that character's own list
of r numbers plus the whole next side, which every character shares. At rank 8 that is 8 + 240 =
248 weights moved by a single pair.

The letter `q` shows how uneven this gets, since it appears only once in Harbor's training text, in
the word `quiet`. So the full table's `q` row learns from that one pair in each pass. Over 20 epochs that
row gets only 20 updates, while every weight on the shared side of the factored model is updated on
every one of the 480 steps.

In the coach picture, one game showing that player `t` suits position `h` changes only `t`'s own 30
ratings in the full table. With stats cards, the coach updates `t`'s card and also every position's
card, and those position cards are used to rate every other player too. With only 378 pairs to learn
from, that sharing gets far more out of each example.

### More weights is not more fitting power

The full table has more weights than any rank 8 model, and more capacity too.
Every grid that a rank 8 model can build is also a possible 900-number grid. Yet after 20 epochs at the default
recipe it fits worse.

Capacity is only a ceiling, the most a model could ever fit with unlimited training. The full table
has the higher ceiling, but after 20 epochs it is still far below it, because each of its rows gets
so little practice. So a parameter count tells you at best about the ceiling, not how close a given
run gets to it.

You can check that the full table was short of practice rather than capacity. At a rate of 4 on
`Constant`, its training loss in the Model card's comparison row reaches 1.872. That is far below
the 2.039 your rank 8 model reaches at the default.

### Read the Gap as a trend

The **Gap** tells the other half of the story. The full table has the smallest gap, only 0.019,
although the next part explains why that is not the win it looks like. Among the factored models,
the gap tends to grow with rank past rank 4, but it grows in a bumpy way:

- at rank 4, the gap is 0.038, so the two curves almost touch;
- at rank 6, the gap jumps to 0.095, more than twice as wide;
- at rank 8, the gap dips back down to 0.077;
- at rank 12, the gap reaches 0.128, the widest of these four.

The held-out text is only 119 characters, which is two sentences. So the held-out loss can shift by a
few hundredths through small accidents, such as which letters happen to land in those sentences.
Random ups and downs like these are called **noise**.

Ranking rank 6 against rank 8 on so little text is like ranking two basketball players on two free
throws each. A single step such as rank 6 to rank 8 is mostly noise, so it is the overall trend that
counts.

### A small gap can still be bad news

A gap only means something when you read it next to the loss itself. At the very start of training,
the two curves begin at the same point, a gap of about 0.001, yet the model knows nothing.

The full table's gap of 0.019 is small for a similar reason. Its training loss of 2.283 is worse
than any rank from 4 up, so it has fit its practice text too loosely to have much to lose on new
text. Train it at a rate of 8 on `Constant`, and the Model card's comparison row reads 1.853 for
training and 2.066 held out, a gap of 0.213.

A loss that stays high because the model cannot represent the pattern, or has not trained long
enough, is called **underfitting**. The full table at the default recipe shows the second kind,
since bigger steps let it fit much better.

Rank 1 shows the first kind. Its gap of 0.100 is no comfort, because its training loss of 2.925 is
not far below the 3.401 of a model that learned nothing. However you train it, it stays well behind
the full table. When a model is stuck like this, more capacity would help and more steps would not.

### Overfitting on Proverbs

On **Proverbs** at rank 12 and 30 epochs, you can watch the opposite failure happen. The dashed
held-out line bottoms out at 2.668 around epoch 18, then climbs back to 2.818. Meanwhile the solid
training line keeps dropping, all the way down to 1.865.

Past epoch 18, extra practice made the model worse at new text, because it was fitting quirks of its
training sentences that new sentences do not share. Fitting the training text more closely than new
text of the same kind is called **overfitting**.

Picture the friend with the answer key again, but now they have memorised it so firmly that they
write the practice answers down on new questions too. The model does the same, making confident bets
on patterns from its training sentences. Each confident wrong bet costs a lot of surprise on new
text, so the held-out loss rises.

### Enough, then too much

Rank 8 and rank 12 train to almost the same place, 2.039 against 2.038, and rank 12 does no better
on the held-out text. Its 2.165 against rank 8's 2.117 is a difference of 0.048, which is inside the
noise, and at 30 epochs the two swap places.

So the extra 240 weights bought nothing here, which is the shape to look for when you size a model:
more capacity helps only until the model has enough. For a clear case of too much capacity costing
you on new text, look back at Proverbs.

### Perplexity turns the loss into a count

The **Model card** shows **perplexity** beside the held-out loss, and it turns the loss back into a
number of choices. At the defaults, the held-out loss of 2.117 becomes a perplexity of 8.31. That
means the model is about as unsure as if it were picking among 8 equally likely characters, which is
a big drop from the 30 it started with.

On a scientific calculator, the eˣ key undoes ln, and e to the power 2.117 is about 8.31. You can
check this against the surprise scale from earlier, where a 1 in 8 chance gave a surprise of about
2.08. By the same logic, a model that learned nothing would have a perplexity of exactly 30.

### Samples follow the loss

The samples improve as the loss falls, from letter soup before training to word-shaped pieces such
as `the` in a stream that is still not fluent. A sample shows you what a loss number looks like as
text, but it measures nothing, so never judge a run by its samples alone.

### Rank 15 is where the saving ends

Past rank 15, the factored model saves nothing at all, because 2 × 15 × 30 is already 900, the size
of the full table. **Model size** stops at 12, so you cannot reach that point in this lab.

### The best learning rate depends on the schedule

Picture adjusting the hot tap in a shower that is slow to respond. If you make big turns the whole
time, you keep overshooting, too hot and then too cold, and you never settle. If you start with big
turns and switch to tiny ones near the end, you get close quickly and then settle on the right
temperature.

A learning rate of 4 behaves like those big turns, because on `Constant` every step stays large
right to the end. The run keeps overshooting and finishes at 3.016 held out. Under `Cosine decay`,
the same peak reaches 2.054, better than the default's 2.117, because the decay hands back small
steps for the end of the run.

At a rate of 0.5, though, no step is too big to begin with, so shrinking the steps only wastes the
later part of the run. That is why `Cosine decay` ends worse there, at 2.251 against 2.117 for
`Constant`. A schedule only makes sense when you judge it together with the rate it multiplies.

### Why only the factored model can blow up

The full table cannot explode, because of how its push is built. For one pair, the push lowers each character's score by the chance the model gave it. The one
exception is the character that really came next, which is raised by 1 minus its chance.

The worst case is a model that is 100 percent sure of a wrong character. Then the wrong character is
lowered by 1, the right one is raised by 1, and the rest do not move. The size is the square root of
1 + 1, which is about 1.41. Every other case is smaller, and averaging over a batch cannot make it
bigger.

So at a learning rate of 8 on `Constant`, the full table trains normally to 2.066 held out, and its
biggest push has a size of only 0.34.

The factored model is different, because it multiplies two sets of numbers together, so the push on
each side grows with the size of the other side. A big step can make one side larger, which makes
the next push on the other side larger, which makes the next step larger still.

On `Constant` at a rate of 8, the factored run halts before the end, so this comparison uses
`Cosine decay`, where the run does finish. With no clip, its **Peak gradient norm** reaches about
`1.2e+12`, which is calculator shorthand for 1.2 trillion.

The run ends at a held-out loss of 22.245, far worse than the 3.401 of a model that learned nothing.
With a clip of 0.50, the same run ends at 2.065. That even beats the default, because `Cosine decay`
shrinks the steps late in the run: the clip held the early, oversized steps in check, and the
schedule did the rest.

### Clipping contains a bad rate without fixing it

On `Constant` the clip gets no such help, so at a rate of 8 it lets the run finish, but only at
3.089 held out, against the default's 2.117. The steps are shorter, but they are not better
aimed, because the rate is still too large for every step of the run. Clipping is a safety limit
that stops one oversized step from wrecking the weights, not a cure for a learning rate that is
wrong.

### Batch size trades steps against noise

Picture two teachers marking the same pile of homework over the same number of weeks. One gives
feedback after every 8 questions, while the other waits until 64 questions are done. The second
teacher's feedback is based on more work each time, so it is steadier, but the student hears it far
less often.

Batch size works the same way when **Epochs** is fixed, because a bigger batch averages over more
pairs and smooths out the random wobble in each push. The catch is that bigger batches leave fewer
updates, so batch 64 makes only 120 of them and ends clearly highest, at 2.618.

Batch 8 makes 960 updates and ends at 2.089, which is within noise of batch 16. So the clear lesson
is at the large end: on this text, with the epochs fixed, too few updates cost more than steadier
pushes gain.

### Junk text: the footers notice it, the held-out loss does not

The raw scrape adds steps, 880 against 480, because it has more pairs in each pass. It also lowers
**Footer lines** from 3.090 to 2.509, which shows that the model has learned to predict the footer
junk. Yet **Held out** barely moves, 2.105 against 2.117, and with other seeds the difference
sometimes goes the other way, although this lab keeps the seed fixed at 9.

So the held-out loss alone would never have told you to clean the text, while the **Footer lines**
loss does. In this lab, cleaning keeps junk out of the model and saves training steps rather than
lowering the held-out loss.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Nothing in the training is faked: the pushes, all four curves, the held-out losses and the samples
are all worked out live. The one made-up part is the raw scrape, described below. Each point on a
curve is the loss of the weights saved at that epoch, measured on the whole split rather than on one
noisy batch.

What is missing is size and context, since the model sees exactly one character back, so `t`
predicts the same thing in `the` as in `it`. A real model has many layers, which are stages that each
rework every token's list of numbers. This model has none, and no attention either, which is the
part of a real model that lets it look back across many earlier tokens.

Nothing sits between the two halves of the factored model, the current-character lists and the
next-character lists, while a real model does its main work in the layers between them.

Because the held-out text is only 119 characters, treat any difference under about 0.05 nats as
noise. It is also taken from the end of the document rather than picked at random, so it may not be
typical of the rest.

The three samples come from the factored model you sized, so they change when you move **Model
size**. The thin curves belong to the full table, which has no samples of its own. **Epochs** stops
at 30 and **Model size** stops at 12, and the three corpora are fixed, so you cannot paste in your
own text.

The scrape is synthetic, meaning the lab made it up from the corpus's own sentences with fixed junk
slotted in, rather than collecting it from real web pages.

The step method is the plainest kind, called stochastic gradient descent or SGD, where "stochastic"
means random, because each batch is a shuffled handful of pairs. Real models use step methods with
momentum, which carries part of each step into the next, and with a separate step size for each
weight. So a rate of 0.5 here and a rate of 0.0003 in a real model are on different scales and
cannot be compared, as Toy versus real explains.

The clip shrinks the push on both halves of the factored model together, and only the factored model
can explode. Every recipe result comes from one seed on about 380 characters, so differences under
about 0.05 nats are noise there too. Weight decay, a real-world extra described below, is not
offered, and the warmup always takes 15 percent of the steps.

The callout on the **Size and epochs** card is written with the default recipe in mind. Raise the
rate to 2 or 4 on `Constant` and the full table pulls ahead, and the callout then blames capacity.
There the factored model is really overshooting, since the same rank 8 reaches a lower loss at the
default rate.

### Where the real mechanism fails

"Smaller fits better" is a real result, but it depends on this setup, with plain SGD, 379 characters
of training text, and a full table whose rows share nothing. It also depends on the default learning
rate and short run. On `Constant` at a rate of 2 or more, the full table pulls ahead. Bigger steps
let its rarely updated rows catch up, while the factored model starts to overshoot.

Step methods that give each weight its own step size also shrink that advantage. They give weights
that rarely get practice, such as the `q` row, bigger steps to make up for it, and this lab has no
switch for them.

Scaling laws are patterns that describe how loss falls as models and data grow. With real amounts of
data, bigger models usually do better, and scaling laws say to grow the weights and the text together
rather than pushing either one alone.

A held-out loss is only honest while the split is honest. Suppose near-copies of the same sentence
land on both sides of a real split between training text and test text. Then the model has partly
seen the test already, so its loss looks better than it deserves, which is one more reason the
near-repeat stage in cleaning matters.

A real recipe also leans on tools that this lab leaves out, such as a step method that gives each
weight its own step size. It also uses weight decay, which gently shrinks every weight a little at
each step so that none grows too large. Real runs also use mixed precision, which does some of the
arithmetic with shorter, less exact numbers to save time and memory.

**A common mix-up:** a low loss is not a capable model. Loss only says how well the model predicts
the next character on this kind of text. It promises nothing about following instructions, refusing
what it should refuse, citing sources or getting facts right.

A model card records which model was trained on which text, with which recipe, and how it scored. It
is a statement of scope, not a quality rating, and its job is to let someone else judge whether your
number applies to their problem.

## Going deeper

### The same two halves sit inside real models

The two thin halves of your factored model are not a toy shortcut, because every real language model
has these same two pieces at its two ends. One turns each token into a list of numbers, and the
other scores every possible next token against that list. These two pieces are usually called the
embedding and the unembedding.

In a real model each list is thousands of numbers long, and much of the interesting work happens in
the many layers stacked between the two ends. Your model has no layers there, and no attention to
look further back. It only ever sees one character, which is why it can only learn which character
tends to follow which.

### Two reasons the small model wins

The first reason is uneven practice, which the letter `q` showed in What to notice. A rare
character's row in the full table barely gets updated, while the shared side of the factored model
learns from every step. What matters is how much practice each weight gets, not how many weights
there are.

The second reason is that the factorization is a constraint, since it forces characters to share
structure whether the text calls for it or not. That keeps the factored model from bending each row
to fit its few training pairs exactly, which helps it on new text.

The full table's tiny gap at the default recipe has a different cause, as you saw earlier. It learns
so slowly that it has fit its training text only loosely, so it has little to lose on new text, and
once it trains harder its gap grows.

### What real model cards add

Real model cards go much further than this one, recording where the data came from and under what
licence, along with the exact recipe. They also list which tests were run on which version, the
failure modes people already know about, and what the model is meant to be used for.

None of that is a quality rating either, because a card describes what a model covers rather than
how good it is. Your card does the smallest version of the same job, because it reports two losses
that disagree instead of only the one that looks good.

### Toy versus real

**Learning rates do not carry over** from this lab to real models, because this lab uses plain SGD
with rates from 0.125 to 16. The Llama 2 paper trained its models of 7 billion and 13 billion
weights with a method called AdamW, which rescales each weight's own step.

The peak learning rate for those Llama 2 models was 0.0003, which the paper writes in scientific
notation as 3.0 × 10⁻⁴. The Llama 3 paper lists the same rate for its 8 billion weight model. Since
AdamW gives every weight its own step size, its rates and this lab's rates cannot be compared.

**The same three ideas show up at full size** in Llama 2. It used a schedule that warmed up over
2,000 steps and then followed a cosine curve down to 10 percent of the peak. It also used weight
decay of 0.1 and clipped its gradients at 1.0. Cosine decay, warmup and a clip are three of this
lab's recipe settings, although the lab's cosine ends at 3 percent of the peak rather than 10.

**The scale is wildly different**, since the Llama 2 paper reports 2.0 trillion training tokens for
its 7 billion weight model. Each of its batches held 4 million tokens, while this lab works through
16 pairs at a time.

The same paper reports 184,320 hours of work on GPUs, which are chips built to do huge amounts of
arithmetic at once. That adds up to about 21 years if a single chip had done all the work alone,
while this lab trains on about 380 characters in milliseconds.

**Cleaning is a chain of steps** in real data work, using the same kinds of stages as this lab's
cleaning step: filtering junk and removing near and exact repeats. It runs those stages over
collections of text vastly larger than this one, and it judges the results on large held-out tests
rather than on one small split.

- Touvron and co-authors, *Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models* (arXiv 2307.09288),
  section 2.2 and Tables 1 and 2.
- Llama Team, AI @ Meta, *The Llama 3 Herd of Models* (arXiv 2407.21783), Table 3.
