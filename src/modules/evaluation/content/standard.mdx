## The big picture

This module is about **evaluation**: scoring a trained model on items it never saw, under a rule
fixed in advance, and reading the score as an estimate with an error bar. The question is when a
leaderboard rank reflects what the models learned, and when it is an artefact of the test.

It builds on *Train one from scratch*, which judged a run by its held-out loss. The same kind of
character bigram is now scored on two-option multiple-choice items, and the lesson then widens
from models to applications.

### Where it shows up

- **Multiple-choice benchmarks.** **Likelihood scoring** is one rule for them: pick the option
  the model gives the highest log-probability, in total or divided by length, with nothing
  generated. Lettered options, generated answers, and graders are other rules, and each can order
  the same models differently.
- **Generated answers checked by tests**, scored by **pass@k**: the chance that at least one of k
  samples passes.
- **Leak defences** against **contamination**: test text that leaked into training. Real leaks
  arrive as paraphrases, translations, and scraped mirrors. Private test sets close that path, and
  canary strings, unique markers planted in a test set, let you check later whether a model
  ingested it.
- **Applications**: regression suites that re-run a golden set (fixed cases with known good
  outcomes) on every change; task metrics for retrieval, agents, and safety; and LLM judges or
  human votes for free text.

### Why it matters

Training data, settings, and checkpoints are chosen by scores, so a misread score steers each of
those choices. Keep selecting by one benchmark and its score drifts away from the ability it was
meant to measure, even with no leak; that is **overfitting the benchmark**. In a product, scores
decide which change ships, and whatever they do not cover can get worse unnoticed.

### What you will be able to explain

- How an explicit scoring rule replaces an impression, and why changing the rule can reorder the
  same checkpoints.
- How to read a benchmark score together with its confidence interval.
- How to recognize contamination from a benchmark gain out of proportion to the change on a
  **control**, an unseen corpus no leak touches.
- What to measure for an application: golden sets for regressions, task metrics for retrieval,
  agents, and safety, and a judge checked against human labels.

### What the lab shows

Nothing on the page is a stored result. The lab trains its own character bigram at 3, 10, 30, and
80 epochs, one seed each, on the harbor corpus minus its last three sentences. Those sentences are
the held-out benchmark source, and the Proverbs corpus is the control. Log-probabilities,
**margins** (correct score minus distractor score), and perplexities are measured; standard
errors, 95% intervals, and **Items for ±5 pts** are closed-form.

- **Benchmark editor** holds the items, one per line as `context | correct | plausible distractor`
  (six fixed defaults you can rewrite), and **Training budget multiplier**, which scales all four
  budgets.
- **Leaderboard** ranks the four checkpoints under **Scoring rule**: Length-normalised, Raw
  log-probability, or Sample and check. Changing the rule re-scores them without retraining.
  Sample and check adds **Attempts allowed (k)** and makes 20 seeded draws per item, each a choice
  between the two options rather than free text.
- **Item-level results** shows every item's margin for the largest checkpoint, or its draw count
  and pass@k under Sample and check.
- **Contamination control** holds the switch **Add the benchmark source sentences to the corpus**,
  which appends the three held-out sentences to the training text.

The key experiment: under the default rule, press that switch, then compare **Margin shift** with
**Control shift**. The benchmark moves far more than general text does. Nothing in the application
half of the lesson runs here.

## What it is

A benchmark is a **scoring rule** applied to a **held-out set**, and a score is an estimate with
an error bar. This lab makes all three visible, and lets you change the rule.

The default rule is **likelihood scoring**. Each line in **Benchmark editor** is
`context | correct | plausible distractor`. The model scores the continuation “ candidate” —
the space plus every character — and the candidate with the higher mean log-probability per
character wins. Nothing is generated, so decoding settings cannot move the result.

Dividing by length is **length normalization**. Raw log-probability favours the shorter
candidate, because every extra character multiplies in another probability below one. Dividing
by the continuation's length in bytes is the idea behind lm-evaluation-harness's `acc_norm`.

### Three scoring rules

**Scoring rule** re-scores the same trained checkpoints without retraining them:

- **Length-normalised** is the default above: mean log-probability per character.
- **Raw log-probability** compares totals instead of means.
- **Sample and check** has each checkpoint answer by sampling one of the two options in
  proportion to the probability it gives each full continuation, 20 seeded draws per item. An
  item scores **pass@k**: the chance that at least one of k attempts picks the key, estimated
  from those draws.

### The held-out half

The items were written from the last three sentences of the harbor corpus, and those sentences
are removed from training. The Proverbs corpus is the **control**: unrelated text no leak
touches. Four checkpoints train for 3, 10, 30, and 80 epochs, scaled by **Training budget
multiplier**. A model that learned nothing scores `ln(30) = 3.401` nats per character, a
**perplexity** of 30.

### The error bar

With `n` items and pass rate `p`, the standard error is `√(p(1 − p)/n)`. Six items at 50% give
±20 points. The **Leaderboard** draws each pass rate with a 95% **confidence interval**
(Wilson's, which unlike `p ± 1.96·SE` does not collapse at 0/6 or 6/6), and each mean
**margin** with a t interval.

## Why it’s here

Every capability claim is a held-out measurement, and it means something only if the held-out
text was absent from training and the sample of items was large enough to separate the models.
Neither condition shows in the number itself, and neither does the rule that produced it. Here
you can break all three on purpose.

## How to play with it

Read the six items first: every distractor is wrong but ordinary — “evening” against
“morning”, “trails” against “sails”. Then read the **Leaderboard** at the defaults. The 80- and
30-epoch checkpoints both pass 3 of 6, both with interval 19%–81%, and their mean margins are
0.012 and 0.011. The callout's paired difference is +0.002, with an interval from −0.220 to
+0.224.

Now change **Scoring rule**. Raw log-probability gives every checkpoint two more passes: 80
epochs goes from 3 of 6 to 5 of 6, and the 30-epoch checkpoint edges ahead of it. Sample and
check at Attempts allowed 1 puts the 10-epoch checkpoint first and the 80-epoch one third.
Raise Attempts to 5 and the 3-epoch checkpoint leads at 94% while the 80-epoch one is last at
83%.

Open **Item-level results**. At 80 epochs “the boats stay in | port | town” fails by −0.840:
after a space, this bigram expects “t”. “the water stays | warm | wide” passes by +1.213 on the
letters inside the two words.

Then press **Add the benchmark source sentences to the corpus** and compare **Margin shift**
with **Control shift**.

## What to notice

- Six items cannot rank these checkpoints. All three other intervals overlap the leader's, and
  a ±5-point interval at 50% would need 385 items.
- The rule is a design decision. The two extra raw passes per checkpoint come from the two
  items whose correct answer is shorter, “sails” against “trails” and “repeats” against
  “retreats”. No checkpoint learned anything between the two views.
- **Pass@k** measures coverage. The 3-epoch checkpoint spreads probability over both options,
  so a few attempts nearly always include the key. The 80-epoch one is confidently wrong on
  “port” against “town” and stays near zero on it however many attempts it gets.
- Under all three rules every interval still overlaps the leader's. Changing the rule moves the
  ranking by more than the data can resolve.
- A paired comparison is sharper than two separate intervals: both checkpoints answer the same
  items, so item difficulty cancels. Here it still cannot separate the top two.
- **Contamination** moves the benchmark hard. At 80 epochs the leak adds +0.519 margin per item
  (interval +0.220 to +0.817), takes the leaderboard to 6 of 6, and drops benchmark perplexity
  from 9.33 to 5.83. At 30 epochs, 3/6 becomes 5/6 and perplexity falls from 10.08 to 6.65.
- The control moves too, by −2.8% at 80 epochs and −5.7% at 30. The leak added real English.
  The signature is the disproportion, not a frozen control.
- At 6 of 6, **Leader's standard error** reads ±0.0 points. Six perfect answers do not make a
  certain score; the Wilson interval still runs from 61% to 100%.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Training, scoring, perplexities, and intervals are all computed. The model is
a bigram that sees one character of context, so “water” and “harbor” are invisible to it; a pass
is not comprehension. Each checkpoint is one run at one seed, so the intervals cover the choice
of items, not training noise. The leak is a verbatim copy, the easiest kind to catch.

Sample and check draws a choice between the two options, not free text, because this bigram
almost never writes a whole target word by chance. Its pass@k therefore measures coverage over
two options. The 20 draws per item add sampling noise that the interval does not include.

**In real systems.** Leaks arrive as paraphrases, translations, forum answers quoting the test,
and scraped mirrors; exact-match and n-gram checks miss most of them. **Overfitting the
benchmark** needs no leak at all: choosing data, settings, and checkpoints by one score slowly
fits the benchmark's quirks.

You have just seen the scoring rule change a ranking. Lettered-option prompts, generated answers,
and graders each order the same models differently again, so a number means something only with
its rule, harness, and version beside it.

## Going deeper

### Evaluating applications, not just models

A benchmark scores a model on fixed questions. A product is a model plus retrieval, prompts,
tools, and guardrails, and each piece can fail alone. The leaderboard's habits carry over:
fixed cases, a named rule, and an interval on every rate. Nothing in this section runs in this lab; it is
the mechanism for what you would build around it.

**Golden sets and regression suites.** A **golden set** is a fixed list of cases with known good
outcomes, taken from real traffic and past failures. A **regression suite** re-runs it on every
change to the model, prompt, retriever, or tools, so a fix in one place cannot silently break
another. Size still limits resolution: 27 passes out of 30 is 90%, with a Wilson interval from
74% to 97%. Compare runs on the same cases, as the paired callout does.

**Retrieval pipelines.** Score the stages separately. *Context recall* asks what share of the
facts needed to answer reached the prompt. *Faithfulness* asks whether each claim in the answer
is supported by the retrieved text. *Answer correctness* asks whether the final answer matches
the key. Full recall with a wrong answer is a generator failure; low recall is a retrieval
failure. The Retrieval-augmented generation lab computes context recall, correctness, and a crude
support check that stands in for faithfulness, on five questions.

**Agents.** Score the outcome and the path: *task success rate* over a fixed task set, checked
on the end state; *steps* taken; and *cost* in tokens, tool calls, or time. Success without cost
hides a looping agent, and cost without success rewards giving up. Runs vary, so repeat each
task and report the rate with an interval.

**Safety.** Run a fixed suite of attack prompts and report the **attack success rate**: the share
that elicit the prohibited behaviour. Pair it with the *false-refusal rate*, the share of
ordinary requests wrongly refused, because a model that refuses everything scores 0% on the
first. Neither covers attacks outside the suite.

**LLM-as-judge.** When answers are free text, a second model grades them against a rubric. That
is cheap and scalable, and **LLM-as-judge** is biased toward the first-listed answer, longer
answers, and its own style. To check one, have people label a sample, measure how often the judge
agrees with them against how often two people agree, swap the answer order, and see whether
verdicts flip. Trust it only where it agrees about as well as humans do.

**Human and arena evaluation.** In **human evaluation**, people compare two anonymous answers,
and pairwise votes become Elo ratings. After each vote the winner gains `K × (1 − E)`, where `E`
is the win chance the ratings predicted, `1 / (1 + 10^((rB − rA)/400))`. With equal ratings and
K = 32 the winner gains 16 points. A 400-point favourite who wins gains under 3. People catch
what rules miss; they also prefer length, confidence, and formatting.

### Scoring generated answers

When answers are generated and checked by tests, success depends on the sampling budget.
**Pass@k** is the chance at least one of k samples passes. From n samples with c passing, the
unbiased estimate is `1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)`. With n = 10, c = 3, k = 5 it is 0.917; the
plug-in `1 − (1 − c/n)^k` gives 0.832. The Reasoning models lab plots this ceiling.

### Keeping benchmarks honest

Private test sets never publish labels, which closes the leak path and costs reproducibility.
Canary strings planted in a test set let you check later whether a model ingested it. Harness
name and version belong next to any number, because rule variants are not comparable. And
report intervals: a gap smaller than about two standard errors is not evidence.

Neither defence helps a saturated benchmark. Once the best models cluster near the ceiling, the
remaining items are mostly ambiguous or mislabelled, and the ranking measures label noise. That
is when a benchmark is retired.
