## The big picture

Picture two friends arguing about which of them is better at trivia. The fair way to settle it is a quiz. A quiz only settles anything if the questions are new to both of them. It also needs enough questions to rule out a lucky guess. And both friends must agree, before they start, how the answers will be marked.

An AI model learns by reading huge amounts of example text, and this is called **training**. People compare AI models the way the two friends compare themselves, using fixed tests called **benchmarks**. A fair test uses questions the model never read during training. This module shows how those tests turn a model's answers into a score. It also shows how a score can mislead you even when nobody cheats.

### Where you have met it

Whenever you read that one AI model beats another, you are reading a benchmark score. The same goes for a claim that a new model scored higher than an older one. Behind each claim sit three things. One is a list of questions the model was never supposed to see. Another is a rule for marking its answers, and the third is a score worked out from both.

The same habits carry over to whole apps built around a model. One example is an assistant that searches through documents before it answers. Another is an assistant that carries out a task step by step, called an **agent**. These apps need careful tests too, and the last part of this lesson shows what to measure for them.

### Why it matters

A test score looks like a plain fact, but the number alone never tells you how it was made. Without careful tests, nobody could tell whether a new model is really better. They could not tell whether it got lucky on a short test. They also could not tell whether it saw the test during training.

Each of those problems can make a weaker model look stronger, and the score hides all of them. The lab lets you cause each problem on purpose and watch the numbers change. Then you know what to check the next time someone shows you a ranking.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- How a model gets a score from a clearly stated marking rule, not someone's impression. Also, why a new rule can change who comes first.
- How to read a score together with the range it could really fall in. Also, why six questions cannot tell two close models apart.
- How to spot a test that leaked into training. You compare the jump in the test score with the movement of a score on unrelated text.
- What to measure when you test a whole app. That means a fixed set of known cases rerun after every change. It also means task scores for search, agents and safety, and an AI grader checked against people.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab tests a tiny model that really trains on a few sentences about a harbor. Every score, chart and range you see comes from that model's real output. Two parts are kept simple on purpose. The six test questions start out fixed, though you can rewrite them, and the leak is a word-for-word copy. That is simpler to spot than a real leak. The lab has four cards:

- **Leaderboard** ranks four versions of the model and draws a range beside every score. Its **Scoring rule** control changes the marking rule.
- **Item-level results** shows how the most-trained version did on each question, and by how much it passed or failed.
- **Benchmark editor** holds the six test questions, and you can read and rewrite them. It also has a slider that sets how long the four versions train.
- **Contamination control** has a switch that leaks the test's source sentences into the training text. You can then see what a leak does to every score.

## What it is

### The model on test

The lab trains a very small language model. This is a program that learns to guess the next character of a text. A character here means one letter, space or full stop, so “the fog” is seven characters. The model learns from a handful of sentences about harbor weather. One example is “the morning fog settles over the harbor and the air feels cool.”

This model looks back only one character when it guesses. After a space, the only clue it can use is that space. Nothing inside it stands for what a harbor is or what a sentence means. That matters later, when you see which questions it gets wrong.

The lab saves four versions of the model, and each one trains for a different amount of time. Training time is counted in **epochs**, and one epoch is one full pass through the training text. The four budgets are 3, 10, 30 and 80 epochs. Each saved version is called a **checkpoint**, and the Leaderboard uses that word for its rows.

The **Training budget multiplier** slider in Benchmark editor stretches or shrinks all four budgets together. It runs from a quarter of the default length up to three times it. It starts at 1.00×, which leaves the four budgets as they are.

### A test made of two-ending questions

In this lab, a benchmark is a short list of fill-in-the-blank questions, and each question is called an **item**. Each item is one line in **Benchmark editor**, written in the form `context | correct | plausible distractor`. That means the start of a sentence, then the right ending, then a wrong ending chosen to sound believable. That wrong ending is a **distractor**.

Here is one of the six default items: `the fog returns in the | morning | evening`. The word “evening” is wrong only because the harbor notes say the fog comes back in the morning. It is an ordinary word that shares many letter patterns with “morning”. So a model that has not learned this text has no clear way to tell the two apart.

This lab's model learns which letters tend to follow which, and that is all. It cannot connect the fog with the morning. So even when it passes this item, the pass comes from letter patterns, as Item-level results will show.

### Likelihood scoring: rating the endings instead of writing one

The model never writes an answer on this test. Instead, the lab asks how likely the model would be to produce each ending after the context. The more likely ending wins, and this way of marking is called **likelihood scoring**. Nothing gets written, so the random settings that make a chatbot's replies vary cannot change the result.

Rating an ending works one character at a time. The model gives each character a chance between 0 and 1 of coming next. The chance of the whole ending is all of those chances multiplied together. Every chance is below 1, so the product shrinks with each added character. It soon becomes a very tiny number.

To keep those numbers manageable, scorers turn each chance into a **log-probability**. You do not need to know how the conversion works, only three facts about it. A chance of 1 becomes 0, and smaller chances become negative numbers that sit further below zero. For example, 0.5 becomes about −0.7, and 0.1 becomes about −2.3. Adding log-probabilities does the same job as multiplying the chances.

The lab scores every ending with the space in front of it. So “ morning” counts as eight characters, which is the space plus seven letters. Its total log-probability is the sum of eight negative numbers, one for each character. A total closer to zero means a more likely ending.

### Why length gets in the way

Here is the catch. A longer ending adds up more negative numbers, so its total sinks lower even if every guess was good. Picture a made-up model that gives every character in two endings the same log-probability of −2. The ending “ sails” has six characters once you count its space, so its total is −12.

The ending “ trails” has seven characters, so its total is −14. Comparing totals hands the win to “sails”, even though this made-up model rated every character equally well. The shorter ending won for no reason except that it was shorter.

The fix is to divide each total by its number of characters, which gives the average log-probability per character. In the example both averages come out at −2, so length gives neither ending a head start. The winner has to earn it character by character. Dividing by length like this is called **length normalization**, and it is the lab's default rule.

### The margin: how close was the call?

A pass or a fail hides how close the call was. So the lab also shows each item's **margin**, which is the right ending's score minus the wrong ending's score. A positive margin means the item passed, and a negative margin means it failed.

The scores are negative numbers, so take care with the subtraction. Say the right ending averages −2.0 per character and the wrong one averages −2.5. Then the margin is −2.0 − (−2.5) = +0.5, so the item passes. The further the margin sits from zero, the less close the call was. A pass by 0.01 and a pass by 1.2 count the same in a pass count. As margins, they look very different.

### The scoring rule is a choice

Turning a model's output into a number takes a rule. The rule someone picks is called a **scoring rule**. A person makes this decision, and it is not a fact about the model. The **Scoring rule** control on the Leaderboard offers three rules:

- **Length-normalised** is the default. It compares each ending's average log-probability per character, so a long ending is not punished for its length.
- **Raw log-probability** compares the totals instead, which hands the shorter ending a head start whatever the model has learned.
- **Sample and check** makes each checkpoint pick an ending at random, weighted by how likely it rates each one. Then the lab checks whether the pick was right.

Switching the rule marks the same four checkpoints a second time, without training them again. Nothing inside the models changes, so any change in the ranking comes from the rule alone.

### Sample and check, and pass@k

Under Sample and check, a checkpoint picks between the two endings the way a weighted coin lands. Say it rates the whole right ending three times as likely as the wrong one. Then it picks the right ending about 3 times in 4. The weights come from the whole ending's chance, the same total that Raw log-probability compares. So a shorter right ending gets the same head start here. The lab records 20 picks for every item and reuses the same random numbers each time. So the results come out the same whenever you look.

The **Attempts allowed (k)** slider appears once you choose Sample and check. It runs from 1 to 10 and sets how many tries each checkpoint gets at every item. An item then scores **pass@k**, the chance that at least one of k tries picks the right ending. So pass@1 is the chance of being right on the first go.

To try the idea with small numbers, suppose a checkpoint picks the right ending half the time. Say it gets two tries, so it misses both with a chance of 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. So its pass@2 is 1 − 0.25 = 0.75, or 75%. The lab works out pass@k from the 20 picks it recorded, not with this shortcut. It still estimates the same chance.

### The held-out set and the control

A test only measures something if the model has not seen it. The six items were written from the last three sentences of the harbor notes. The lab removes those sentences before training, so no checkpoint has seen them. Text kept out of training so it can be used for testing is called a **held-out set**. You can read these three sentences in **Contamination control**.

The lab also keeps a second piece of text away from training. It is ten proverbs, such as “the early bird gets the worm”, that have nothing to do with harbors. That text is the **control**, a yardstick that shares nothing with the test. A leak of the test copies none of it into training. A real improvement shows up on the control too, roughly in proportion. Suppose the test score jumps far more than the control moves. Then suspect that the test leaked, even when the control improves a little.

Benchmark editor counts the characters in all three texts, under **Training characters**, **Benchmark source** and **Control**. These are three different pieces of text, and only the training text is trained on. When you switch on the leak, the Training characters count grows, because the three source sentences join it.

### Perplexity: how well does the model fit a passage?

Pass counts only look at six items. So the Leaderboard also measures how well each checkpoint fits whole passages of text. It uses **perplexity**. Read it as the number of equally likely characters the model is choosing between at each step. Lower is better, because a lower number means the model has narrowed down its guesses.

The lab's model can write 30 different characters. These are the 26 letters, a space, a full stop and two special markers. A model that learned nothing gives all of them the same chance. It scores a perplexity of 30, like a blind guess every time. A perplexity of 9 means the model is as unsure as if it chose from 9 equally likely characters.

The table shows two of these numbers for each checkpoint. **Benchmark perplexity** is measured on the three held-out sentences, and **Control perplexity** is measured on the proverbs.

### How sure is a score?

Picture a coin that you flip six times and that lands heads three times. That does not prove the coin is fair. A coin weighted to land heads only 30% of the time could also give three heads in six flips. So could a coin weighted toward 70% heads, because six flips cannot tell any of these coins apart.

Six test items work like six flips. So a score of 3 out of 6 tells you much less than it seems to. The lab measures that wobble with the **standard error**. Its formula appears inside the callout at the bottom of the Leaderboard: `√(p(1 − p)/n)`. In it, p is the pass rate and n is the number of items.

For 3 passes out of 6, p is 0.5, so p(1 − p) is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. Dividing by 6 gives about 0.042, and its square root is about 0.20. That is 20 percentage points, which is roughly what **Leader's standard error** reads at the start. So the leader's score is 50%, give or take about 20 points.

The lab turns that wobble into a range. First, a checkpoint's **true pass rate** is the share it would pass on a huge number of similar items. No six-item test can show you that directly. Instead, picture giving the same checkpoint 100 different six-item tests and drawing a range from each one. About 95 of those 100 ranges would contain the true pass rate. A range built this way is called a 95% **confidence interval**.

A rough version of that range stretches two standard errors either side of the score. For 3 out of 6, it runs from about 10% to 90%. With so few items, that rule is only a rough guide. So the chart draws a more careful version called the Wilson interval, which stays sensible near 0% and 100%. For 3 out of 6, it runs from 19% to 81%, and that is the range to read. Either way, a checkpoint passing 3 of 6 could be weak or could be strong.

### Comparing two checkpoints item by item

Here the coin picture stops matching, because coin flips are all alike and test items are not. Some items are much harder than others. That difference between items makes an item-by-item comparison useful, since both checkpoints answer the same six items.

Picture two friends who take the same quiz. Scored separately, each friend's total wobbles with luck. For example, this quiz may have happened to be full of simple or tough questions. That wobble widens each friend's own range. Compare them question by question instead, and a question both of them miss adds nothing to the difference. Only the questions where they disagree count. So the range for that difference is usually much narrower than the two separate ranges would suggest.

The lab goes one step further than right or wrong, because it compares margins. That means every item adds a small difference, not just the ones where the two disagree. An item that is hard for both checkpoints still pulls both margins down by about the same amount. So most of its difficulty cancels out in the subtraction.

The callout at the bottom of the Leaderboard does exactly this. It subtracts the runner-up's margin from the leader's on every item. Under Sample and check it subtracts each item's pass@k instead, because that rule scores items by pass@k. Then it averages the six differences and gives that average its own 95% range. Suppose that range runs from below zero to above zero. Then the items cannot say which of the two is better.

## Why it’s here

Every claim about what a model can do comes down to a score on a test. That score means something only if three things are true. The test text has to be truly absent from training. There have to be enough items to separate the models. And the scoring rule has to be stated, so others can use the same one. None of these three conditions shows up in the score.

Picture a reading test built on a story that nobody in the class has read. It checks how well students understand writing that is new to them. Now say one student happens to have read that exact story at home. Their score jumps, even though their reading has not improved at all. The test has quietly stopped measuring what it was built to measure.

A second test on a different unread story would reveal the problem. That student's score there would stay about where it was. That second test plays the same part as the lab's control. In AI this problem is called **contamination**. It means test text, or the text a test was written from, ends up in a model's training data.

The picture stops matching in one way: reading one story does not make a student better at other stories. But a model trained on leaked text usually gets a little better at unrelated text too. So the lab's control moves slightly. The warning sign is the size of the gap between the two scores. It is not a control that stays perfectly still.

The other two conditions fail just as quietly. With only a few questions, luck decides close contests. A two-question quiz could rank a lucky guesser above someone who studied. A marking rule that favours short answers can also reorder a ranking without anyone getting better or worse.

## How to play with it

The lab's five steps take you through it in order, and each part below matches one of those steps.

### 1. Write the items

Before you write an item, open **Benchmark editor** and read the six default items in the Benchmark items box:

```
in winter the same harbor | freezes | flowers
the boats stay in | port | town
in summer the harbor fills with | sails | trails
the water stays | warm | wide
the fog returns in the | morning | evening
and the pattern | repeats | retreats
```

Every wrong ending is wrong but ordinary, such as “evening” against “morning” or “trails” against “sails”. Each one is a real word that could fit the sentence. To pick the right ending reliably, a checkpoint has to learn something from the harbor text. Even letter patterns count as something.

To see why that matters, change `morning | evening` to `morning | xyzzy` in the box. The fog item now passes by a wide margin. This model only knows which letter tends to follow which. Pairs like “xy” and “zz” never appear in the training text. An item with a nonsense distractor stops testing anything, so change it back before you go on.

When you write an item of your own, take it from the three held-out sentences. An item written from the other harbor sentences is contaminated from the start. Every checkpoint trained on those sentences.

### 2. Read the leaderboard

Now look at **Leaderboard** at its starting settings, where the chart has one row per checkpoint. A dot marks its pass rate, and a line shows its 95% range. The leader's range is shaded down the whole chart. That way you can see at a glance which other rows fall inside it. The table below the chart gives the same results as numbers, along with each checkpoint's two perplexities.

The 80-epoch and 30-epoch checkpoints both pass 3 of 6 items. Both of their ranges run from 19% to 81%. The 80-epoch checkpoint sits on top only because its average margin is a tiny bit larger. You can read this in the **Mean margin** column: 0.012 against 0.011.

The callout under the table pairs the leader's margin with the runner-up's on the same six items. The gap averages +0.002, and its 95% interval runs from −0.220 to +0.224, which crosses zero. So these six items cannot say which checkpoint is better. This test does not settle the order of the top two.

### 3. Change the scoring rule

**Try this:** switch **Scoring rule** from Length-normalised to Raw log-probability. Every checkpoint gains two passes, and the 80-epoch checkpoint jumps from 3 of 6 to 5 of 6. The 30-epoch checkpoint is also at 5 of 6. It now edges ahead of the 80-epoch one on mean margin, though neither one was trained again.

Next choose Sample and check, with **Attempts allowed (k)** at 1. The 10-epoch checkpoint moves into first place, the 80-epoch one drops to third, and the 3-epoch one comes last. Now drag **Attempts allowed (k)** to 5. The 3-epoch checkpoint, which trained least of all, takes the lead at 94%. Meanwhile the 80-epoch one falls to last place at 83%.

Notice what happened there: the checkpoints and the six items never changed. Yet each of the four checkpoints has now had a turn in first place. The only thing that moved was the rule you picked.

### 4. Inspect the items

Open **Item-level results**, which always shows the most-trained checkpoint. That is the 80-epoch one, unless you move the multiplier. Each row shows both endings' scores and the margin. A bar grows right of centre for a pass and left of centre for a fail.

This card follows whichever rule is chosen, so set **Scoring rule** back to Length-normalised first. Then find “the boats stay in | port | town”, which fails by −0.840. Both endings start with a space. In the harbor notes, a space is most often followed by “t”, as in “the”. So “ town” collects a high score on its first letter. This model also has no way to link “port” with boats, because it only ever looks one character back.

Now find “the water stays | warm | wide”, which passes by +1.213, the biggest margin of the six. That win comes from the letters inside “warm” and “wide”, not from any link between water and warmth. A big margin is not the same thing as understanding.

### 5. Contaminate it

Finally, look at **Contamination control** and read its two readouts, **Margin shift** and **Control shift**, before you press anything. The lab always trains every checkpoint twice, once with the leak and once without. So these readouts compare the two versions whichever way the switch is set.

Now press **Add the benchmark source sentences to the corpus**. Its chip changes from “held out” to “leaking”, and the Leaderboard's badge changes from “clean” to “contaminated”. The scores now come from checkpoints trained with the three source sentences added. The questions themselves never enter training, only the sentences they were written from. Real models train on huge amounts of text collected from websites. A web page that copies a test's source text can leak a public test in the same way.

Then compare how far each score moved, measured against where it started. Margin shift reads +0.519, so the 80-epoch row's **Mean margin** climbs from 0.012 to about 0.53. That is more than forty times what it was. Control shift reads −2.8%, a change of under 3 in every 100.

To compare like with like, look at the 80-epoch row's two perplexities in the table. Benchmark perplexity falls from 9.33 to 5.83, a drop of more than a third. Control perplexity drops by that same 2.8%. The test moved far more than the control did, and that lopsided gap is the sign of a leak.

## What to notice

- **Six items cannot rank these checkpoints.** Even the paired range for the top two crosses zero. All three other ranges overlap the leader's, and **Intervals overlapping the leader's** counts them. To pin a score at 50% down to within 5 points either way, you would need 385 items. The **Items for ±5 pts** readout shows that number.
- **The rule is a choice, not a discovery.** The two extra passes under Raw log-probability come from the two items whose right ending is shorter. Those are “sails” against “trails”, and “repeats” against “retreats”. No checkpoint learned anything between the two views, because the rule handed the shorter endings a head start.
- **Pass@k rewards a checkpoint that spreads its chances.** The 3-epoch checkpoint has trained so little that it gives both endings a fair share of chance. So a few tries nearly always include the right one. The 80-epoch checkpoint is confidently wrong on “port”, because it gives nearly all its chance to “town”.
- **Extra tries cannot fix a confident mistake.** None of the 80-epoch checkpoint's 20 picks lands on “port”. So its pass@k on that item stays at zero, however many tries you allow.
- **No rule settles the ranking**, because under all three rules every other range still overlaps the leader's. Changing the rule reshuffles the ranking, and six items are too few to say which order is right.
- **Pairing helps, but not enough here.** Both checkpoints answer the same items, so a hard item counts against both and cancels out. Even so, the paired range for the top two still crosses zero.
- **A leak moves the test a lot.** At 80 epochs it raises the margin by +0.519 per item, and that checkpoint passes 6 of 6. The Item-level results callout gives that rise a 95% range of +0.220 to +0.817. That is well clear of zero, so the jump is very unlikely to be luck.
- **A less-trained checkpoint gains too.** At 30 epochs, 3 of 6 becomes 5 of 6. Its benchmark perplexity drops from 10.08 to 6.65. On the test text, it went from choosing among about ten characters to fewer than seven.
- **The control moves a little as well.** Control perplexity falls by 2.8% at 80 epochs. The leaked sentences are ordinary English, so they help a little with any English text. So the sign of a leak is not a frozen control. It is the huge difference in size between the two shifts.
- **A perfect score is not a certain score.** With the leak on, the leader passes 6 of 6 and **Leader's standard error** reads ± 0.0 pts. That is because p(1 − p) is 1 × 0 = 0, so the simple formula claims total certainty. The drawn range still runs from 61% to 100%.

**A common mix-up:** a higher place on the leaderboard means a better model. Here the top two are separated by +0.002, and the range crosses zero. A different scoring rule puts them in a different order. A range that crosses zero does not mean the two are equally good either. It only means these items cannot tell. A rank printed without its range and its rule can be noise.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

The lab really computes the training, the scoring, the perplexities and the ranges from the checkpoints' output. Nothing on the Leaderboard is made up, and the simplifications are in what gets tested and how:

- The model looks back only one character. So words like “water” and “harbor” do not exist for it as words. When it passes an item, it follows patterns of letter pairs, which is not understanding.
- Each checkpoint comes from one training run with fixed random choices. Its ranges cover which items happened to be picked, but not the luck of training. A second run with different random choices could shift every score.
- The leak is a word-for-word copy of three known sentences, which is the simplest kind of leak to catch.
- Sample and check picks between two endings instead of writing free text. This model almost never writes a whole target word by chance. So its pass@k measures how often a few tries cover the right one of only two choices.
- The 20 picks per item under Sample and check add their own luck. The drawn ranges do not include it.
- Benchmark editor scores only the first 60 lines and cuts each line at 160 characters. That way a pasted test cannot freeze the page.

### Where real benchmarks fail

Real leaks rarely arrive as exact copies. Instead they come as rewordings, translations and forum posts that discuss the test. They also come as copies of the test posted on other websites. Checks that hunt for exact copies or matching runs of words miss most of them.

A benchmark can also be fooled with no leak at all. Say a team keeps choosing its data, settings and checkpoints by whichever gives the best score on one test. The model slowly fits that test's quirks. The score then climbs faster than the ability it was meant to measure. This is called overfitting the benchmark.

You saw one rule reorder a leaderboard, and real tests come in more variations than that. Questions with lettered options can order the same models differently again. So can written answers checked for an exact match, and so can grading by another model. So a score means something only when three things sit beside it. They are its rule, the testing program that ran it, and that program's version.

## Going deeper

### Evaluating applications, not only models

A benchmark scores a model on fixed questions, but a real product is a model plus other parts. Those can include a search step that finds documents, tools the model can use, and safety rules. The text the model receives for each request is called the **prompt**. Any one of these parts can fail on its own. That includes the search step, the tools, the safety rules and the prompt.

The habits from the lab carry over: fixed cases, a named rule, and a range on every rate. Nothing in this section runs in the lab, because it describes what you would build around a model.

### Golden sets and regression suites

A **golden set** is a fixed list of cases with known good results. The cases come from real use and from past failures. It never changes, so scores from different versions of a system can be compared fairly.

Sometimes something that used to work stops working after a change, and that step backward is called a **regression**. A **regression suite** reruns the golden set after every change to the model, prompt, search step or tools. A fix in one place then cannot quietly break another. Picture a homework-help assistant. A change that improves its maths answers should not quietly break its spelling answers. The suite would catch it if it did.

Size still limits what a golden set can show. Suppose an assistant passes 27 of its 30 golden cases, a score of 90%. The 95% range for that score runs from 74% to 97%. So a small drop between two versions may be luck and not a real step backward. That is why you compare runs on the same cases, as the lab's paired callout does.

### Search-based assistants

Some assistants search a set of documents first and then write an answer from what they found. You should grade those two stages separately:

- *Context recall* asks how many of the facts needed for the answer made it into the prompt.
- *Faithfulness* asks whether every claim in the answer is backed by the text the search found.
- *Answer correctness* asks whether the final answer matches the known right answer.

Picture a school-handbook assistant asked “When does the library close?” Context recall checks whether the search found the page that says the library closes at 4 pm. Faithfulness checks that the reply only says things that page supports. Answer correctness checks that the final reply really says 4 pm.

The search step is often called the **retriever**, and the writing step is called the **generator**. Suppose recall is full but the answer is wrong. Then the facts arrived and the generator failed to use them. If recall is low, the retriever is to blame, because the generator never saw the facts. The Retrieval-augmented generation lab in this app computes recall and correctness on five questions. It also runs a rough support check that stands in for faithfulness.

### Agents

An agent is a system where a model works through a task in steps. A step might be searching or using a tool, and the agent keeps going until the job is done. You grade an agent on three things, and the first is the *task success rate*. It asks whether the job got done, checked by looking at the end result.

The other two are how many *steps* it took and what it *cost*. You count cost in text processed, tool uses or time. Success without cost hides an agent that loops round and round before it finishes. Cost without success rewards an agent that gives up early. Runs vary from one try to the next, so repeat each task and report the rate with a range.

### Safety

Run a fixed set of prompts written to push the model into something it should refuse. Then report the **attack success rate**. This is the share of those prompts that get the forbidden behaviour from the model. Pair it with the *false-refusal rate*, which is the share of ordinary, harmless requests the model wrongly refuses.

A model that refuses everything scores 0% attack success and is useless, and only the second number reveals that. Neither number says anything about attacks that are not in the set. A kind of attack the set never tried can still work.

### Judges

When answers are free writing, a second model can grade them. It uses a written checklist of what a good answer needs, called a rubric. This is called **LLM-as-judge**, where LLM stands for large language model, the kind of model behind chat assistants. It is cheap and quick. But it tends to favour the answer shown first, longer answers, and answers in its own style.

Picture a friend who marks your essays and always gives the higher grade to the longer one. You would want to check that friend against a teacher before trusting the grades. An AI judge needs the same check, so have people label a sample of answers. Then compare how often the judge agrees with them against how often two people agree with each other.

Then swap the order of the two answers and see whether the judge's verdict flips. Trust the judge only where it agrees with people about as well as people agree with each other.

### People and arenas

In **human evaluation**, people read two anonymous answers and pick the better one. Websites that run these head-to-head votes are often called arenas. The votes become Elo ratings, a points system where each vote moves points from the loser to the winner. The winner gains `K × (1 − E)` points. Here K is the most points a single vote can move. E is the chance of winning that the ratings predicted beforehand.

To try the formula with real values, start with two equal ratings. Each side was predicted to win half the time, so E is 0.5. With K = 32, the winner gains 32 × 0.5 = 16 points. Now take a favourite rated 400 points higher, which was predicted to win about 91% of the time. So E is about 0.91, and 1 − E is about 0.09. A win earns it about 32 × 0.09, which is under 3 points.

An expected win barely moves the ratings, while an upset moves them a lot. People catch problems that fixed rules miss. But they also tend to prefer answers that are longer, more confident and neatly formatted.

### Scoring written answers

Sometimes a model writes answers and an automatic test checks them. Then success depends on how many tries you allow. **Pass@k** is again the chance that at least one of k tries passes.

Suppose a model wrote 10 answers and 3 of them passed. You do not need to run 5 fresh tries. You can estimate pass@5 from the 10 answers you already have. It is the chance that 5 answers picked at random from the 10 include at least one pass. The only way to miss is to pick all 5 from the 7 failures. There are 21 ways to choose 5 answers from those 7. There are 252 ways to choose 5 from all 10. You could check these counts by listing every choice, but the next step is what matters.

So the chance of missing is 21 in 252. That makes pass@5 equal to 1 − 21/252, which is about 0.917, or about 92%. The Reasoning models lab shows how this climbs as you allow more tries.

### The two kinds of range on the chart

The titles above the chart's two columns name the kind of range each one draws. Pass rates get a Wilson interval, which stays sensible at 0 of 6 and 6 of 6. At those scores, the simple formula shrinks to a range of zero width. Averages, such as the mean margin or the mean pass@k, get a t interval. That kind is built for averages of measurements and not for counts of passes.

### Keeping tests honest

- Private test sets never publish their answers, which closes the leak path. But outsiders then cannot rerun the test to check the results.
- Marker strings are unusual lines of text planted inside a test set. They let you check later whether a model trained on that test.
- The name and version of the testing program belong next to any score. Different versions of a rule do not give comparable numbers.
- Every score should come with a range. A gap smaller than about two standard errors is not evidence that one model is better.
- Once the best models all score near the top, the items they still miss are mostly unclear or mislabelled. The ranking then measures labelling mistakes more than skill, and that is when a benchmark gets retired.
