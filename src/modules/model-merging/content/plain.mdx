## The big picture

Picture a model, a computer program that learns from example text so it can guess what letter or
word comes next. Say it has already learned from one kind of text, such as a few lines about the
weather. Now picture two copies of it, where one copy practises on recipe steps and the other
practises on proverbs, short sayings such as “a stitch in time saves nine”.

Training a model a little further like this is called **fine-tuning**, and each copy is called a
fine-tune. Each fine-tune gets better at its own new job, but what you really want is one model that
can handle both jobs at once.

This module is about **model merging**, a way to build that one model with arithmetic instead of
more training. You work out what each round of extra training changed inside the model, combine
those two sets of changes, and add the result back onto the original. The whole merge takes only
seconds, and unlike training, it needs no training text at all.

### Where you have met it

Large language models, the kind of program behind a chatbot, are often adapted to a new job by
training them a little further on new text. Each adaptation leaves behind its own full set of
numbers, and people have built tools to combine several of those sets into one. One of them,
mergekit, is described by its makers as “a toolkit for merging pre-trained language models”.

The *LoRA & adapters* lab showed a cheaper way to adapt a model, in which each new job is stored as a
small add-on file called an adapter. An adapter’s changes can be merged with exactly the same
arithmetic, which is why software built for adapters offers the same merge methods you will try here.

### Why it matters

Without merging, there are two ordinary ways to get several skills out of one model, and both of
them cost something. You can keep a separate model for every skill and switch between them, or you
can gather all the training text and train again on all of it. Merging is a third route, which reuses
the training you have already done instead of repeating it.

The catch is that a merge is a guess, because nothing checks the combined numbers against either job
while the arithmetic runs. A skill can quietly get weaker in the process, and the only way to find out
is to test the merged model on every job. This lab runs those tests for you and shows exactly where
each merge loses ground, and why.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these three ideas in your own words:

- why a merge is arithmetic on the changes two fine-tunes made to one shared starting model, rather
  than a second round of training;
- how to tell apart two reasons a merge can score worse: each job’s own changes getting watered down,
  and one job’s changes truly getting in the other’s way;
- what three methods, called TIES, DARE and SLERP, each do differently from a plain average of the two
  sets of changes.

### What the lab lets you do

Every number in this lab comes from real training inside the app, using a very small model that
guesses the next letter of a text. The texts and training runs are fixed, and every score is measured on
the same text the models trained on. The only random part you can redraw is DARE’s, with **New
random mask**.

The lab has three cards, and each one shows a different part of
the story:

- **Task vectors** draws the two lists of changes as two coloured maps, and counts where they agree
  and where they pull in opposite directions.
- **Merge recipe** lets you pick one of five merge methods, prints the exact arithmetic with real
  numbers, and draws ten of the changes before and after the merge.
- **Two task losses** scores every merge on both jobs at once, compares it with the models trained
  for one job alone, and splits any lost skill into two causes.

There is one surprise to watch for: the plainest merge, an even average, ends up worse at recipes
than the recipe model and worse at proverbs than the proverb model. Yet in that same merge, each job’s changes actually helped the other
job, and working out how both can be true is the heart of this lesson.

## What it is

### The model: a table of 900 scores

The model in this lab reads one character and guesses which character comes next, using an alphabet
of 30 characters. Those 30 are a space, the 26 letters, a full stop and two bracket symbols that
never appear in the texts.

Everything the model has learned sits in one table, with a row for each character it might read and
a column for each character that might come next. Each cell holds a score, and a higher score makes
that pairing more likely. For example, the cell in row `h` and column `e` grows high when the training
text keeps putting an `e` after an `h`, as in “the”.

To make a guess, the model turns one row of scores into chances that add up to 100%, giving bigger
scores bigger chances. The raw scores, before they become chances, are called **logits**, which is
the word the lab uses on its maps.

The lab names each cell by its pair of characters, so `h→a` means the score for an `a` coming right
after an `h`. With 30 rows and 30 columns, the table holds 900 numbers, which are called **weights**,
and the lab’s formulas write the whole set as θ, the Greek letter theta.

### The starting model

The starting model, called the **base**, was trained on Harbor weather notes, which is eight short
lines about fog, wind, rain and boats. It went through that text 48 times, and one full pass over the
training text is called an **epoch**.

The lab measures how well a model fits a text with a **loss**, the average of how surprised the model
is by each real next character. If the model gave the real next character a high chance, say 90%, it
is barely surprised. If it gave it only 1%, it is very surprised, and the loss goes up.

A lower loss is better. The lab measures surprise in units called **nats**, so it reports each loss in
nats per character.

You do not need to know how a nat is defined, because one fact is enough to read the numbers. A model
spreading its guesses evenly over all 30 characters would score about 3.4, so anything below 3.4 means
the model has picked up real patterns from its training text. The base’s loss is 2.553 on Recipe
steps and 2.684 on Proverbs, two texts it never trained on, so it already beats even guessing on both.

### Fine-tuning leaves a list of changes

Next, two copies of the base are trained further, one on Recipe steps and one on Proverbs, for 20
epochs each. Recipe steps is eight lines such as “stir the onions until they turn soft and sweet”,
and Proverbs is ten sayings such as “the early bird gets the worm”.

This is the fine-tuning from the big picture, and the lab calls the two fine-tunes the
**specialists**, since each one is trained for a single job. After training, the recipe specialist’s
loss on recipes is 2.123, a clear drop from the base’s 2.553. In the same way, the proverb
specialist’s loss on proverbs is 2.169, down from the base’s 2.684.

Now compare each fine-tune with the base cell by cell. In each of the 900 cells of the table,
subtract the base’s score from the fine-tune’s score. This uses the numbers in the table, not the
losses you just read. That gives a list of 900 changes, one for each weight, telling you how
far training moved every score up or down.

A list of numbers like this is called a **vector**, so this list of changes is called the fine-tune’s
**task vector**. The **Task vectors** card writes it as `τ = θ_tuned − θ_base`, where τ, said “tau”,
stands for the task vector. The other two symbols, `θ_tuned` and `θ_base`, stand for the fine-tune’s
weights and the base’s weights, so the formula reads “tuned minus base”.

Here is one real cell, `h→a`, which is the score for an `a` coming right after an `h`. In the
proverbs, an `a` follows an `h` five times, in words such as “what”, “has” and “than”, while in the
recipe steps that never happens.

So the proverb fine-tune raised the `h→a` score by 1.18, and the recipe fine-tune lowered it by 0.89.
The two task vectors therefore hold +1.18 and −0.89 in that one spot, which is the kind of
disagreement this lesson keeps coming back to.

### Reading the two maps

The card draws each task vector as a 30 by 30 map, `τ recipes` and `τ proverbs`, with rows for the
previous character and columns for the next. A blue cell means the fine-tune raised that logit, a red
cell means it lowered it, and grey means the cell did not change, or changed too little to show.

The deeper the colour, the bigger the change, and both maps share one colour scale so that you can
compare them fairly. A few rows stay grey on both maps, namely the rows for `j`, `q`, `x`, `z` and
the two bracket symbols. None of those characters appears in either text, so training never gives
their rows any reason to move.

Every other row changes in all 30 cells, because training on a row nudges every score in it, raising
the real next characters and lowering the rest. Both texts use the same set of 24 characters, so both
fine-tunes change the same 24 rows. With 30 cells in each row, that makes 720 changed cells out of the
table’s 900.

### A picture to hold on to: two sound engineers

Picture a sound desk at a concert, with a long row of sliders that each set how loud one microphone
or instrument is. Say this desk has 900 sliders, and think of their starting positions as the base
model.

Two sound engineers each get an exact copy of that desk, and each one adjusts their copy for a
different song. When they finish, each engineer writes down how far they moved every slider, such as
“slider 3, up a lot” or “slider 7, down a little”. Each engineer’s list of moves is a task vector,
because it records the change from the starting desk rather than the final positions.

A merge takes a fresh copy of the original desk and applies some mix of the two lists of moves. The
simplest mix moves every slider by half of what the first engineer did, plus half of what the second
engineer did.

Where both engineers moved a slider the same way, the merge moves it that way too. Where one moved it
up and the other moved it down, the two moves partly cancel each other out. That clash is called a
**sign conflict**, because the two changes have opposite signs, one plus and one minus.

The picture stops matching in two places, and the first is about labels. On a real desk every slider
has a label, such as “drums”, so you can read what each move does. In this tiny model you can read
each number too, because each is the score for one pair of characters. In a big model, though, a
single number usually has no meaning that anyone could name.

The second difference is that on a desk each slider sets its own sound and nothing else. Here the 30
scores in a row share out 100% of the chances between them, so raising one score lowers the chances
of every other character in that row.

### Why both must start from the same base

The two lists of moves only make sense together because both engineers started from the same desk.
That requirement is called the **shared base**, which means every model in a merge must have been
fine-tuned from the same starting model. Then any one cell, say cell number 417, means the same thing
in both task vectors, so adding them cell by cell adds like to like.

Now picture two desks that were wired by different people, so slider 417 controls the drums on one
desk and the singer on the other. Adding the two engineers’ moves slider by slider would then mix up
sounds that have nothing to do with each other.

Bigger models have the same problem, because between their input and their output they have inner
parts called **hidden units**. Each hidden unit comes to respond to some pattern during training, and
nothing decides ahead of time which unit ends up with which pattern.

Before any training, a model’s numbers are set to random values, and training then adjusts them step
by step. So two models trained from different random starting points can do the same job with their
hidden units in a different order. Averaging them number by number would then mix unrelated units,
exactly like the two desks wired by different people.

Fine-tunes of one model avoid this problem, because both inherit the same wiring from their base and
usually move only a short way from it. Imagine sliding from one fine-tune’s weights to the other’s,
with every cell moving at the same steady pace.

Say one cell holds 1.0 in the first fine-tune and 3.0 in the second, which are made-up numbers. A
quarter of the way along, that cell holds 1.5, halfway it holds 2.0, and three quarters of the way it
holds 2.5. Every other cell slides from its first value to its second in the same way, at the same
time.

Now check the loss at each stop along the way. For fine-tunes of one model, the loss usually stays
low at every stop, with no hump in the middle where the model suddenly gets much worse. That property is called **linear mode connectivity**, and it is why averaging
fine-tunes of one model works at all. Two models trained from different random starts usually do not
have it, so somewhere between them the loss climbs.

### Five ways to combine the lists

The **Merge recipe** card offers five methods in its **Merge method** control, and all five start
from the same two task vectors. All five also end the same way: the combined list is added onto the
base, and the result is the model you would actually use.

The lab calls that result **Served weights**, and it prints the formula for it with the current
numbers filled in. The small line above the card’s title also says “no gradient step”, where a gradient step means one
small training update, so merging trains nothing at all.

#### Linear blend

`Linear blend` takes a weighted average of the two fine-tunes, which is an average where each one can
count for a different share. This is called **weight averaging**, and the **Blend t** slider sets the
proverbs’ share, so at 30% the slider reads “70% recipes · 30% proverbs”.

At 50% each list counts for exactly half, and the formula reads `θ = θ_base + 0.50·τ_recipes + 0.50·τ_proverbs`. In words, that means “the base, plus
half of the recipe list, plus half of the proverb list”.

For the `h→a` cell, half of −0.89 is about −0.45, and half of +1.18 is about +0.59. Added together,
they move that cell up by only about +0.14, so the average gives neither task much of what it wanted.

Because both fine-tunes share the base, averaging their task vectors gives exactly the same model as
averaging the two fine-tunes’ own weights. A saved copy of a model’s weights is called a
**checkpoint**, which is the word the lab uses when its formula note points this out. Here is why,
using a made-up base score of 2.00 for the `h→a` cell:

- the recipe fine-tune lowered that score by 0.89, so the recipe fine-tune holds 1.11 in the cell;
- the proverb fine-tune raised that score by 1.18, so the proverb fine-tune holds 3.18 in the cell;
- the average of those two scores is 2.145, which is the base’s 2.00 plus 0.145, the same as adding
  half of each change to the base.

#### Task arithmetic

`Task arithmetic` adds both lists at full strength and then scales the total by one number, written
λ, the Greek letter lambda. The **Scale λ** slider sets that number from 0 to 1.50, and adding task
vectors to a base like this is called **task arithmetic**.

At 1.00 the formula reads `θ = θ_base + 1.00·(τ_recipes + τ_proverbs)`, so the `h→a` cell moves by
−0.89 + 1.18, which is +0.29. At a Scale λ of 0.5 it gives exactly the same model as the 50/50
average, since half of the total is the same as half of each list.

#### TIES

`TIES` is short for **TIES-Merging**, which is named after its three steps of trim, elect sign and
merge:

1. **Trim:** keep only the biggest changes in each list, as a share set by the **Keep top** slider,
   and set every other change to zero. Size alone counts here, so −0.89 is a bigger change than +0.5.
2. **Elect:** for each cell, add up the kept changes from both lists, and whichever sign has the
   larger total wins that cell’s vote.
3. **Merge:** average only the kept changes that agree with the winning sign, and then scale the
   result by λ, the same as in task arithmetic.

In sound-desk terms, TIES checks which way each slider got the bigger total push, and then it uses
only the moves that went that way.

Take the `h→a` cell again, where both changes are big enough to survive a trim that keeps the top 20%
of each list. Their total is +0.29, so plus wins the vote, and the recipe list’s minus change is left
out of the average. Only the proverb change is left, so with **Scale λ** at 1.00 the merged cell gets
the full +1.18 instead of the average’s +0.14.

Where both lists agree and both changes survive the trim, TIES averages them, much as the plain blend
does. Beyond that, TIES differs from the plain blend in three ways:

- The trim throws away most of each list, so with **Keep top** at 20%, 678 of the 900 merged cells
  end up at zero.
- In a cell where only one list’s change survives the trim, that change goes in at full strength
  instead of half, and the lab has 84 cells like that.
- In a conflict cell where both changes survive, the losing side is left out instead of cancelling
  the winner, as `h→a` showed.

Those full-strength cells help explain why TIES with **Scale λ** at 1.00 lands close to plain task arithmetic
at the same setting, as you will see in the lab.

#### DARE

`DARE` stands for Drop And REscale, and it works by making a random draw for every change in each
list. Each change is thrown away, meaning set to zero, with a chance of p, which you set with the
**Drop rate p** slider. The changes that survive are then multiplied by `1 / (1 − p)` to make up for
the missing ones.

At p = 0.5, each change has a 50% chance of being dropped, and the multiplier is 1 divided by 0.5,
which is 2. So
every kept change is doubled, which is why the slider reads `50% dropped · ×2.00` at that setting.
After that, the two thinned lists are added together and scaled by λ, the same way task arithmetic
does it.

To see why doubling makes up for the losses on average, picture one change of +0.6, which is a
made-up size. Half the time it is dropped to zero, and half the time it is doubled to +1.2, so over
many draws it averages out to the original +0.6.

In any single merge, though, that change is either zero or +1.2, and never the +0.6 it started as.
The random choice of which changes to drop is called a **mask**, and the **New random mask** button
draws a fresh one each time you press it.

#### SLERP

`SLERP` stands for spherical linear interpolation, and it blends along a curve instead of a straight
line so that the blend does not shrink. To see what shrinking means here, you first need a way to
measure the length of a list of numbers.

Think of a list of two numbers, 3 and 4, as an arrow on graph paper that goes 3 squares across and 4
squares up. Its length is the square root of 3² + 4², which is the square root of 25, so the arrow is
5 squares long. That is the same rule you would use for the long side of a right-angled triangle.

The same rule works for 900 numbers: square each one, add up all the squares, and then take the
square root of the total. You cannot draw an arrow that needs 900 numbers to describe it, but its
length, and the angle between two such arrows, still work exactly as they do on paper.

The lab prints each task vector’s length under its map as `‖τ‖`, where the double bars mean “length
of”, and both lengths are about 10. The badge on **Task vectors** reads cosine 0.34, where the
**cosine** is a score for how closely two arrows point the same way.

A cosine of 1 means the same direction, 0 means a right angle, and −1 means opposite directions. So
a cosine of 0.34 means the two task vectors point partly the same way, at an angle of 69.9°, a little
less than a right angle. The cosine on the badge and that angle are the same fact written in two
different ways.

Now picture two friends who start at the same lamp post and each walk a little over 10 steps, in
directions about 70° apart. If you stand halfway along the straight line between where they stop, you
are only about 8.5 steps from the lamp post. That is because cutting across the corner brings you back
toward the start, which is exactly how an average shrinks.

If you walk around the lamp post instead, on a curve that keeps your distance, you stay a little over
10 steps away the whole time. A plain average takes the straight line between the two arrows, while
SLERP takes the curve.

In the lab, the halfway point of the plain average has a length of only 8.46, which is shorter than
either task vector. SLERP’s halfway point has a length of 10.32, and you can read both numbers on the
`‖τ_merged‖` badge of **Merge recipe**.

At the halfway point, SLERP points in exactly the same direction as the average, and the only
difference is that its arrow is longer. You can check this with the formula the lab prints, which
uses sin, the sine key on a calculator.

At **Blend t** 50%, the formula reads
`θ = θ_base + (sin(0.50Ω)·τ_recipes + sin(0.50Ω)·τ_proverbs) / sin Ω`. Here Ω, the Greek letter
omega, stands for the angle between the two lists, which is 69.9°, so half of it is 34.95°.

Each list is multiplied by sin 34.95°, which a calculator gives as about 0.57. The total is then
divided by sin 69.9°, which is about 0.94. Since 0.57 divided by 0.94 is about 0.61, SLERP takes about
0.61 of each list.

The plain average takes only 0.50 of each list, and that bigger share is what keeps SLERP’s arrow
long. So at the halfway point, SLERP gives the same model as `Task arithmetic` with **Scale λ** at
about 0.61, and the curve does nothing more than set that bigger share. SLERP can only combine two lists at a time, so it cannot merge three models in one go.

## Why it’s here

As the big picture said, merging gives you one fixed model with both skills from two fine-tunes you
already have. It works even when you no longer have the text to train again.

The trouble is that nothing tunes the merged numbers for either job, so whether both skills survive
the arithmetic has to be measured. Measuring has a trap in it, though, because a merged model can
score worse than a specialist for two quite different reasons.

The first reason is **shrinking**, which happens when a method uses less than all of each job’s own
list. The 50/50 average, for example, uses only half of the recipe list, so the recipe skill gets
watered down even if the proverbs did nothing.

The second reason is **interference**, which is the harm one job’s list does to the other job when
both lists are added together. That is the real clash between the two skills, and it is a separate
thing from shrinking, even though both make the score worse.

### The water test

Picture a friend who makes a strawberry and banana smoothie with half a cup of each fruit, instead of
her usual full cup of strawberries. She complains that it tastes less of strawberry than usual, and
she blames the banana for spoiling it.

Before she blames it, she can make one more smoothie, with half a cup of strawberries and half a cup
of water in place of the banana. The water version shows how much flavour she lost by halving the
strawberries, and comparing it with the banana version shows what the banana really did.

The lab runs that same test on every merge, and to measure interference on recipes it reruns the
merge with the proverb list set to zeros. That list of zeros plays the part of the water, and the
difference between the two scores is the interference. The picture stops matching in one way: the
water smoothie is a real drink she could pour, while the lab’s water version is only a check it
works out. Nobody would ever use it as a model.

Here is the test with real numbers for the 50/50 average, scored on the recipe text:

1. The recipe specialist, which uses the whole recipe list, scores 2.123 on recipes.
2. Half the recipe list on its own, with zeros in place of the proverbs, scores 2.294, which is 0.171
   worse, and all of that comes from shrinking.
3. Half the recipe list plus half the proverb list scores 2.257, which is 0.037 *better* than the
   water version’s 2.294. So the interference is −0.037, and the minus sign means the proverb list
   helped the recipes.

On **Two task losses**, the **Gap** column shows the total change from the specialist, which is 2.257
minus 2.123, or +0.134. The **Interference** column shows the part caused by the other list, and
whatever is left of the gap is the cost of shrinking.

The two parts add up to the gap: 0.171 of shrinking plus −0.037 of interference gives 0.134. Because
the proverbs helped a little, the total gap is smaller than the shrinking on its own.

## How to play with it

### Read the two task vectors

Start on **Task vectors**, with **Task vectors from** left on `Full fine-tunes` and **Recipe epochs**
and **Proverb epochs** both at 20. Each of those two sliders reads “20 passes” at that setting, since
an epoch is one pass through the text.

Under the maps, **Recipe specialist, recipes** shows 2.123 and **Proverb specialist, proverbs** shows
2.169, the two specialists’ scores on their own texts. **Overlapping entries** reads 720 of 900, which
counts the cells that both fine-tunes changed, and the badge above the maps reads cosine 0.34.

**Sign conflicts** reads 96 (13%), so about 1 in 8 of those shared cells were pushed in opposite
directions by the two fine-tunes. Look for the cells with a thin diagonal line through them, because
those are the 96 conflicts, and they are marked on both maps.

Then drag **Recipe epochs** down to 0, and watch the slider change to read `untrained · τ = 0` as the
recipe map turns grey.

Every merge now works with the proverb list alone, because an untrained
fine-tune has a task vector made entirely of zeros. The one exception is SLERP, which needs two
non-zero lists to measure an angle, so its row falls back to the base model’s losses. Set the slider back to 20 before you go on, so the
numbers match the rest of this lesson.

### Average, then split the cost

On **Merge recipe**, leave **Merge method** on `Linear blend` with **Blend t** at 50%, where the
slider reads “50% recipes · 50% proverbs”. The **Served weights** formula shows the arithmetic, and
under it you can read the merged model’s loss on each of the two texts.

Now go to **Two task losses**, whose plot puts the recipe loss along the bottom and the proverb loss
up the side. Lower and further left is better on both jobs, so you want a merge’s point as close to
the bottom left corner as it can get.

The fixed markers are the `harbor base`, the `recipe specialist`, the `proverb specialist`, and a
cross for `two separate models`. That cross sits at each specialist’s score on its own job, which is
what you would get by keeping both models.

The large filled circle is the merge you picked, while small hollow circles show the other four
methods, each tagged with a short name: Lin, TA, TIES, DARE or SL. If a point would fall off the edge of the plot, it is drawn at the
edge with a dashed outline and labelled with its true losses.

In the table below the plot, the `Linear blend` row shows 2.257 on recipes and 2.330 on proverbs.
Both are worse than the **Specialists** row, so the merge loses to each specialist at its own job, by
gaps of +0.134 and +0.161.

Then read the two **Interference** columns, which show −0.037 and −0.055, and notice that both of
them are negative. The note under the table puts this into words, saying that adding each list helped
the other task rather than hurting it.

**Try this:** switch **Merge method** to `Task arithmetic` and set **Scale λ** to 1.00, which makes
the losses 2.279 and 2.258. Interference now turns positive, at +0.156 on recipes and +0.089 on
proverbs, so this time each list is hurting the other job.

Then drag **Scale λ** slowly from 0 up to 1.50, and watch the large circle travel along the dashed
line that the legend calls “task sum, λ from 0 to 1.5”. The dotted line beside it is the linear
blend’s path. When you finish, set **Scale λ** back to 1.00, because TIES and DARE use
the same slider and this lesson’s numbers assume 1.00.

### Resolve sign conflicts

Switch **Merge method** to `TIES`, where **Keep top** starts at 20% of each vector. Move it up to 100%
first, and then slide it slowly down to 10% while you watch the strip of bars on **Merge recipe**.

The strip shows ten cells, with the five biggest sign conflicts on the left and the five biggest
agreements on the right. Each cell has three bars: the recipe change on the left, the proverb change
in the middle, and the merged value on the right, with that value printed underneath.

With TIES selected, any change that was trimmed away turns into a dashed outline, and the winning
sign is printed as ▲ + above the merged bar or ▼ − below it. Find the conflict cell named `h→a`,
whose merged bar reads +1.18, the full proverb change, where `Linear blend` gave only +0.14.

### Drop and rescale

Switch **Merge method** to `DARE`, then sweep **Drop rate p** from 0 up to 0.9 and watch the large
filled circle, which now stands for DARE. In the strip, dropped changes become dashed outlines. A short tick,
which the legend calls “height before rescaling”, marks how tall each kept change was before it was
multiplied.

On the way up, stop at 0.50 and read the `DARE` row in the table, which shows 2.762 on recipes and
2.602 on proverbs. Those numbers belong to the starting mask, and once you draw a new one you cannot
get that mask back, so read them first.

Then carry on to 0.9 and press **New random mask** a few times. The button starts out reading
`New random mask · #4`, and the number goes up by one with each press, as each press draws a fresh
mask.

### Curve instead of line

Switch **Merge method** to `SLERP`, keeping **Blend t** at 50%, and look at the `‖τ_merged‖` badge,
which now reads 10.32 instead of 8.46. The note under the formula gives the angle between the two
lists as Ω = 69.9°, which matches the cosine of 0.34 on the first card.

## What to notice

### Shrinking, not clashing, explains the average

- The average’s gap is mostly shrinking, because the recipe list at half strength, alone, scores
  2.294, and adding half the proverb list *improves* that to 2.257.
- Both texts are ordinary English, so the two lists point partly the same way, which is what the
  positive cosine of 0.34 tells you.
- The proverbs help here because the recipe list is only at half strength, so the recipe model still
  has room to improve. Half the proverb list supplies some of that missing push.
- At full strength the recipe list has already done its job, so the same overlapping push overshoots
  and the interference turns positive, as the next section shows.
- The cosine is 0.34 in both cases, so the cosine alone cannot tell you whether the other list will
  help or hurt. How strongly each list is added matters just as much.
- About 87% of the 720 shared cells agree in sign, so two lists can overlap almost everywhere without
  being in conflict.

### Full strength is where they collide

- **Task arithmetic** at full strength is where the lists collide, costing recipes +0.156 and
  proverbs +0.089 against the same merge without the other list.
- In that row the **Gap** and **Interference** numbers match. With the proverb list removed, the merge
  is exactly the recipe specialist, so the whole gap comes from the other list and none from shrinking.
- **Scale λ** has a sweet spot, since both losses fall until λ is about 0.7 to 0.8 and then both rise
  as the dashed path bends back.
- At λ = 0.8, the losses are 2.218 on recipes and 2.241 on proverbs, which beats the 50/50 average on
  both jobs.
- Below the sweet spot each list is too weak, so turning λ up repairs the shrinking, while above it
  the interference grows faster than the repair.

### TIES picks a winner in each conflict

- TIES changes the conflict cells most: in `h→a`, recipes wrote −0.89 and proverbs wrote +1.18, and
  TIES keeps +1.18 where the average keeps +0.14.
- At **Keep top** 20%, only 38 of the 96 conflicts survive the trim on both sides, so only those 38
  need a vote.
- In the other 58, at least one side was trimmed away first, so there was nothing left on that side
  to vote with.
- With 20% kept, TIES lands at 2.273 on recipes and 2.280 on proverbs, which is close to plain task
  arithmetic in this lab.

### DARE breaks this model

- At p = 0, DARE drops nothing and multiplies by 1, so it gives exactly the same numbers as `Task
  arithmetic` at the same **Scale λ**.
- At p = 0.5, with the starting mask, DARE scores 2.762 on recipes and 2.602 on proverbs, worse than
  every other method here.
- At p = 0.9, nine in ten changes are dropped and each kept change is multiplied by 10, and the
  losses climb to nearly 6.
- That is 5.967 and 5.661, far worse than the score of about 3.4 for a model that spreads its guesses
  evenly over every character.
- The multiplying keeps each change right on average but not in any single draw, and this tiny model
  has no spare copies of its changes to fall back on.
- Losing changes alone would only drift the model back toward the base, with losses close to the
  base’s own. Going far past even guessing comes from the other half of the rule.
- Each surviving change is multiplied by 10, so those scores land ten times too far, and the model
  becomes confidently wrong.
- Each press of **New random mask** moves the DARE point, because a different set of changes is
  thrown away each time.

### SLERP keeps its length

- SLERP at 50% scores 2.226 on recipes and 2.284 on proverbs, which beats the plain average on both
  jobs.
- The two lists are 69.9° apart, so the straight-line halfway point is only 8.46 long, while the
  curve’s halfway point is 10.32 long.
- Since both halfway points face the same direction, keeping more of each list’s strength is what
  makes SLERP the better blend here.
- SLERP at 50% is the same model as `Task arithmetic` at **Scale λ** about 0.61. The slider moves in
  steps of 0.05, but at 0.60 the losses come out at 2.229 and 2.287, almost the same.
- `Task arithmetic` at 0.80 does better still, so the curve is not special in itself.

### LoRA lists clash far more

- With `LoRA rank 4`, **Sign conflicts** reads 331 (46%), out of the same 720 shared cells, so nearly
  half the shared cells are pushed in opposite directions.
- That is far more than the roughly 1 in 8 for full fine-tunes, and the cosine drops to 0.19, so the
  two LoRA lists point in less similar directions.
- At the 50/50 average the LoRA interference stays near zero, while each list is only at half
  strength.
- The clash shows up once both lists are added whole: `Task arithmetic` at **Scale λ** 1.00 gives
  interference of about +0.51 and +0.38, against +0.156 and +0.089 for full fine-tunes.

### One file has a price

No merge reaches the `two separate models` cross, and that leftover gap is the price of serving one
model instead of keeping two.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Every task vector, merge and loss in this lab is computed live from real fine-tunes, so nothing is
recorded in advance or made up. The limits come instead from how small and narrow the experiment is,
and each one is worth keeping in mind.

There are only two tasks, and each fine-tune runs once with one **seed**, the starting number for its
random choices. A different seed could give somewhat different lists. The model is
also tiny, with only 900 weights, and its texts hold only eight recipe lines and ten proverbs.

This lab’s base is its own 48-epoch run, not the 60-epoch Harbor model from the *Fine-tuning &
transfer* and *LoRA & adapters* labs. That is why its loss on recipes is 2.553 rather than the 2.556
you may remember. The difference is only 0.003, far smaller than the gaps between a merge and a specialist
that this lesson studies, though no lab tests whether it matters.

Each merge is also scored on the very sentences the fine-tunes trained on, so nothing is held back for
a fair test. Picture a student who practises with the exact questions that will be on the exam, so a
high mark shows only that they learned those questions. In the same way, these scores say which merge
fits these sentences, not which one would do best on new recipes or proverbs.

The strip shows only 10 of the 900 cells, picked because they are the biggest conflicts and
agreements, while the other 890 cells still count in every loss.

SLERP is applied here to the task vectors rather than to whole models, which lets you see the curve
clearly. Real tools such as mergekit apply SLERP to whole models instead.

Think of each whole model
as one long arrow, made of the long base arrow plus a short arrow of changes. Two such arrows almost
overlap, so there is hardly any corner to cut. In real use, SLERP on two fine-tunes of one base
therefore gives nearly the same result as a plain blend.

This model is a single table with no hidden units, so it cannot show what goes wrong when two models
lack a shared base. Every task vector in the lab starts from the same base on purpose, so you only
meet that failure in this lesson’s words.

The lab also tilts against TIES and DARE, which were designed for huge models whose changes are small
and highly repetitive. In such models most changes can be trimmed or dropped at little cost, while
here every one of the 900 numbers matters.

Picture a school play run by a crew of 900, where each person has a different job that nobody else
knows how to do. If you send half the crew home at random and ask the rest to work twice as hard, the
total effort might stay the same. Yet if the one person who knew the lights went home, nobody can work
them.

Now send nine in ten home, as DARE does at p = 0.9, and ask the rest to work ten times as hard. Worse
than the missing jobs, each person who stays now overdoes their own job, so the sound person turns the
music up far too loud. That is what DARE does to this lab’s tiny model.

A huge model is more like a crew where many people can cover each job, which is the setting DARE was
built for.

DARE also uses a single random mask for each setting, and the lab never averages its results over many
masks. **New random mask** shows you how far one fresh draw can move the result, which is the only
spread you get to see.

The LoRA maps carry one extra quirk, because adding the same amount to every score in a row changes no
guess at all. If every score in the `h` row went up by 1, the gaps between them would stay the same, so
the chances for the next character would not change.

The model only cares how the scores in a row compare with each other, and full fine-tunes never add a
shift like that. LoRA adapters do add such shifts, so part of what the LoRA
maps and cosine show makes no difference to any prediction.

**A common mix-up:** a merged model that loses to each specialist at its own job is not proof of
interference.
Averaging halves each task’s own list, and half a list scores worse even when nothing else is added at
all. Only the **Interference** columns, which rerun the merge with the other list set to zero, measure
what the other task really did.

### Where real merging falls short

Real merges are tuned and judged on held-out tests for every task at once, which means tests built
from examples the models never trained on. The scale λ is tuned on those tests too, and choosing it
well matters as much as choosing the method.

Interference grows as you merge more tasks, and also as the tasks become more different from each
other. Two tasks that share a lot, like the two English texts here, clash less than tasks that pull a
model in very different directions.

A merged model can keep its test scores while losing behaviour that no single score captures, such as
the way it lays out its answers. It might also lose its habit of refusing requests it should refuse,
or its **calibration**, which is how well its confidence matches how often it is right.

No merge comes with a promise, because merging runs no training step, so nothing guarantees that any
skill survives until you test it. Licences also travel with the weights, and a licence sets the rules
for how a model may be used. So a merged model inherits every rule from every model that went into it.

## Going deeper

### Subtracting a task

Task arithmetic works in reverse too, so you can subtract a task vector instead of adding one, written
`θ_base − λ·τ`. This has been used to remove a behaviour that a fine-tune taught, such as writing
toxic text, meaning text that is rude or hurtful.

### Adapters are task vectors too

An **adapter**, such as the LoRA add-on from *LoRA & adapters*, is a small trained update that is stored
apart from the base. Its correction, written `(α / r)·B·A` in that lab, is a list of changes to the
base’s weights, which makes it a task vector too. That is why the software built for adapters offers
the same merge methods that you have tried in this lab.

The `LoRA rank 4` option here uses adapters with a rank of 4 and an alpha of 8, as the line of text
above the maps says. So each adapter’s correction is its two small tables multiplied together and
then doubled, since 8 divided by 4 is 2.

### Methods stack

The methods can also be combined, and DARE is often used first to thin out the lists before they are
merged with TIES. SLERP takes exactly two models, so merges of many models use the linear, TIES or
DARE methods instead.

Tools such as mergekit apply these rules to one block of weights at a time. They sometimes use a
different blend for each layer, which is one of the stages a big model passes its input through.

### The cosine, worked out

The cosine on the badge comes from a short calculation that you could do by hand for a small list.
Multiply the two lists cell by cell, add up all the products, and divide the total by the two lengths
multiplied together.

Take two short lists, 3 and 4, and 4 and 3, which are both arrows 5 squares long. Multiplying cell by
cell gives 12 and 12, which add up to 24. Dividing 24 by 5 times 5, which is 25, gives a cosine of
0.96, so those two arrows point almost the same way.

Lists that rise and fall together give a cosine near 1, unrelated lists give one near 0, and lists
that move in opposite ways give one near −1.

### Other ways to combine skills

*LoRA & adapters* made the kind of lists this lab combines, and *Distillation & pruning* takes a
different route to a similar goal. It reaches one smaller model with many behaviours by training
rather than arithmetic, which costs computer time but actually tunes the result for the job.

### Merge, or serve the adapters side by side

Merging is not the only way to get several tasks out of one base, and *LoRA & adapters* ends on the
other route. That route keeps one copy of the base and many adapters, and it picks the right adapter
for each request as it arrives.

Serving software called vLLM does this when it is started with the `--enable-lora` option. A research
paper called S-LoRA describes serving thousands of adapters at the same time from one shared base.

The two routes trade differently, because serving keeps each task’s adapter exact but costs effort in
scheduling requests and managing memory. A merge costs nothing extra when the model runs, but, as this
lab measures, it can add interference between the tasks. How much depends on the method and the
scale.

Serve the adapters separately when tasks must never interfere, or when you need to add and remove
them while the system is running. Merge them when one fixed model is enough and your held-out tests
show that the interference is small.

### Toy versus real

- **Tools:** mergekit, from Arcee AI, describes itself as “a toolkit for merging
  pre-trained language models”. You describe a merge in a short settings file, written in a format
  called YAML, that names the models and the method.
- **Methods:** mergekit’s methods include Linear, SLERP, Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE and DELLA, among
  others, and this lab rebuilds the first five on a table of 900 numbers.
- **Memory:** mergekit works through a model one piece at a time instead of loading it all at once,
  so a merge can run on a computer’s main processor alone. It can also use a graphics card with as
  little as 8 GB of its own memory. A gigabyte, or GB, is about a billion bytes, and one byte holds
about one letter of text.
- **Testing:** here a merge is arithmetic on 900 numbers and the check is two losses, while a real
  merge is judged on held-out tests for every task. On those tests, one score can hold steady while
  another slips, so every task needs checking.
- **Adapters:** in Hugging Face’s PEFT library, `merge_and_unload()` folds one adapter into its base
  for good. The mergekit tool can go the other way with `mergekit-extract-lora`, building an adapter
  that comes close to a full fine-tune’s changes.
- **Side by side:** several adapters can also stay unmerged and be served side by side, as the section
  above describes.
