## The big picture

A **model merge** turns separate fine-tunes of one pretrained model into a single set of weights
by arithmetic alone, with no training data and no gradient step. The question this module asks is
whether skills learned in separate runs add together, and what each merging rule costs each task.

Its unit is the **task vector**: the change a fine-tune made to its base's weights, entry by entry.
*Fine-tuning & transfer* rewrote a base table's own weights, and *LoRA & adapters* left you one
low-rank adapter per task; both changes are task vectors. This lab builds two from one Harbor
base, for Recipe steps and Proverbs, and combines them five ways.

### Where it shows up

- mergekit, from Arcee AI, merges pretrained language models from a YAML configuration, and its
  methods include all five this lab runs.
- Adapter libraries apply merge methods to LoRA adapters too, because the low-rank update a LoRA
  adapter adds, `(α / r)·B·A`, is itself a task vector. Hugging Face PEFT can also fold a single
  adapter back into its base weights with `merge_and_unload()`.
- Subtracting a scaled task vector instead of adding it has been used to remove a behaviour a
  fine-tune taught, such as toxic generation.

### Why it matters

Serving many tasks otherwise means keeping one model or adapter file per task, or training one
model again on all the data. A merge combines the files you already have, in seconds, with no
training data, and costs nothing extra at inference.

It assumes a **shared base**: every input was fine-tuned from the same checkpoint, so each entry
means the same weight in all of them.

### What you will be able to explain

- How a merge works as arithmetic on task vectors that share one base, not as a second training
  run.
- How to separate the cost of shrinking each task vector from true **interference**: the change in
  one task's loss caused by adding the other task's vector.
- What TIES, DARE and SLERP each change about a plain average.

### What the lab shows

Nothing is synthetic. The app trains a 900-weight character bigram base and two fine-tunes of it,
full or LoRA rank 4; every merge is exact arithmetic on their task vectors, and every loss is
measured on the full Recipe steps and Proverbs corpora. Corpora, training seeds and learning rate
are fixed, nothing is held out, and DARE's drop mask is the only random draw you can change, with
**New random mask**.

- **Task vectors**: **Task vectors from** (`Full fine-tunes` or `LoRA rank 4`), **Recipe epochs**
  and **Proverb epochs** shape the two vectors, mapped cell by cell with sign conflicts struck
  through. Under the maps, **Overlapping entries** counts the entries both vectors changed, and
  **Sign conflicts** how many of those disagree in sign.
- **Merge recipe**: **Merge method** picks `Linear blend`, `Task arithmetic`, `TIES`, `DARE` or
  `SLERP` and shows only its own controls among **Blend t**, **Scale λ**, **Keep top**,
  **Drop rate p** and **New random mask**. **Served weights** prints the operation with current
  numbers.
- **Two task losses**: every merge plotted by recipe and proverb loss against the base, the

The key experiment changes only how much of each vector is added: compare `Linear blend` at

## What it is

A **model merge** combines trained weights with arithmetic and no gradient step. The unit it
works on is the **task vector**: fine-tuned weights minus the base they started from,
`τ = θ_tuned − θ_base`. Every method in this lab builds one combined vector from two task vectors
and serves `θ_base + τ_merged`.

The base is the 900-weight character bigram table, trained 48 epochs on Harbor weather notes. It
scores 2.553 nats per character on Recipe steps and 2.684 on Proverbs. Two full fine-tunes start
from it, 20 epochs each. The recipe specialist reaches 2.123 on recipes; the proverb specialist
reaches 2.169 on proverbs.

That base is this lab's own run, not the 60-epoch Harbor table of *Fine-tuning & transfer* and
*LoRA adapters*, which reads 2.556 on recipes. Each lab fixes its own epoch count, and none tests
the difference.

### Why a shared base matters

Both task vectors are differences from the same checkpoint, so entry 417 means the same weight in
both. That **shared base** is what makes adding them meaningful. Two networks trained from
different random initializations can compute the same function with their hidden units in a
different order, and averaging them entry by entry mixes unrelated units. Fine-tunes of one
pretrained model tend to share a low-loss basin, a property called **linear mode connectivity**.

### The five recipes

- **Weight averaging** (`Linear blend`): `θ_base + (1 − t)·τ_r + t·τ_p`, the same as averaging
  the two fine-tuned checkpoints.
- **Task arithmetic**: `θ_base + λ·(τ_r + τ_p)`. At λ = 0.5 it is the average.
- **TIES-Merging**: trim each vector to its largest entries, elect one sign per weight, average
  only the entries that agree.
- **DARE**: drop entries at random with probability p, rescale survivors by `1 / (1 − p)`, then add.
- **SLERP**: blend along the arc between the two vectors rather than the chord.

## Why it’s here

The cost of skipping training is that a merge is a guess. Nothing optimizes the combined weights
for either task, so whether two skills survive the addition has to be measured. This lab measures
it on both tasks at once, and separates two reasons a merge can lose: the method shrinking each
task's own vector, and genuine **interference** from the other task.

## How to play with it

Start on **Task vectors**. At 20 epochs each, the cosine badge reads 0.34, **Overlapping entries**
reads 720 of 900, and **Sign conflicts** reads 96 (13%). Struck-through cells on both maps are
those conflicts.

On **Merge recipe**, leave **Merge method** on `Linear blend` with **Blend t** at 50%. The table on
**Two task losses** reads 2.257 on recipes and 2.330 on proverbs: gaps of +0.134 and +0.161 over
the specialists. Now read the interference columns: −0.037 and −0.055.

Switch to `Task arithmetic` at **Scale λ** 1.00: 2.279 and 2.258, and interference turns
positive, +0.156 and +0.089. Sweep **Scale λ** and watch the dashed path on the plane.

Pick `TIES` and move **Keep top** from 100% down to 10%. Pick `DARE`, sweep **Drop rate p** from 0
to 0.9, and press **New random mask** a few times. Finally switch **Task vectors from** to
`LoRA rank 4` and repeat the average.

## What to notice

- The average's gap is not interference. The recipe vector at half strength, alone, scores 2.294;
  adding half the proverb vector *improves* that to 2.257. Almost all of the +0.134 gap is
  **weight averaging** halving each task's own vector.
- Interference appears when both vectors are added whole. **Task arithmetic** at λ = 1 costs recipes
  +0.156 and proverbs +0.089 compared with the same merge without the other vector.
- λ trades the two effects. Both losses fall until about λ = 0.7 to 0.8 (2.218 and 2.241 at 0.8),
  then both rise.
- **TIES-Merging** changes the conflict columns most. In the `h→a` column recipes wrote −0.89 and
  proverbs +1.18: the average keeps +0.14, TIES keeps +1.18. At 20% kept it lands at 2.273 and
  2.280, close to plain task arithmetic here.
- **DARE** does not survive this model. At p = 0.5 the default mask gives 2.762 and 2.602; at
  p = 0.9, 5.967 and 5.661. The rescale keeps each entry's expected value but not its actual value,
  and these deltas carry no spare copies.
- **SLERP** at t = 0.5 gives 2.226 and 2.284, better than the average on both. The vectors are 69.9°
  apart, so the chord's midpoint is only 8.46 long while the arc's is 10.32.
- LoRA task vectors conflict far more: 331 of 720 entries (46%), cosine 0.19.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Every task vector, merge and loss is computed live. The limits are scale and
selection. There are two tasks, one seed each, a 900-weight model and eight-to-ten-sentence
corpora scored in full, so nothing is held out.

The strip shows 10 of 900 coordinates, chosen as the largest conflicts and agreements. SLERP is
applied to task vectors, not whole checkpoints. And a bigram table has no hidden units, so it
cannot show the permutation failure that breaks merges without a **shared base**.

The toy also tilts against TIES and DARE. Both were designed for large models whose fine-tuning
deltas are small and highly redundant, where most entries can be trimmed or dropped at little
cost. Here every entry of a 900-weight table matters.

**In real systems.** Merges are tuned and judged on held-out benchmarks for every task at once, and
λ matters as much as the method. Interference grows with the number of tasks and with how
different they are. Merged models can keep benchmark scores while losing formatting, refusal or
calibration behaviour that no single loss captures. Licences also travel with weights, so a merge
inherits every constraint of its inputs.

## Going deeper

Task arithmetic also subtracts: `θ_base − λ·τ` has been used to remove a behaviour, such as toxic
generation, learned by a fine-tune. The same algebra works on an **adapter**: a LoRA product
`(α / r)·B·A` is a task vector, which is why adapter libraries offer the same merge methods.

TIES and DARE compose. DARE is a pre-processing step, and its sparsified vectors are often merged
with TIES rather than summed. SLERP combines exactly two inputs, so multi-model merges use linear,
TIES or DARE variants instead. Tools such as mergekit apply these rules tensor by tensor, sometimes
with a different t per layer.

Merging sits beside two neighbours in this track. LoRA adapters produced the vectors this lab
adds. Distillation and pruning reach one smaller model from many behaviours by training rather than
by arithmetic, which costs compute but optimizes the result directly.

### Merge, or serve the adapters side by side

Merging is not the only way to get several tasks out of one base. *LoRA & adapters* ends on the
alternative: keep one copy of the base and many adapters, and pick one per request. vLLM does this
when started with `--enable-lora`, and the S-LoRA paper describes serving thousands of concurrent
adapters from one base.

The two routes trade differently. Serving keeps each task's adapter exact and costs scheduling and
memory management. A merge costs nothing at inference and, as this lab measures, adds interference.
Serve separately when tasks must never interfere or must be added and removed live. Merge when one
fixed model is enough and your held-out evaluations show the interference is small.

### Toy versus real

- **Tools.** mergekit, from Arcee AI, is "a toolkit for merging pre-trained language models". A merge
  is a YAML configuration that names the models and the method. Its methods include Linear, SLERP,
  Task Arithmetic, TIES, DARE and DELLA, among others; this lab reproduces the first five on a
  900-number table.
- **Scale.** mergekit uses an out-of-core approach, so a merge can run entirely on CPU or with as
  little as 8 GB of VRAM. Here a merge is arithmetic on 900 numbers and the check is two losses. A
  real merge is judged on held-out benchmarks for every task, and one benchmark can hold while
  another degrades.
- **Adapters.** In Hugging Face PEFT, `merge_and_unload()` folds one adapter into its base. mergekit
  can go the other way and extract a PEFT-compatible low-rank approximation from a fine-tuned model
  with `mergekit-extract-lora`. Several adapters can also stay unmerged and be served side by side.
