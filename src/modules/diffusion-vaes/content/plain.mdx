## The big picture

You may have seen a picture that an AI tool made from a few typed words, or heard of tools that make
music and video clips in a similar way. This module opens up the machinery behind tools like these,
using the smallest pictures that still mean something to a person. Those pictures are handwritten
digits from 0 to 9, each one drawn in a tiny square.

A program that makes new things, such as sentences or pictures, is called a **generative model**.
Most other labs in this course look at generative models that write text one small piece at a time,
guessing the next piece over and over. This lab is about a different family, the ones that make a
whole picture at once instead of piece by piece.

The lab shows two ways of doing that, and they work almost like opposites:

- A **VAE** learns to squeeze each picture down to a code of only two numbers, and to redraw a
  picture from any two numbers in one go. Two numbers work like the two coordinates of a point on a
  graph, so every picture gets its own spot on a flat map.
- **Diffusion** ruins pictures on purpose by adding static, like the fuzz on an old TV with no
  signal, a little at a time. It then learns to undo that damage, so it can start from pure static
  and clean it up, step by step, until a digit appears.

Both are real, small models trained on 60,000 handwritten digits, and every digit they make in the
lab is worked out on the spot while you watch.

### Where you have met it

The ideas in this lab are the machinery behind image, audio and video generation. In fact, many
modern image generators use both ideas at once, starting with a squeezer much like the VAE that shrinks the
picture. All the slow diffusion work happens in that smaller space, and the result is expanded back
into a full picture at the very end. That combination is most of why making high-quality images with
AI became affordable.

### Why it matters

To make a picture, a program must choose a brightness for every pixel, the tiny squares a picture is
made of, and here there are 784 of them. Those pixels all have to agree with each other, since a
stroke that starts on one side of a digit must carry on and join up with the rest. If you choose
every pixel at random, you get static, because nothing makes neighbouring pixels line up.

Yet a generative model needs some randomness, or it would draw the same picture every time. Both
models in this lab answer the same puzzle, which is how to turn random input into pixels that fit
together. Without the VAE’s learned map of codes, two random numbers would mean nothing, and without
diffusion’s learned clean-up, random static would stay static.

The two answers come with very different costs, and real systems are designed around them. The VAE
is quick, because its drawing step runs only once per picture, but its pictures tend to come out
blurry. Diffusion’s digits in this lab are readable far more often, but it pays for that by running
its whole clean-up calculation again for each of its 100 steps.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how building a picture through many small clean-up steps differs from rebuilding it from a short
  code in a single step;
- why the first way is slow, while the second way tends to give blurry pictures;
- how to read a map where every point is the code for a picture, and why points close together give
  similar digits;
- why the crowded middle of that map gives blurry blends of two or more digits;
- why a perfect clean-up tool would only ever copy its examples, while a real, imperfect network can
  make new pictures.

## What it is

### A picture is a grid of numbers

Every picture in this lab is a small square, 28 pixels across and 28 pixels down, which makes 784
pixels in all. The computer stores each pixel as a single number for its brightness. In the
diffusion half of the lab, −1 means black and 1 means white, with greys in between, so a digit is a
white stroke on a black background.

The digits come from a collection called MNIST, and both models learned from all 60,000 of its
training digits. Another 10,000 test digits were kept aside, so the models never saw them during
training.

Both models are built from **neural networks**, and a neural network is a large calculation with
many adjustable numbers inside it. Training means nudging those numbers, over and over, until the
network’s answers come out right.

### The VAE: squeeze a picture into two numbers, then redraw it

Think of the character creator in a video game, where a few sliders set things like height and
build. Every position of the sliders gives you a complete character, and moving a slider a little
changes the character a little. The VAE builds something like a character creator for handwritten
digits, with only two sliders.

It does this with two networks that train together as a team. The first, called the **encoder**,
looks at a 784-pixel picture and squeezes it down to only two numbers. The second, called the
**decoder**, takes any two numbers and expands them back into a 784-pixel picture in a single pass.

During training, each digit goes through the encoder and then the decoder, and the pair is scored on
how closely the copy matches the original. Only two numbers fit through the middle, so those two
numbers have to hold whatever matters most about each digit.

The pair of numbers is called the picture’s **latent**, a word that means hidden, since it is a
compressed code you never see in the picture itself. The lab calls the two numbers `z₁` and `z₂`,
and you can treat them like map coordinates, with `z₁` saying how far across and `z₂` how far up. So
every code is a point on a flat map, which is the **Latent map** card in the lab.

VAE is short for **variational autoencoder**, and an autoencoder is a model that learns to turn a
picture into a code and then turn that code back into a copy. The word “variational” comes from the
math used to train it, which you won’t need here.

The character creator stops matching the VAE in three ways. In a game, designers decide what each
slider does, such as setting the height, but nobody told the VAE what its two numbers should mean,
so training settled that. A character creator also goes only one way, from sliders to a character,
while the VAE learns the return trip too, from a finished picture back to its two numbers. And in a
game every slider setting gives a proper character, while some points on the VAE’s map give only a
blurry mix of digits.

### Keeping the codes packed together

If the encoder were free to put codes anywhere, it might push the 0s far off to one side and the 7s
far off to the other. That would leave wide empty gaps, and a made-up code for a brand-new digit
could land in one of them. No training digit’s code ever landed in such a gap, so the decoder was
never taught a digit there.

So the VAE adds a second rule to its training score: the codes should stay packed in a round cloud
centred on the point (0, 0). That target cloud is called the **prior**, a word that means “before
looking at any picture”, and the lab draws it on the map as two dashed circles.

The penalty for not fitting the cloud is called the **KL divergence**. It compares each picture’s
code with the target cloud and charges for two things. One is a code whose spot sits far from the
centre. The other is about the size of a small wobble around each code, which you will meet in the
next section. If the rule worked perfectly, you could pick any random point from the cloud, hand
it to the decoder and get a believable digit back.

This lab applies the rule lightly, multiplying the KL penalty by 0.35, about a third of its full
strength, before adding it to the training score. As a result, real digits’ codes spread wider than
the target cloud. Random picks, though, come from the cloud itself, so most of them land near the
centre, where the regions of several digits overlap. Only about half of those picks become a clear
digit, as Where it breaks shows.

### A best-guess spot with a small wobble

The encoder actually gives a little more than two plain numbers, because for each of the two it
gives a best-guess spot, called the mean and written μ (mu). It also gives a small spread around
that spot, written σ (sigma), so you can read its answer as “the code is about here, give or take
σ”.

During training, the decoder does not receive the exact spot but a point picked at random from the
small patch around μ. That way the decoder has to draw well for the whole patch, and not only for
one exact point. The lab writes the pick like this, with ε (epsilon) standing for a random number,
usually between about −2 and 2, that is rolled before the calculation starts:

`z = μ + σ·ε`

Here is a worked example with a ready-made drawing of a 3 that the lab offers, which you can load
with the `3` button in **Encode a drawing**. For this drawing, μ for `z₁` is 1.46 and σ is about
0.03. If the random roll gives ε = 0.5, then z₁ = 1.46 + 0.03 × 0.5, which comes to 1.475. The
wobble moves the point only a tiny distance, because σ is so small.

Rolling ε first has its own name, **reparameterization**, and it solves a practical problem.
Training keeps asking questions like this one: if μ were a little bigger, would the score get better
or worse? If z were only “some random point near μ”, no rule would link a change in μ to a change in
z, so that question would have no answer.

Writing `z = μ + σ·ε` fixes this. The roll ε is set aside first, like a dice roll written down
before the game, and then nudging μ up by 0.1 moves z up by exactly 0.1. Everything after the roll
is ordinary arithmetic, so training can see how μ and σ affect the score.

The KL penalty from the previous section looks at this wobble too. It compares each picture’s small
patch with the whole target cloud, whose spread is 1. A patch far narrower than 1 counts as a poor
fit, so it costs points too.

### Diffusion: ruin pictures on purpose, then learn to undo it

Picture someone learning to restore old photos by practising on photos that were ruined on purpose.
You take a clean photo and sprinkle a little static over it, then a little more, until nothing is
left but static. At every stage, the trainee studies the damaged photo and tries to point out
exactly which specks are static. After enough practice, they can take a photo covered in static and
remove some of it, so that more of the picture underneath shows through.

Diffusion works the same way, in two halves, and the first half, adding static, takes no skill at
all. The second half, working out which part of a picture is static, is the part that has to be
learned.

The photo restorer stops matching in one important way, because a restorer always has a real photo
hidden under the static. When diffusion makes a new picture, it starts from static with nothing
under it at all, so the digit that appears is built up by the clean-up itself.

### Adding static: the forward process

The first half, called the **forward process**, adds static to a clean digit over 100 steps,
numbered from t = 0 to t = 99. Here t stands for the step, and the lab labels the clean digit `x₀`,
meaning the picture before any static at all. How much static goes in at each step follows a fixed
plan called the **noise schedule**. Nobody trains this plan and no network is involved, so this half
is pure arithmetic.

At every step, each pixel of the noisy picture is a mix of the clean pixel and a random static
value:

`noisy pixel = signal share × clean pixel + noise share × static`

The static is a separate random number for each pixel, mostly between −2 and 2, and the lab writes
it as ε, the same Greek letter as before. As the steps go by, the signal share falls from about 1
toward 0, while the noise share rises from about 0 toward 1. Under each tile of the strip, the lab
prints the signal share as `√ᾱ`, which you can read as “the square root of alpha-bar”.

Here is the mix worked out at step t = 59, where the tile reads 0.60 for the signal share. The noise
share there, 0.80, is not printed under the tile, but it follows from a rule explained just below:

- A white pixel of the digit has the value 1, so if its static is −0.5, it becomes 0.60 × 1 + 0.80 ×
  (−0.5), which is 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20.
- A black background pixel has the value −1, so if its static is 0.5, it becomes 0.60 × (−1) + 0.80
  × 0.5, which is −0.60 + 0.40 = −0.20.

Both pixels end up as greys close to zero, which makes them much harder to tell apart, and that is
how the digit fades into the static. You may also notice that 0.60 and 0.80 behave like the sides of
a 3-4-5 triangle, because their squares, 0.36 and 0.64, add up to exactly 1. The schedule keeps that
true at every step. That rule stops the pixel values from swinging steadily wider, or shrinking toward
zero, as the static takes over, and it lets you work out either share from the other.

The plan has to end with almost nothing of the picture left, because making a new picture will start
from fresh static. The network only knows what to do with the kind of input it practised on, so the
last forward step has to look like plain static too. Here the last step keeps just 3% of the digit,
which is the 0.03 under the last tile, or 0.031 to three decimal places.

### Guessing the static: the denoiser

The second half needs learning, so a network called the **denoiser** is trained to guess the static
that was added to a noisy picture. It is given the noisy picture and its step number, and one
network handles all 100 steps, since the step number tells it how much static to expect.

Training runs as a loop: take a digit, pick a random step, add static using the mix above, and ask
the denoiser to guess that static. Then score the guess by how far off it is, and nudge the
network’s adjustable numbers so that it does a little better next time.

The lab’s denoiser has 1,181,217 adjustable numbers, and it was trained for 45 passes over all
60,000 MNIST digits, so it saw every training digit 45 times. Its error is the average, over all 784
pixels, of the squared gap between its guess and the real static. By the end of training that error
was 0.036, against 1.0 for a lazy guess of “no static anywhere”.

Guessing the static is useful because, once you know what static was added, you can take it away and
work out the clean picture. The lab shows that worked-out picture with the caption `predicted x̂₀`,
where the little hat on top means “a guess at”. Beside it, `predicted ε̂` shows the guessed static
itself, with mid-grey standing for zero.

### Cleaning up: the reverse process

Making a new picture is called the **reverse process**, and it starts by handing 784 numbers of
fresh static to the denoiser, which guesses the static in it. An update then makes three small moves,
and the result is a slightly cleaner picture. The denoiser is called again on that, for 100 calls in
all. Each move has a reason:

- **It removes only one step’s worth of the guessed static**, not all of it, because each call is
  meant to undo just one forward step. Why not remove it all at once? Why it’s here answers that,
  under “One jump from static to a digit is too big”.
- **It scales the picture up very slightly**, because each forward step shrank the picture a little
  as the signal share fell, so the way back has to grow it again.
- **It sprinkles in a little fresh static.** Each forward step added static at random, so many
  slightly different pictures could have come just before the current one. The step back picks one
  of them at random. That keeps the right amount of static for the next call, and it helps stop the
  run from drifting toward a smooth, blurry average of many possible digits.

The very last call skips the fresh static, because its output is meant to be the finished picture.

The **Reverse diffusion** card prints the update for the next call with its real numbers filled in.
The recipe is called **DDPM**, the name of the standard step-by-step method that this lab uses. The
calls work backward through the steps: call 1 cleans the picture at step 99, call 2 the one at step
98, and so on. When you first open the lab, the **Denoiser calls** slider sits at 62, so the line
shows call 63. That call works at step 99 − 62 = 37 and turns the picture at step 37 into the
picture at step 36:

`x_36 = (x_37 − 0.0359 · ε̂) × 1.0102 + 0.1415 · z`

In that line, `x_37` is the current picture and `ε̂` is the guessed static. The letter `z` stands
for the fresh static, and `x_36` is the slightly cleaner picture that comes out. Follow one pixel
with a value of 0.50, where the guessed static is 2.0 and the fresh static is −0.3:

1. Remove part of the guess: 0.50 − 0.0359 × 2.0 = 0.4282.
2. Scale up slightly: 0.4282 × 1.0102 is about 0.4326.
3. Sprinkle in fresh static: 0.4326 + 0.1415 × (−0.3) is about 0.39.

That pixel moved from 0.50 to about 0.39. Most calls change the picture only a little like this,
because the trip from static to a digit has been split into 100 small steps. The numbers in the
line change from call to call, because each step of the schedule added a different amount of static.

### A perfect denoiser to compare against

The lab offers a second denoiser, the `Ideal denoiser`, which is not a network at all but exact
math. It works with 64 reference digits that the VAE’s decoder drew, laid out in a grid 8 across and
8 down. At every call, it compares the noisy picture with each reference, faded to match the current
step, and gives each reference a share of weight by how well it matches. The shares always add up to
100%.

Its guess at the clean picture is a blend of the references, with each one counting as much as its
share. If it gave 75% of the weight to one reference and 25% to another, its guess would be three
parts of the first mixed with one part of the second. While the weight is spread over many
references the guess looks blurry, but once one reference holds almost all of it, the guess becomes
that reference exactly.

For those 64 digits, no denoiser could guess the static better, which is why the lab calls it ideal.
That perfection has a surprising cost, though: the ideal denoiser can only ever give back one of its
64 references. Here is why, in three steps:

1. Its guess at the clean picture is always a blend of the 64 references and nothing else, because
   those 64 are the only clean pictures it knows.
2. As the static thins out, one reference always ends up matching far better than the rest, so the
   blend narrows down to that single picture.
3. The finished result is therefore an exact copy of that one reference.

Picture two friends getting ready for an art quiz with the same 64 sample drawings. One memorizes
all 64 perfectly, so whatever the quiz asks, they reproduce the closest sample line for line. The
other cannot hold every line in memory and picks up the general look instead, so their drawings
resemble the samples without matching any of them.

The ideal denoiser is like the first friend, and the trained network is more like the second. The
analogy bends in two places, though. The trained network did not study the 64 references at all,
but a much bigger set, all 60,000 MNIST training digits. And the ideal denoiser memorizes nothing,
because it is handed the 64 references ready-made. Which one it ends on is settled gradually by the
static, not chosen at the start.

### Two opposite designs, side by side

Set next to each other, the two designs differ at almost every point:

- **Where a new picture starts:** the VAE starts from two numbers, which make a point on its map,
  while diffusion starts from 784 numbers of static.
- **What gets learned:** the VAE learns a map of codes and a way to draw any point on it, while
  diffusion learns to guess the static in a noisy picture.
- **How many network runs each picture takes:** the VAE’s decoder runs once, while diffusion’s
  denoiser runs 100 times, one call after another.
- **The usual flaw:** VAE pictures tend to come out blurry, while diffusion is slow.

The big practical difference is patience, so picture a relay race with 100 runners, where each
runner has to wait for the baton before setting off. Even with very fast runners, you cannot finish
sooner by starting them all at once. Diffusion’s calls work the same way, since call 50 needs the
picture that call 49 produced, so they cannot run side by side.

## Why it’s here

### One jump from static to a digit is too big

You might wonder why diffusion bothers with 100 calls instead of training one network to turn static
straight into a digit. From pure static, every digit is about equally likely, so the safest single
guess is a blurry blend of many digits at once.

To see why a blend is “safest”, picture a game where a friend flashes a digit at you for a split
second and you have to redraw it. You lose points on every pixel by how far its brightness is from
the right one, and a big miss costs far more than a small one. If you can’t tell whether it was a 3
or a 5, a faint mix of both is only a little wrong almost everywhere. A crisp wrong guess is badly
wrong on many pixels, so on average the faint mix loses fewer points.

A network trained to make the whole jump in one go is scored in a similar way, so it learns to draw
that blurry mix. You can see this in the lab: with the `Ideal denoiser`, the `predicted x̂₀` picture
during the first few calls is a soft blend of many references.

Diffusion avoids getting stuck with that blur by splitting the trip into 100 small steps. Each call
moves the picture only a short way toward the current guess, adds a pinch of fresh static, and then
asks again. As the static thins out, the guess gets sharper, and the picture gradually settles on a
single digit. Each step is also a much smaller job than the whole trip, because the denoiser only
has to deal with the static at one level.

### Pixels are a poor place to mix pictures

The VAE solves a different problem, which is giving you a space where you can move smoothly from one
picture to another, something pixels alone are bad at. Averaging a 0 and a 2 pixel by pixel gives
you a faint 0 and a faint 2 lying on top of each other, which looks like neither digit.

On the VAE’s map, every point is a code that the decoder turns into one picture. A small move on the
map gives a small change in the picture, so you can walk from one digit to another and watch the
shape change along the way.

The map can blur too, in two kinds of places. In a gap between two digits’ regions, few or no
training digits’ codes landed, so the decoder was never taught a digit there. It still draws
something, because its output changes smoothly from point to point, and what it draws is a blurry
mix of the digits on either side. In the crowded middle of the map, the codes of several different
digits sit close together, so a point there could belong to any of them. The decoder hedges between
them there, for the same reason as in the flash-a-digit game.

Even so, the VAE’s walk is far better than averaging pixels, which gives a double image the whole
way. Along most of the walk the decoder draws one digit-like shape that changes a little at a time.
The blur shows up only in the stretches where one digit’s region hands over to the next. Finding
those stretches is part of reading the map. In most models the latent stays hidden deep inside the
machinery, but here it becomes somewhere you can explore.

### Not every generative model guesses the next piece

The text models in other labs build their answer one piece at a time, from left to right, and once a
word is written it stays. Diffusion works on the whole picture at every step instead, so even a late
call can still adjust a stroke in the top-left corner. A text model of that kind cannot go back and
change a word it has already written.

With **Denoiser** on `Ideal denoiser`, the 64 references appear in a grid below the readouts. Each

### Walk around the latent map

Switch **Model** to `VAE`, and the **Latent map** card shows a mosaic of 256 small digits in a 16 ×
16 grid. Each tile is what the decoder draws for the code at that tile’s centre. The map runs from
−4 to 4 in both directions, with `z₁` going across and `z₂` going up. The two dashed circles mark
the target cloud, at distances of 1 and 2 from the centre. The lab labels them 1σ and 2σ, and this
σ is the target cloud’s spread, which is 1. It is not the encoder’s tiny wobble from earlier, even
though the two share the same letter.

Drag the point, which starts at (−2.00, −0.80) among the 5s on the left, and watch the big digit
beside the map change smoothly. You can also move it in one direction at a time with the **Latent
z₁** and **Latent z₂** sliders, in steps of 0.05.

Two readouts sit under the sliders. **‖z‖** is how far your point is from the centre, measured in
units of the target cloud’s spread. That spread is 1, so the number is just the distance. You can
check it with Pythagoras: at the starting point it is √((−2)² + (−0.8)²) = √(4 + 0.64), which is
about 2.15.

**log p(z)** says how often random picks from the target cloud land near your point. It uses a
squeezed scale measured in **nats**, where each drop of about 2.3 means random picks land there
about 10 times less often. The closer it is to zero, the more often random picks land near that
spot. It reads about −1.84 at the centre and −4.16 at the starting point, a drop of about 2.3. So
the starting point gets about one tenth as many random picks as the centre. It drops faster the
further out you go.

Now try a planned route, starting on the 0s along the bottom and going up the right side through the
2s to the 1s at the top right. From there, go along the top to the 7s at the top left. Walking from
one code to another, usually in a straight line, and watching the picture change along the way is
called **latent interpolation**. For a straight-line try, pick any two digits on the map and drag
the point straight from one to the other. Smooth change tells you the map is well organized, while
a blurry stretch marks a spot where one digit’s region hands over to another. Finish by dragging
through the crowded middle and watching the digit blur.

### Squeeze your own drawing

In **Encode a drawing**, click the `7` button and then the `3` button, or draw your own digit with a
mouse or pen. Each stroke is sent to the encoder when you lift the mouse button or pen, and `Clear`
wipes the pad. The card shows two copies: `decoder(μ)`, drawn from the encoder’s best-guess spot, and `decoder(μ + σ·ε)`, drawn from a point
wobbled at random. The encoded spot also appears on the **Latent map** as a diamond, with an oval
around it that shows twice its wobble.

The numbers beside the copies describe this one drawing:

- **Posterior mean μ** is the encoder’s best-guess spot, where “posterior” means “after looking at
  the drawing”, the partner word to prior.
- **Posterior σ (prior σ = 1)** is the size of the wobble, shown next to the target cloud’s spread
  of 1.
- **Reconstruction BCE, decoder(μ)** scores how far the copy is from your drawing, added up over all
  784 pixels, in nats. Here nats are simply the unit the score is counted in, the same one the KL
  and training-loss rows use, so the scores can be added together. BCE is short for binary
  cross-entropy, and lower is better. The pen leaves
  soft grey edges, so even a perfect copy would score a little above 0.
- The row starting `KL` is the packing penalty for this drawing, the KL divergence from earlier.
- **Training loss, BCE + 0.35 × KL** is the training score for this one drawing, worked out from
  the `decoder(μ)` copy. Training itself scored the wobbled copy, but the two are almost the same
  here, as the note under the numbers shows.

For the `3` preset, the BCE is 121.1 nats and the KL is 7.12. Multiplying the KL by 0.35 gives about
2.49, so the training loss comes to about 123.6 nats. Almost all of that score comes from the copy’s
mistakes, and very little comes from the packing rule.

## What to notice

### Making static is arithmetic, but removing it takes a network

Adding static is one line of arithmetic for each tile, with nothing to learn. Only the reverse
direction, going from static back toward a digit, needs a trained network.

### Seed 7 gives one new digit and one copy

With seed 7 from pure noise, the trained network turns static into a digit. Its **Background
pixels** number goes from 0.17 to 0.82, which means about 1 pixel in 6 is near black at the start
and about 4 in 5 at the end.

The `Ideal denoiser`, given exactly the same static, lands exactly on reference #64. The network’s
picture is nearest to reference #28, at 0.26, and is none of them.

That 0.26 is a mean squared distance: square the gap between the two pictures at every pixel, then
average those squares, so that 0 would mean a perfect copy. A distance of 0.26 is what you would get
if every pixel were off by about 0.5, on the scale from −1 for black to 1 for white. Across every
seed on the slider, the network’s picture never comes closer than 0.07 to any reference.

Those 64 references are not what the network learned from, though, so missing them is only part of
the story. The stronger evidence, in Where it breaks, compares the network’s pictures with its own
60,000 training digits, and they sit about as far from those as brand-new real digits do.

### The ideal denoiser makes up its mind

The ideal denoiser spreads its weight over many references at first, so its guess at the clean
picture is a blurry blend. At seed 7 one reference holds half the weight after 15 calls and 99%
after 32, and from then on its guess is that picture. The remaining calls clear away the leftover
static around it.

### Guessing static is hardest when there is little of it

The network guesses the static worst at step 0 (error 0.60) and almost perfectly at step 99 (under
0.001). That can seem backwards, since you might expect a lightly damaged picture to be the simplest
one to fix.

Picture a friend who tries to weigh a single letter by stepping on a bathroom scale with it and then
without it, and taking the difference. The letter is so light that the scale’s tiny errors swamp it,
yet the same trick works well for a heavy suitcase.

At step 0 the static is the letter, because the noise share there is only about 0.025. To get the
static out of a noisy pixel, you take away the clean part and then divide by the noise share. You
divide because the static was multiplied by that share on the way in. Dividing by 0.025 is the same
as multiplying by 40, so any mistake in the clean part grows 40 times. Being off by 0.01 on a pixel’s clean value,
for example, means being off by 0.4 in the guessed static, because 0.01 ÷ 0.025 = 0.4.

At step 99 the static is the suitcase, since the picture there is almost entirely static, so
guessing the static comes close to reading off the picture itself. Even at its worst, the network
beats the dotted line, which sits at 1.03 for always guessing zero static.

The flip side is that at step 99 the digit is the letter. Only 3% of it is left, so even a tiny slip
in the static guess becomes about 32 times bigger in the guess at the clean picture. That is how
`predicted x̂₀` can still be a blurry blend after the first call, even though the static guess is
nearly perfect. It is also another way to see why one jump from static to a digit does not work.

### Neighbours on the map look alike

Neighbouring points on the map give neighbouring digits, so a small move never makes the picture
jump suddenly. A map like this, with no sudden jumps anywhere, is called **continuous**, and that is
what makes a two-number code something you can steer around.

Each digit owns a region, and the regions meet in a crowded middle, where the decoder blurs. Near
the centre, the codes of several different digits sit close together, so a point there could belong
to any of them. The decoder hedges by drawing a faint mix, as in the flash-a-digit game. Eight
of the ten digits show up among the 12 tiles closest to the centre. The lab’s makers checked those
tiles with a separate network trained only to read digits, which gives each picture a confidence
score from 0 to 1. Its average score for those middle tiles was only 0.70, against 0.96 for tiles
more than 3 from the centre.

### Two numbers is a tight squeeze

The `7` comes back looking like a 3, because the encoder files it among the 3s. Its best-guess spot
is (1.55, 0.02), very close to where the `3` preset lands. Its BCE of 211.6 nats is also far worse
than the 3’s 121.1, because the copy misses so much of the drawing.

The `3` comes back as a blurry 3, and the `0` and `1` presets come back as themselves. That makes
the `7`, with its heavy bar across the top, the odd one out. Two numbers are a tight squeeze, so a
wrong digit is possible, not only a blurry one.

### The wobble hardly shows

For the `3` and `7` presets, σ is only about 0.03 to 0.05, while the target cloud’s spread is 1.
That is why `decoder(μ)` and `decoder(μ + σ·ε)` look almost identical, since the wobble is tiny next
to the distance between different digits on the map. The encoder pins each drawing to a very small
patch, and the KL term charges it a penalty for doing so.

That penalty is most of the KL row. For the `3`, about 6 of its 7.12 nats come from that tiny σ, and
only about 1 from its distance to the centre. That is why the `7`, slightly further out but with a
somewhat bigger σ, has the lower KL, at 6.26.

### Two starting points that are nearly the same

`Noised x₀` and `Pure noise` lead to almost the same final picture. For a given seed, `Noised x₀`
uses the very same static as `Pure noise` and adds only 3% of the digit on top. That 3% is all that
is left of the digit by the last step of the forward process. The two starting points are therefore
nearly identical before the clean-up even begins. A different seed changes the static itself, and
that does change the digit.

## Where it breaks

This lab is honest about its models, which are both small, and neither one is a production system,
meaning the large kind that real products run on.

### What this lab simplifies or leaves out

- **The trained denoiser is small and cannot be steered:** you cannot ask it for a 4, because it
  takes no label, so the seed picks the digit. It has 1,181,217 adjustable numbers and only ever saw
  28 × 28 handwritten digits.
- **Most of its pictures are clear, but not all:** the digit-reading network looked at 256 of its
  pictures made from pure static. It read them with 0.95 average confidence, against 0.99 for real
  digits. It scored more than 0.9 for 86% of them, and the rest are malformed or ambiguous.
- **Its pictures are new, not copies:** on average they sit 4.45 away from the nearest training
  digit, against 4.11 for real test digits. Bigger means more different, so the network’s pictures
  sit a little further from its training digits than brand-new real digits do. Only 2% of them sit
  within 1.5 of a training digit, against 4% of real digits.
- **Those distances use their own scale**, which is measured a different way from the lab’s 0.26, so
  compare them only with each other.
- **The ideal denoiser is not a network:** it is exact math over 64 digits that the VAE drew. It
  shows what a perfect denoiser would do on that set, and it can only ever give back one of
  those 64. From pure static, seeds 1 to 24 land on 20 different ones.
- **The noise plan does not quite reach zero.** Its last step keeps 3% of the digit, so starting
  from a noised digit or from pure static gives almost the same picture.
- **There is no steering and no shortcut:** the lab has no text prompt or way to push the model
  toward a digit, and every picture takes all 100 calls.
- **The map’s middle is crowded:** two numbers cannot keep ten digits apart, so the regions of
  several digits overlap near the centre.
- **Real digits spread wider than the target cloud:** the packing rule is weighted at only 0.35, so
  the codes of real digits spread out further than the cloud. In fact, 15% of real digits land far
  out, more than 3 from the centre, where only about 1 random pick in 100 goes.
- **Random picks often make poor digits.** Random picks come from the narrower cloud, so they mostly
  land in the crowded middle and rarely reach the clear outer regions. In a test, only 48% of 10,000
  picks came out as a clearly readable digit, against 86% for the diffusion network.
- **The VAE uses two numbers on purpose:** two numbers let the lab draw the codes as a flat map, but
  they force the digits to overlap heavily and blur. Real image systems keep far more than two
  numbers per picture.
- **Your drawings are not MNIST digits:** the MNIST digits were resized and centred in a standard
  way before training, and drawings and presets skip that step. A digit drawn very small, very thick
  or off to one side encodes worse than a dataset digit would.
- **The error chart comes from one picture:** the 0.60 at step 0 is for this one digit and one draw
  of static. Averaged over the 10,000 test digits, the network’s error at step 0 is 0.13.
- **The default digit is a harder case**, because the decoder drew it with a soft grey background.
  The presets, with flat black backgrounds, score 0.07 to 0.10 at step 0.
- **Some numbers are quoted, not measured live.** The digit-reader’s scores and where the test
  digits land were measured beforehand and copied in, because the lab carries no MNIST images and no
  digit-reading network.

### Where the real mechanisms fall short

VAE output is blurry, and that is true of real systems too, not only of this small lab. Think back
to the flash-a-digit game from Why it’s here, where a faint mix of a 3 and a 5 lost fewer points
than a crisp wrong guess.

The VAE’s training score works the same way, because it checks the copy pixel by pixel and charges
more for bigger misses. When several answers are plausible, their average scores better than
committing to one of them, so the decoder learns to hedge.

This lab’s training score is also a loosened version of the textbook one. Weighting the packing rule
below 1 buys sharper copies at the cost of a less tidy map, so it is a trade-off rather than a free
win.

Diffusion is slow, because every step is a full run of the network and each step needs the one
before it. Faster methods take bigger steps, but every step they take still costs a full network
run.

When a text description steers a diffusion model, a setting called guidance strength decides how
hard the model is pushed toward the description. Too high a setting gives a scorched,
over-contrasted look.

Measuring how good a generative model is turns out to be awkward for both families. One way to grade
a model is to show it real pictures it never trained on and ask how likely it thinks each one is. A
good model should rate real pictures as likely. A text model can give that answer exactly, piece by
piece. A VAE can only give a number that the true answer is at least as big as, and a diffusion
model gives a similar limit or a costly estimate. The common scores for image quality compare broad
patterns across many pictures, so they can miss problems that a person spots at a glance.

**A common mix-up:** “A perfect denoiser would invent brand-new digits.” The ideal denoiser shows
the opposite: the perfect answer, for a fixed set of training images, is to reproduce one of them.
Real models make new images partly *because* they are imperfect, and big models have still been
caught copying training images that appeared many times.

## Going deeper

### Why a real network can make new pictures

The ideal denoiser shows that the exact best answer to the training task, on a fixed set of
pictures, is to memorize them. Trained networks make new pictures because they never reach that
perfect answer. Their design and their limited number of adjustable numbers make them smooth over
the gaps between training pictures. As a result, their output can land between the examples instead
of on top of one.

The lab shows the two outcomes side by side. With the same static, the ideal denoiser lands exactly
on one of its 64 references, while the network draws something that is none of them. The network
never saw those 64, though, so the stronger test is against its own training digits. Measured
beforehand, its pictures sit a little further from their nearest training digit than real test
digits do, 4.45 against 4.11, so it is not copying those either. An ideal denoiser built on all
60,000 training digits would copy one of them instead.

### Using both ideas together

In practice the two ideas are used together, in a design called **latent diffusion**. A VAE-like
autoencoder first shrinks the picture about 8 times in each direction, so the grid ends up with 64
times fewer positions, since 8 × 8 = 64. All the slow diffusion work then happens in that small
space, and the decoder expands the result back to full-size pixels once, at the very end.

The small space is still a grid with a few numbers at each position, unlike this lab’s VAE, which
keeps only two numbers for the whole picture. Running every denoiser call on a much smaller grid
costs far less, and that is most of why high-quality image generation became affordable.

### Steering with words

Text prompts join through attention, the part of a network that lets each piece look at other
pieces, which the Attention lab covers. That way the denoiser guesses the static *given a
description*. A trick called **classifier-free guidance** then makes the description count for
more. At each step the model makes two guesses, one with the description and one without. The final
guess is then pushed past the one with the description, even further in the direction that the
description pulled it.

A setting usually called guidance strength controls how far that push goes. Here it is with small
numbers. Say that for one pixel the static guess without the description is 0.2, and with it is 0.5,
so the description pulled the guess up by 0.3. With a guidance strength of 3, the model moves 3
times that pull: 0.2 + 3 × 0.3 = 1.1, which goes well past 0.5.

Too low a strength and the model ignores your description, while too high gives the scorched,
over-contrasted look from Where it breaks.

### Getting there in fewer steps

The underlying math has been reworked too, and a newer approach called **flow matching** trains the
network to follow a nearly straight path from static to picture. That straighter path needs fewer
steps, and several recent image models, such as Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux, use it.

Other tricks squeeze a many-step process down to 1 to 4 steps, closing much of the speed gap with a
VAE. Each of those steps is still a full run of the network, though.

### The lab’s denoiser design

The lab’s note calls its denoiser a convolutional U-Net, which is the name of its design. It looks
at the picture through small windows that slide across it, and it works at several sizes, from fine
detail up to the overall shape of the digit.

### Diffusion for text

Diffusion has also been tried on text, refining every position of a passage at the same time instead
of one piece after another. So far its quality has trailed the usual one-piece-at-a-time models, and
it remains an area of active work.
