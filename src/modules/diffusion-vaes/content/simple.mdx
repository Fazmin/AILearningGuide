## The big picture

You may have seen a picture that an AI tool made from a few typed words. Some tools also make music and video clips in a similar way. This module opens up the machinery behind them, using the smallest pictures that still mean something to a person. Those pictures are handwritten digits from 0 to 9, each one drawn in a tiny square.

A program that makes new things, such as sentences or pictures, is called a **generative model**. Most other labs in this course look at models that write text one small piece at a time. They guess the next piece over and over. This lab is about a different family, the ones that make a whole picture at once.

The lab shows two ways of doing that, and they work almost like opposites:

- A **VAE** learns to squeeze each picture down to a code of only two numbers. It also learns to redraw a picture from any two numbers in one go. Two numbers work like the coordinates of a point on a graph. So every picture gets its own spot on a flat map.
- **Diffusion** ruins pictures on purpose by adding static a little at a time, like fuzz on an old TV. It then learns to undo that damage. So it can start from pure static and clean it up, step by step, until a digit appears.

Both are real, small models trained on 60,000 handwritten digits. Every digit they make in the lab is worked out on the spot while you watch.

### Where you have met it

The ideas in this lab are the machinery behind image, audio and video generation. In fact, many modern image generators use both ideas at once. They start with a squeezer much like the VAE that shrinks the picture. All the slow diffusion work happens in that smaller space. The result gets expanded back into a full picture at the very end. That combination is most of why making high-quality images with AI became affordable.

### Why it matters

To make a picture, a program must choose a brightness for every pixel. A pixel is one of the tiny squares that make up a picture. Here there are 784 of them. Those pixels all have to agree with each other. A stroke that starts on one side of a digit must carry on and join up with the rest. If you choose every pixel at random, you get static, because nothing makes neighbouring pixels line up.

Yet a generative model needs some randomness, or it would draw the same picture every time. Both models in this lab answer the same puzzle. How do you turn random input into pixels that fit together? Without the VAE's learned map of codes, two random numbers would mean nothing. Without diffusion's learned clean-up, random static would stay static.

The two answers come with very different costs, and real systems are designed around them. The VAE is quick, because its drawing step runs only once per picture. But its pictures tend to come out blurry. Diffusion's digits in this lab are readable far more often. It pays for that by running its whole clean-up calculation again for each of its 100 steps.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how many small clean-up steps differ from rebuilding a picture from a short code in one step;
- why the first way is slow, while the second way tends to give blurry pictures;
- how to read a map where each point is a picture's code, and why nearby points give similar digits;
- why the crowded middle of that map gives blurry blends of two or more digits;
- why a perfect clean-up tool would only copy its examples, while an imperfect network can make new pictures.

## What it is

### A picture is a grid of numbers

Every picture in this lab is a small square, 28 pixels across and 28 pixels down. So there are 784 pixels in all. The computer stores each pixel as a single number for its brightness. In the diffusion half of the lab, −1 means black and 1 means white, with greys in between. So a digit is a white stroke on a black background.

The digits come from a collection called MNIST, and both models learned from all 60,000 of its training digits. Another 10,000 test digits were kept aside, so the models never saw them during training.

Both models are built from **neural networks**. A neural network is a large calculation with many adjustable numbers inside it. Training means nudging those numbers, over and over, until the network's answers come out right.

### The VAE: squeeze a picture into two numbers, then redraw it

Think of the character creator in a video game, where a few sliders set things like height and build. Every position of the sliders gives you a complete character. Moving a slider a little changes the character a little. The VAE builds something like a character creator for handwritten digits, with only two sliders.

It does this with two networks that train together as a team. The first, called the **encoder**, looks at a 784-pixel picture and squeezes it down to only two numbers. The second, called the **decoder**, takes any two numbers and expands them into a 784-pixel picture.

During training, each digit goes through the encoder and then the decoder. The pair is scored on how closely the copy matches the original. Only two numbers fit through the middle. So those two numbers have to hold whatever matters most about each digit.

The pair of numbers is called the picture's **latent**, a word that means hidden. It is a compressed code you never see in the picture itself. The lab calls the two numbers `z₁` and `z₂`. You can treat them like map coordinates, with `z₁` saying how far across and `z₂` how far up. So every code is a point on a flat map, which is the **Latent map** card in the lab.

VAE is short for **variational autoencoder**. An autoencoder is a model that learns to turn a picture into a code and back into a copy. The word "variational" comes from the math used to train it, which you won't need here.

The character creator stops matching the VAE in three ways:

- **Who sets the meaning:** In a game, designers decide what each slider does, such as setting the height. Nobody told the VAE what its two numbers should mean, so training settled that.
- **Which way it goes:** A character creator goes only one way, from sliders to a character. The VAE learns the return trip too, from a finished picture back to its two numbers.
- **What every setting gives:** In a game, every slider setting gives a proper character. Some points on the VAE's map give only a blurry mix of digits.

### Keeping the codes packed together

Suppose the encoder were free to put codes anywhere. It might push the 0s far off to one side and the 7s far off to the other. That would leave wide empty gaps, and a code for a brand-new digit could land in one. No training digit's code ever landed in such a gap. So the decoder was never taught a digit there.

So the VAE adds a second rule to its training score. The codes should stay packed in a round cloud centred on the point (0, 0). That target cloud is called the **prior**, a word that means "before looking at any picture". The lab draws it on the map as two dashed circles.

The penalty for not fitting the cloud is called the **KL divergence**. It compares each picture's code with the target cloud and charges for two things. One is a code whose spot sits far from the centre. The other is the size of a small wobble around each code. You will meet that wobble in the next section. Suppose the rule worked perfectly. Then you could hand any random point from the cloud to the decoder and get a believable digit back.

This lab applies the rule lightly. It multiplies the KL penalty by 0.35, about a third of full strength. Then it adds the result to the training score. As a result, real digits' codes spread wider than the target cloud. Random picks, though, come from the cloud itself. Most of them land near the centre, where the regions of several digits overlap. Only about half of those picks become a clear digit, as Where it breaks shows.

### A best-guess spot with a small wobble

The encoder actually gives a little more than two plain numbers. For each of the two, it gives a best-guess spot, called the mean and written μ (mu). It also gives a small spread around that spot, written σ (sigma). You can read its answer as "the code is about here, give or take σ".

During training, the decoder does not receive the exact spot. It gets a point picked at random from the small patch around μ. That way the decoder has to draw well for the whole patch, and not only for one exact point. The lab writes the pick like this. Here ε (epsilon) is a random number, usually between about −2 and 2, and it is rolled before anything else:

`z = μ + σ·ε`

Here is a worked example with a ready-made drawing of a 3 that the lab offers. You can load it with the `3` button in **Encode a drawing**. For this drawing, μ for `z₁` is 1.46 and σ is about 0.03. Say the random roll gives ε = 0.5. Then z₁ = 1.46 + 0.03 × 0.5, which comes to 1.475. The wobble moves the point only a tiny distance, because σ is so small.

Rolling ε first has its own name, **reparameterization**, and it solves a practical problem. Training keeps asking questions like this one: if μ were a little bigger, would the score get better or worse? Suppose z were only "some random point near μ". Then no rule would link a change in μ to a change in z. The question would have no answer.

Writing `z = μ + σ·ε` fixes this. The roll ε is set aside first, like a dice roll written down before the game. Then nudging μ up by 0.1 moves z up by exactly 0.1. Everything after the roll is ordinary arithmetic, so training can see how μ and σ affect the score.

The KL penalty from the previous section looks at this wobble too. It compares each picture's small patch with the whole target cloud, whose spread is 1. A patch far narrower than 1 counts as a poor fit, so it costs points too.

### Diffusion: ruin pictures on purpose, then learn to undo it

Picture someone learning to restore old photos by practising on photos that were ruined on purpose. You take a clean photo and sprinkle a little static over it. Then you add a little more, until nothing is left but static. At every stage, the trainee studies the damaged photo. They try to point out exactly which specks are static. After enough practice, they can take a photo covered in static and remove some of it. More of the picture underneath then shows through.

Diffusion works the same way, in two halves. The first half, adding static, takes no skill at all. The second half is working out which part of a picture is static. That is the part that has to be learned.

The photo restorer stops matching in one important way. A restorer always has a real photo hidden under the static. When diffusion makes a new picture, it starts from static with nothing under it at all. So the clean-up itself builds the digit that appears.

### Adding static: the forward process

The first half is called the **forward process**. It adds static to a clean digit over 100 steps, numbered from t = 0 to t = 99. Here t stands for the step. The lab labels the clean digit `x₀`, meaning the picture before any static at all. A fixed plan called the **noise schedule** says how much static goes in at each step. Nobody trains this plan and no network is involved, so this half is pure arithmetic.

At every step, each pixel of the noisy picture mixes the clean pixel with a random static value:

`noisy pixel = signal share × clean pixel + noise share × static`

The static is a separate random number for each pixel, mostly between −2 and 2. The lab writes it as ε, the same Greek letter as before. As the steps go by, the signal share falls from about 1 toward 0. The noise share rises from about 0 toward 1. Under each tile of the strip, the lab prints the signal share as `√ᾱ`. Read it as "the square root of alpha-bar".

Here is the mix worked out at step t = 59, where the tile reads 0.60 for the signal share. The noise share there is 0.80. It is not printed under the tile, but it follows from a rule explained below:

- **A white pixel:** It has the value 1. If its static is −0.5, it becomes 0.60 × 1 + 0.80 × (−0.5), which is 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20.
- **A black background pixel:** It has the value −1. If its static is 0.5, it becomes 0.60 × (−1) + 0.80 × 0.5, which is −0.60 + 0.40 = −0.20.

Both pixels end up as greys close to zero, so they are much harder to tell apart. That is how the digit fades into the static. You may also notice that 0.60 and 0.80 behave like the sides of a 3-4-5 triangle. Their squares, 0.36 and 0.64, add up to exactly 1. The schedule keeps that true at every step. The rule stops the pixel values from swinging steadily wider, or shrinking toward zero, as the static takes over. It also lets you work out either share from the other.

The plan has to end with almost nothing of the picture left. That is because a new picture starts from fresh static. The network only knows what to do with the kind of input it practised on. So the last forward step has to look like plain static too. Here the last step keeps only 3% of the digit. That is the 0.03 under the last tile, or 0.031 to three decimal places.

### Guessing the static: the denoiser

The second half needs learning. A network called the **denoiser** is trained to guess the static that was added to a noisy picture. It is given the noisy picture and its step number. One network handles all 100 steps, because the step number tells it how much static to expect.

Training runs as a loop. Take a digit, pick a random step, and add static using the mix above. Then ask the denoiser to guess that static. Then score the guess by how far off it is. Nudge the network's adjustable numbers so that it does a little better next time.

The lab's denoiser has 1,181,217 adjustable numbers. It was trained for 45 passes over all 60,000 MNIST digits, so it saw every training digit 45 times. Its error is the average squared gap between its guess and the real static, across all 784 pixels. By the end of training, that error was 0.036. A lazy guess of "no static anywhere" scores 1.0.

Guessing the static is useful because you can then take it away. Once you know what static was added, you can work out the clean picture. The lab shows that worked-out picture with the caption `predicted x̂₀`. The little hat on top means "a guess at". Beside it, `predicted ε̂` shows the guessed static itself, with mid-grey standing for zero.

### Cleaning up: the reverse process

Making a new picture is called the **reverse process**. It starts by handing 784 numbers of fresh static to the denoiser, which guesses the static in it. An update then makes three small moves, and the result is a slightly cleaner picture. The denoiser is called again on that picture, for 100 calls in all. Each move has a reason:

- **It removes only one step's worth of the guessed static**, not all of it. Each call is meant to undo one forward step. Why not remove it all at once? Why it's here answers that, under "One jump from static to a digit is too big".
- **It scales the picture up very slightly.** Each forward step shrank the picture a little as the signal share fell. So the way back has to grow it again.
- **It sprinkles in a little fresh static.** Each forward step added static at random. So many slightly different pictures could have come right before this one. The step back picks one of them at random. That keeps the right amount of static for the next call. It also helps stop the run from drifting toward a smooth, blurry average of many possible digits.

The very last call skips the fresh static, because its output is meant to be the finished picture.

The **Reverse diffusion** card prints the update for the next call with its real numbers filled in. The recipe is called **DDPM**, the name of the standard step-by-step method that this lab uses. The calls work backward through the steps. Call 1 cleans the picture at step 99, call 2 cleans the one at step 98, and so on. When you first open the lab, the **Denoiser calls** slider sits at 62, so the line shows call 63. That call works at step 99 − 62 = 37. It turns the picture at step 37 into the picture at step 36:

`x_36 = (x_37 − 0.0359 · ε̂) × 1.0102 + 0.1415 · z`

In that line, `x_37` is the current picture and `ε̂` is the guessed static. The letter `z` stands for the fresh static, and `x_36` is the slightly cleaner picture that comes out. Follow one pixel with a value of 0.50. The guessed static is 2.0 and the fresh static is −0.3:

1. Remove part of the guess: 0.50 − 0.0359 × 2.0 = 0.4282.
2. Scale up slightly: 0.4282 × 1.0102 is about 0.4326.
3. Sprinkle in fresh static: 0.4326 + 0.1415 × (−0.3) is about 0.39.

That pixel moved from 0.50 to about 0.39. Most calls change the picture only a little like this. The trip from static to a digit has been split into 100 small steps. The numbers in the line change from call to call. Each step of the schedule added a different amount of static.

### A perfect denoiser to compare against

The lab offers a second denoiser, the `Ideal denoiser`. It is not a network at all but exact math. It works with 64 reference digits that the VAE's decoder drew. They sit in a grid 8 across and 8 down. At every call, it compares the noisy picture with each reference, faded to match the current step. It gives each reference a share of weight by how well it matches. The shares always add up to 100%.

Its guess at the clean picture is a blend of the references. Each one counts as much as its share. Suppose it gave 75% of the weight to one reference and 25% to another. Its guess would be three parts of the first mixed with one part of the second. While the weight is spread over many references, the guess looks blurry. Once one reference holds almost all of it, the guess becomes that reference exactly.

For those 64 digits, no denoiser could guess the static better, which is why the lab calls it ideal. That perfection has a surprising cost, though. The ideal denoiser can only ever give back one of its 64 references. Here is why, in three steps:

1. Its guess at the clean picture is always a blend of the 64 references. Those are the only clean pictures it knows.
2. As the static thins out, one reference ends up matching far better than the rest. So the blend narrows to that single picture.
3. The finished result is therefore an exact copy of that one reference.

Picture two friends getting ready for an art quiz with the same 64 sample drawings. One memorizes all 64 perfectly, so whatever the quiz asks, they reproduce the closest sample line for line. The other cannot hold every line in memory and picks up the general look instead. Their drawings resemble the samples without matching any of them.

The ideal denoiser is like the first friend, and the trained network is more like the second. The analogy bends in two places, though. The trained network did not study the 64 references at all. It learned from a much bigger set, all 60,000 MNIST training digits. And the ideal denoiser memorizes nothing, because it is handed the 64 references ready-made. The static gradually settles which reference it ends on, so it does not choose at the start.

### Two opposite designs, side by side

Set next to each other, the two designs differ at almost every point:

- **Where a new picture starts:** The VAE starts from two numbers, which make a point on its map. Diffusion starts from 784 numbers of static.
- **What gets learned:** The VAE learns a map of codes and a way to draw any point on it. Diffusion learns to guess the static in a noisy picture.
- **How many network runs each picture takes:** The VAE's decoder runs once. Diffusion's denoiser runs 100 times, one call after another.
- **The usual flaw:** VAE pictures tend to come out blurry. Diffusion is slow.

The big practical difference is patience. Picture a relay race with 100 runners, where each runner has to wait for the baton before setting off. Even with very fast runners, you cannot finish sooner by starting them all at once. Diffusion's calls work the same way. Call 50 needs the picture that call 49 produced, so they cannot run side by side. The race stops matching in one way. Each call changes the baton, because it hands on a slightly cleaner picture.

## Why it’s here

### One jump from static to a digit is too big

You might wonder why diffusion needs 100 calls. Why not train one network to turn static straight into a digit? From pure static, every digit is about equally likely. So the safest single guess is a blurry blend of many digits at once.

To see why a blend is "safest", picture a game. A friend flashes a digit at you for a split second, and you have to redraw it. On every pixel, you lose points by how far its brightness is from the right one. A big miss costs far more than a small one. Say you can't tell whether it was a 3 or a 5. A faint mix of both is only a little wrong almost everywhere. A crisp wrong guess is badly wrong on many pixels. So on average the faint mix loses fewer points.

A similar score trains a network to make the whole jump in one go. So it learns to draw that blurry mix. You can see this in the lab. With the `Ideal denoiser`, the `predicted x̂₀` picture is a soft blend of many references at first.

Diffusion avoids getting stuck with that blur by splitting the trip into 100 small steps. Each call moves the picture a short way toward the current guess. It also adds a pinch of fresh static. Then it asks again. As the static thins out, the guess gets sharper. The picture gradually settles on a single digit. Each step is also a much smaller job than the whole trip. The denoiser only has to deal with the static at one level.

### Pixels are a poor place to mix pictures

The VAE solves a different problem. It gives you a space for moving smoothly from one picture to another, which pixels alone are bad at. Average a 0 and a 2 pixel by pixel. You get a faint 0 lying on top of a faint 2, which looks like neither digit.

On the VAE's map, every point is a code that the decoder turns into one picture. A small move on the map gives a small change in the picture. So you can walk from one digit to another and watch the shape change along the way.

The map can blur too, in two kinds of places:

- **Gaps between regions:** Few or no training codes landed there, so the decoder never learned a digit. It still draws something, because its output changes smoothly from point to point. What it draws is a blurry mix of the digits on either side.
- **The crowded middle:** The codes of several digits sit close together there. So a point could belong to any of them. The decoder hedges between them, for the same reason as in the flash-a-digit game.

Even so, the VAE's walk is far better than averaging pixels, which gives a double image the whole way. Along most of the walk, the decoder draws one digit-like shape that changes a little at a time. The blur shows up only in the stretches where one digit's region hands over to the next. Finding those stretches is part of reading the map. In most models the latent stays hidden deep inside the machinery, but here it becomes somewhere you can explore.

### Not every generative model guesses the next piece

The text models in other labs build their answer one piece at a time, from left to right. Once a word is written, it stays. Diffusion works on the whole picture at every step instead. So even a late call can still adjust a stroke in the top-left corner. A text model of that kind cannot go back and change a word it has already written.

### Walk around the latent map

Switch **Model** to `VAE`. The **Latent map** card shows a mosaic of 256 small digits in a 16 × 16 grid. Each tile is what the decoder draws for the code at that tile's centre. The map runs from −4 to 4 in both directions, with `z₁` going across and `z₂` going up. The two dashed circles mark the target cloud, at distances of 1 and 2 from the centre. The lab labels them 1σ and 2σ. This σ is the target cloud's spread, which is 1. It is not the encoder's tiny wobble from earlier, even though the two share the same letter.

Drag the point, which starts at (−2.00, −0.80) among the 5s on the left. Watch the big digit beside the map change smoothly. You can also move it one direction at a time with the **Latent z₁** and **Latent z₂** sliders. They move in steps of 0.05.

Two readouts sit under the sliders. **‖z‖** is how far your point is from the centre. It is measured in units of the target cloud's spread. That spread is 1, so the number is the plain distance. You can check it with Pythagoras. At the starting point it is √((−2)² + (−0.8)²) = √(4 + 0.64), about 2.15.

**log p(z)** says how often random picks from the target cloud land near your point. It uses a squeezed scale measured in **nats**. Each drop of about 2.3 means random picks land there about 10 times less often. The closer it is to zero, the more often random picks land near that spot. It reads about −1.84 at the centre and −4.16 at the starting point, a drop of about 2.3. So the starting point gets about one tenth as many random picks as the centre. It drops faster the further out you go.

Now try a planned route. Start on the 0s along the bottom. Go up the right side through the 2s to the 1s at the top right. From there, go along the top to the 7s at the top left. Watch the picture change as you walk from one code to another, usually in a straight line. This is called **latent interpolation**. For a straight-line try, pick any two digits and drag the point straight from one to the other. Smooth change tells you the map is well organized. A blurry stretch marks a spot where one digit's region hands over to another. Finish by dragging through the crowded middle and watching the digit blur.

### Squeeze your own drawing

In **Encode a drawing**, click the `7` button and then the `3` button. You can also draw your own digit with a mouse or pen. Each stroke goes to the encoder when you lift the mouse button or pen, and `Clear` wipes the pad. The card shows two copies, and one is `decoder(μ)`, drawn from the encoder's best-guess spot. The other is `decoder(μ + σ·ε)`, drawn from a point wobbled at random. The encoded spot also appears on the **Latent map** as a diamond. An oval around it shows twice its wobble.

The numbers beside the copies describe this one drawing:

- **Posterior mean μ:** This is the encoder's best-guess spot. "Posterior" means "after looking at the drawing", the partner word to prior.
- **Posterior σ (prior σ = 1):** This is the size of the wobble, shown next to the target cloud's spread of 1.
- **Reconstruction BCE, decoder(μ):** This scores how far the copy is from your drawing. The score adds up over all 784 pixels, in nats. Nats are the unit these scores are counted in. The KL and training-loss rows use the same unit, so the scores can be added together. BCE is short for binary cross-entropy, and lower is better. The pen leaves soft grey edges, so even a perfect copy would score a little above 0.
- **The row starting `KL`:** This is the packing penalty for this drawing, the KL divergence from earlier.
- **Training loss, BCE + 0.35 × KL:** This is this drawing's training score, worked out from the `decoder(μ)` copy. Training itself scored the wobbled copy, but the two are almost the same here. The note under the numbers shows this.

For the `3` preset, the BCE is 121.1 nats and the KL is 7.12. Multiplying the KL by 0.35 gives about 2.49, so the training loss comes to about 123.6 nats. Almost all of that score comes from the copy's mistakes, and very little comes from the packing rule.

## What to notice

### Making static is arithmetic, but removing it takes a network

Adding static is one line of arithmetic for each tile, with nothing to learn. Only the reverse direction needs a trained network. That is the direction that goes from static back toward a digit.

### Seed 7 gives one new digit and one copy

With seed 7 from pure noise, the trained network turns static into a digit. Its **Background pixels** number goes from 0.17 to 0.82. That means about 1 pixel in 6 is near black at the start. At the end, about 4 in 5 are.

The `Ideal denoiser`, given exactly the same static, lands exactly on reference #64. The network's picture is nearest to reference #28, at 0.26, and is none of them.

That 0.26 is a mean squared distance. To get it, square the gap between the two pictures at every pixel, then average those squares. A score of 0 would mean a perfect copy. A distance of 0.26 is what you get if every pixel is off by about 0.5. The scale runs from −1 for black to 1 for white. Across every seed on the slider, the network's picture never comes closer than 0.07 to any reference.

The network did not learn from those 64 references, so missing them is only part of the story. The stronger evidence is in Where it breaks. It compares the network's pictures with its own 60,000 training digits. They sit about as far from those as brand-new real digits do.

### The ideal denoiser makes up its mind

The ideal denoiser spreads its weight over many references at first. So its guess at the clean picture is a blurry blend. At seed 7, one reference holds half the weight after 15 calls and 99% after 32. From then on, its guess is that picture. The remaining calls clear away the leftover static around it.

### Guessing static is hardest when there is little of it

The network guesses the static worst at step 0 (error 0.60) and almost perfectly at step 99 (under 0.001). That can seem backwards, since you might expect a lightly damaged picture to be the simplest one to fix.

Picture a friend who tries to weigh a single letter. They step on a bathroom scale with it, then without it, and take the difference. The letter is so light that the scale's tiny errors swamp it. Yet the same trick works well for a heavy suitcase.

At step 0 the static is the letter, because the noise share there is only about 0.025. To get the static out of a noisy pixel, take away the clean part. Then divide by the noise share. You divide because the static was multiplied by that share on the way in. Dividing by 0.025 is the same as multiplying by 40. So any mistake in the clean part grows 40 times. Say you are off by 0.01 on a pixel's clean value. Then you are off by 0.4 in the guessed static, because 0.01 ÷ 0.025 = 0.4.

At step 99 the static is the suitcase. The picture there is almost entirely static, so guessing the static comes close to reading off the picture itself. Even at its worst, the network beats the dotted line, which sits at 1.03 for always guessing zero static.

The flip side is that at step 99 the digit is the letter. Only 3% of it is left. So a tiny slip in the static guess grows about 32 times in the guess at the clean picture. So `predicted x̂₀` can still be a blurry blend after the first call. The static guess itself is nearly perfect. It is also another way to see why one jump from static to a digit does not work.

### Neighbours on the map look alike

Neighbouring points on the map give neighbouring digits, so a small move never makes the picture jump suddenly. A map like this, with no sudden jumps anywhere, is called **continuous**. That is what makes a two-number code something you can steer around.

Each digit owns a region, and the regions meet in a crowded middle, where the decoder blurs. Near the centre, the codes of several different digits sit close together. So a point there could belong to any of them. The decoder hedges by drawing a faint mix, as in the flash-a-digit game. Eight of the ten digits show up among the 12 tiles closest to the centre. The lab's makers checked those tiles with a separate network trained only to read digits. It gives each picture a confidence score from 0 to 1. Its average score for those middle tiles was only 0.70. Tiles more than 3 from the centre scored 0.96.

### Two numbers is a tight squeeze

The `7` comes back looking like a 3, because the encoder files it among the 3s. Its best-guess spot is (1.55, 0.02), very close to where the `3` preset lands. Its BCE of 211.6 nats is also far worse than the 3's 121.1. The copy misses so much of the drawing.

The `3` comes back as a blurry 3, and the `0` and `1` presets come back as themselves. That makes the `7`, with its heavy bar across the top, the odd one out. Two numbers are a tight squeeze, so a wrong digit is possible, not only a blurry one.

### The wobble hardly shows

For the `3` and `7` presets, σ is only about 0.03 to 0.05, while the target cloud's spread is 1. That is why `decoder(μ)` and `decoder(μ + σ·ε)` look almost identical. The wobble is tiny next to the distance between different digits on the map. The encoder pins each drawing to a very small patch. The KL term charges a penalty for that.

That penalty is most of the KL row. For the `3`, about 6 of its 7.12 nats come from that tiny σ. Only about 1 comes from its distance to the centre. That is why the `7` has the lower KL, at 6.26. It sits slightly further out, but its σ is somewhat bigger.

### Two starting points that are nearly the same

`Noised x₀` and `Pure noise` lead to almost the same final picture. For a given seed, `Noised x₀` uses the very same static as `Pure noise`. It adds only 3% of the digit on top. That 3% is all that is left of the digit by the last step of the forward process. So the two starting points are nearly identical before the clean-up even begins. A different seed changes the static itself, and that does change the digit.

## Where it breaks

This lab is honest about its models. Both are small, and neither one is a production system. That means neither is the large kind that real products run on.

### What this lab simplifies or leaves out

- **The trained denoiser is small and cannot be steered.** You cannot ask it for a 4, because it takes no label, so the seed picks the digit. It has 1,181,217 adjustable numbers and only ever saw 28 × 28 handwritten digits.
- **Most of its pictures are clear, but not all.** The digit-reading network looked at 256 of its pictures made from pure static. It read them with 0.95 average confidence, against 0.99 for real digits. It scored more than 0.9 for 86% of them, and the rest are malformed or ambiguous.
- **Its pictures are new, not copies.** On average they sit 4.45 away from the nearest training digit, against 4.11 for real test digits. Bigger means more different. So the network's pictures sit a little further from its training digits than brand-new real digits do. Only 2% of them sit within 1.5 of a training digit, against 4% of real digits.
- **Those distances use their own scale.** It is measured a different way from the lab's 0.26, so compare them only with each other.
- **The ideal denoiser is not a network.** It is exact math over 64 digits that the VAE drew. It shows what a perfect denoiser would do on that set. It can only ever give back one of those 64. From pure static, seeds 1 to 24 land on 20 different ones.
- **The noise plan does not quite reach zero.** Its last step keeps 3% of the digit. So starting from a noised digit or from pure static gives almost the same picture.
- **There is no steering and no shortcut.** The lab has no text prompt and no way to push the model toward a digit. Every picture takes all 100 calls.
- **The map's middle is crowded.** Two numbers cannot keep ten digits apart, so the regions of several digits overlap near the centre.
- **Real digits spread wider than the target cloud.** The packing rule is weighted at only 0.35. So the codes of real digits spread out further than the cloud. In fact, 15% of real digits land far out, more than 3 from the centre. Only about 1 random pick in 100 goes there.
- **Random picks often make poor digits.** Random picks come from the narrower cloud, so they mostly land in the crowded middle. They rarely reach the clear outer regions. In a test, only 48% of 10,000 picks gave a clear digit, against 86% for the diffusion network.
- **The VAE uses two numbers on purpose.** Two numbers let the lab draw the codes as a flat map. But they force the digits to overlap heavily and blur. Real image systems keep far more than two numbers per picture.
- **Your drawings are not MNIST digits.** The MNIST digits were resized and centred in a standard way before training. Drawings and presets skip that step. A digit drawn very small, very thick or off to one side encodes worse than a dataset digit would.
- **The error chart comes from one picture.** The 0.60 at step 0 is for this one digit and one draw of static. Averaged over the 10,000 test digits, the network's error at step 0 is 0.13.
- **The default digit is a harder case.** The decoder drew it with a soft grey background. The presets, with flat black backgrounds, score 0.07 to 0.10 at step 0.
- **Some numbers are quoted, not measured live.** The digit-reader's scores and where the test digits land were measured beforehand and copied in. The lab carries no MNIST images and no digit-reading network.

### Where the real mechanisms fall short

VAE output is blurry, and that is true of real systems too, not only of this small lab. Think back to the flash-a-digit game from Why it's here. A faint mix of a 3 and a 5 lost fewer points than a crisp wrong guess.

The VAE's training score works the same way. It checks the copy pixel by pixel and charges more for bigger misses. When several answers are plausible, their average scores better than committing to one of them. So the decoder learns to hedge.

This lab's training score is also a loosened version of the textbook one. Weighting the packing rule below 1 buys sharper copies at the cost of a less tidy map. It is a trade-off, not a free win.

Diffusion is slow. Every step is a full run of the network, and each step needs the one before it. Faster methods take bigger steps, but every step they take still costs a full network run.

A text description can steer a diffusion model. A setting called guidance strength decides how hard the description pushes. Too high a setting gives a scorched, over-contrasted look.

Measuring how good a generative model is turns out to be awkward for both families. One way to grade a model is to show it real pictures it never trained on. You then ask how likely it thinks each one is. A good model should rate real pictures as likely. A text model can give that answer exactly, piece by piece. A VAE can only give a number that the true answer is at least as big as. A diffusion model gives a similar limit or a costly estimate. The common scores for image quality compare broad patterns across many pictures. So they can miss problems that a person spots at a glance.

**A common mix-up:** "A perfect denoiser would invent brand-new digits." The ideal denoiser shows the opposite. For a fixed set of training images, the perfect answer is to reproduce one of them. Real models make new images partly *because* they are imperfect. Big models have still been caught copying training images that appeared many times.

## Going deeper

### Why a real network can make new pictures

The ideal denoiser shows the exact best answer to the training task on a fixed set of pictures. That answer is to memorize them. Trained networks make new pictures because they never reach that perfect answer. Their design and their limited number of adjustable numbers make them smooth over the gaps between training pictures. As a result, their output can land between the examples instead of on top of one.

The lab shows the two outcomes side by side. With the same static, the ideal denoiser lands exactly on one of its 64 references. The network draws something that is none of them. The network never saw those 64, though, so the stronger test is against its own training digits. Measured beforehand, its pictures sit a little further from their nearest training digit than real test digits do. That is 4.45 against 4.11, so it is not copying those either. An ideal denoiser built on all 60,000 training digits would copy one of them instead.

### Using both ideas together

In practice the two ideas are used together, in a design called **latent diffusion**. A VAE-like autoencoder first shrinks the picture about 8 times in each direction. The grid then has 64 times fewer positions, since 8 × 8 = 64. All the slow diffusion work happens in that small space. The decoder expands the result back to full-size pixels once, at the very end.

The small space is still a grid with a few numbers at each position. This lab's VAE is different, because it keeps only two numbers for the whole picture. Running every denoiser call on a much smaller grid costs far less. That is most of why high-quality image generation became affordable.

### Steering with words

Text prompts join through attention, the part of a network that lets each piece look at other pieces. The Attention lab covers it. That way the denoiser guesses the static *given a description*. A trick called **classifier-free guidance** then makes the description count for more. At each step the model makes two guesses, one with the description and one without. It then pushes the final guess past the one with the description. The push goes even further in the direction that the description pulled it.

A setting usually called guidance strength controls how far that push goes. Here it is with small numbers. Say that for one pixel the static guess without the description is 0.2, and with it is 0.5. So the description pulled the guess up by 0.3. With a guidance strength of 3, the model moves 3 times that pull. That gives 0.2 + 3 × 0.3 = 1.1, well past 0.5.

Too low a strength and the model ignores your description. Too high gives the scorched, over-contrasted look from Where it breaks.

### Getting there in fewer steps

The underlying math has been reworked too. A newer approach called **flow matching** trains the network to follow a nearly straight path from static to picture. That straighter path needs fewer steps. Several recent image models, such as Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux, use it.

Other tricks squeeze the process down to 1 to 4 steps. That closes much of the speed gap with a VAE. Each of those steps is still a full run of the network, though.

### The lab's denoiser design

The lab's note calls its denoiser a convolutional U-Net, which is the name of its design. It looks at the picture through small windows that slide across it. It also works at several sizes, from fine detail up to the overall shape of the digit.

### Diffusion for text

Diffusion has also been tried on text. It refines every position of a passage at the same time instead of one piece after another. So far its quality has trailed the usual one-piece-at-a-time models, and it remains an area of active work.
