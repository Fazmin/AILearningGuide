## The big picture

An AI model is a long chain of small calculations, and the numbers that steer them are called
**weights**.

Picture each weight as a dial on a huge control panel. Turning a dial changes what the model gives
back. Training is the slow work of turning those dials a little at a time. It keeps going until the
model's answers land closer to the right ones.

The control panel picture has one flaw: no person turns these dials by hand. Training turns them,
guided by numbers this lesson shows you how to find.

A big model stacks its calculations in **layers**, and a layer is a row of small calculations. Each
layer's output feeds the next layer, and each layer has its own dials. The last card of this lab
stacks up to twelve layers.

Every time the model gets a practice example wrong, training faces the same question. Which dials
should turn, in which direction, and by how much?

This module is about the method that answers that question, and it is called **backpropagation**. It
starts from the error at the very end of the chain. Then it works backward toward the dials, one step
at a time. When it finishes, every dial has a number that says which way to turn it. That number also
says how strongly the dial affects the error.

The previous lab, Loss & gradient descent, called that number a tilt. It needed a tilt for each of its
two dials and used them without saying where they came from. This lesson calls the same number a
**slope**. This lab finishes the story by showing exactly how to work it out.

### Where it shows up

Almost every slope used to train a model in this course comes from this method. That includes the
one-dial model in this lab and the language models in later labs. Those are the kind of program behind
a chatbot.

During training, backpropagation runs over and over in the background, after every round of practice
examples. Each time, it tells training how to nudge the dials.

The rare exceptions are steps that make a hard choice, such as picking one word. The end of this lesson
comes back to them.

### Why it matters

A large model has billions of dials, so the cost of finding all their slopes matters a great deal.

Without backpropagation, each dial's slope would need its own run of the whole model. One round of
training would then need billions of runs. Backpropagation finds every dial's slope in one backward
sweep. That sweep costs only about twice as much as one ordinary run of the model, from input to guess.

Backpropagation also explains a problem that shaped the design of modern models. In a model with many
layers, the slope that reaches the earliest layers can shrink to almost nothing. It can also grow out
of control, and several standard design choices exist mainly to stop either problem.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how numbers flow forward through a small model to make a guess and an error score;
- how slopes then flow backward from the error to the dial;
- how a rule called the chain rule finds the dial's slope by multiplying each step's own small slope;
- how to check that slope by measuring it;
- why the first layer of a tall stack can get almost no slope, or far too much;
- how four choices change the slope that reaches the first layer:
  - how many layers are stacked one after another;
  - which squashing step each layer applies to its numbers;
  - how big the random numbers are that the dials start at;
  - whether shortcuts let numbers skip past a layer, as well as pass through it.

## What it is

### A model with one dial

Start with the smallest model that still has every important part. It takes one input number, `x`, and
it has one dial, the weight `w`. It multiplies the two numbers and squashes the result into a number
between 0 and 1. Then it compares that guess with the right answer.

Picture it as a short assembly line with three stations. A number moves along the line, and each
station does one small calculation and passes its result on.

1. The **multiply** station works out `z = x · w`. At the lab's starting settings x is 0.8 and w is
   0.6, so z is 0.8 × 0.6, or 0.48.
2. The **sigmoid** station squashes z into a number between 0 and 1. Large positive numbers come out
   close to 1, and large negative numbers come out close to 0. If z is exactly 0, the sigmoid gives
   0.5, right in the middle. Its output is the model's guess. The lab writes it as `ŷ`, read "y-hat",
   and at the start it is 0.618.
3. The **loss** station compares the guess with the right answer, called the **target** and written
   `y`. The target starts at 0.9, and the station finds the gap between the guess and the target. It
   then squares that gap to get about 0.0797.

That last number is the **loss**, a score for how wrong the guess is. A loss of zero means a perfect
guess.

Squaring the gap does two jobs. First, it makes every miss count as a positive number, on either side
of the target. Second, it makes big misses count far more than small ones. Doubling a gap from 0.2 to
0.4 makes the loss four times bigger. Check it yourself: 0.2 × 0.2 is 0.04, and 0.4 × 0.4 is 0.16.

The whole map of stations and arrows is called a **computation graph**. Running numbers through it from
left to right is called the **forward pass**. The lab's tape writes down each forward step in a row,
with the formula, the numbers and the result:

```
z = x · w       0.80 × 0.60             =  0.480
ŷ = σ(z)        1 / (1 + e^(−0.480))    =  0.618
L = (ŷ − y)²    (0.618 − 0.90)²         =  0.0797
```

The `σ` is the Greek letter sigma, and here it stands for sigmoid. In the middle row, the tape writes
the sigmoid out in full: `1 / (1 + e^(−z))`, with z filled in. The e is a fixed number close to 2.718.
The lab does that part for you, so you never need to work it out by hand.

### The question training asks

The loss tells you how wrong the guess is, but not what to do about it. Training needs
one more fact about the dial. If `w` moved up by a tiny amount, would the loss go up or down, and how
fast?

That fact is the slope of the loss at the current weight. Picture standing on a hillside, where the
slope is how steeply the ground rises or falls under your feet.

The hill picture works here for a reason. This model has one dial, so its hill is a single curve you
can draw. A big model has millions of dials, and nobody can draw its hill.

A measure of how fast one number changes when another number moves is called a **derivative**. The
slope of the loss for one particular dial is called that dial's **gradient**.

The lab writes this slope as `∂L/∂w`. It means that a tiny nudge to w changes L by `∂L/∂w` times the
nudge. Say `∂L/∂w` were 2, so a nudge of 0.01 to w would move L by about 0.02.

The curly ∂ marks a **partial derivative**, a slope you measure with everything else held still. Here,
`x` and `y` stay put as you nudge `w`.

### Each station's own number

The trick behind backpropagation is that no station needs the whole formula. Each station answers one
small question about itself. If my input moves up by a tiny amount, how many times that amount does my
output move? The answer is that station's **local derivative**, and it is "local" because the station
looks only at itself.

Take the multiply station first, and keep x at 0.8 while you nudge w up by 0.01. Then z rises by 0.008,
or 0.8 times the size of your nudge. So the multiply station's local derivative toward w is 0.8, the
value of x.

The same reasoning works the other way: nudge x up by 0.01, and z rises by 0.006. That makes the local
derivative toward x equal to 0.6, the value of w. Each input's local derivative is the *other* input's
value from the forward pass. Remember this detail, because it matters later.

The sigmoid station's local derivative has a short formula, `ŷ(1 − ŷ)`. The formula appears on the tape
once the lab works backward to the sigmoid. With a guess of 0.618, the other part is 1 − 0.618, or
0.382. So the formula gives `0.618 × 0.382`, or 0.236. A nudge of 0.01 to z then moves the guess by only
about 0.0024.

This number is largest when the guess sits exactly in the middle, where `0.5 × 0.5` gives 0.25. It
shrinks toward zero as the guess gets close to 0 or 1. At a guess of 0.99, it is only about 0.01. A
sigmoid stuck near one end like this is called **saturated**, and it passes almost nothing backward.

Picture a sponge that is already soaked: pour a little more water on it, and it holds almost none. A
saturated sigmoid is like that, because a nudge to z barely changes its output. The sponge covers only
one end, though. A sigmoid can also get stuck near 0, and it barely responds there either.

The loss station's local derivative is `2(ŷ − y)`, twice the gap between the guess and the target. At
the start that is `2 × (0.618 − 0.90)`, and the lab prints it as −0.565. The minus sign carries real
meaning. The guess sits below the target, so pushing it up would make the loss go *down*.

### The chain rule: multiply along the path

Now you can link the three answers together. Picture three gears in a row: when the first gear turns
once, the second turns 3 times. Each turn of the second gear makes the third turn 2 times. So one turn
of the first gear makes the third turn 3 × 2, or 6 times. Linking gears means multiplying their
numbers.

The stations link the same way. A nudge to w moves z by 0.8 times as much. A nudge to z moves the guess
by 0.236 times as much. A nudge to the guess moves the loss by −0.565 times as much. So a nudge that
starts at w gets multiplied by each number in turn:

```
∂L/∂w  =  2(ŷ − y)  ×  ŷ(1 − ŷ)  ×  x
       =  −0.565    ×  0.236     ×  0.800
       =  −0.1066
```

Follow one nudge down the line: w goes up by 0.01, so z rises by 0.008. The guess then rises by about
0.0019, and the loss falls by about 0.001. That fits the answer, since −0.1066 times 0.01 is about
−0.001. The slope is negative, so the weight should go up.

Multiplying local derivatives along a path like this is called the **chain rule**, the engine of
backpropagation.

The gear picture breaks down in one way. Real gears keep the same numbers forever, but a station's
number can change as its inputs change. The sigmoid's 0.236, for example, belongs to a guess of 0.618,
and a different guess gives a different number. That is why the chain rule talks about tiny nudges.

### Going backward

Backpropagation does this multiplication in a particular order, starting at the loss and working back
toward the dial.

The chain begins with 1 at the loss, and the lab shows this as `∂L/∂L 1`. That makes sense, since a
nudge to the loss changes the loss by exactly that much. Each station takes the number arriving from
the loss side and multiplies it by its own local derivative. Then it passes the result to the station
before it.

The number arriving at a station from the loss side is called the **upstream gradient**. Picture the
backward pass as a stream flowing from the loss toward the dial, with the loss end upstream. Unlike
water, though, the number changes at every station it passes. The lab shows this right-to-left sweep,
called the **backward pass**, as a running total under each circle.

Each row below shows the number that has arrived at one circle. The factor in each row comes from the
station to its right. The sigmoid circle receives 1 times the loss station's −0.565. The multiply
circle receives that result times the sigmoid's 0.236:

```
loss L        ∂L/∂L  =  1
sigmoid ŷ     ∂L/∂ŷ  =  1 × (−0.565)       =  −0.565
multiply z    ∂L/∂z  =  −0.565 × 0.236     =  −0.1333
weight w      ∂L/∂w  =  −0.1333 × 0.800    =  −0.1066
```

Go back to the assembly line: the forward pass carries a number down the line to make a guess. The
backward pass carries a report about the error back up the line. Each station scales the report by its
own number, then passes it on. No station ever uses the whole formula, yet the dial at the far end
receives its exact slope.

Working out how much each dial added to the final error is called **credit assignment**.
Backpropagation answers it with an exact local measure. It finds how much the loss would change for a
tiny nudge to each dial, right now. Every number in the chain comes from the stations' own formulas, so
nothing is guessed.

That measure is about sensitivity, not blame, and sensitivity means how strongly the loss reacts to a
nudge. In the lab, a dial can push the guess far off target and still get a tiny slope. The slope only
says how much a small nudge would help from where things stand.

### Why the forward numbers must be kept

Look again at the multiply station. Its local derivative toward w is the value of x, and toward x it is
the value of w. Both values came from the forward pass, so they have to stay stored. They wait there
until the backward pass comes back to that station.

It is a bit like a long sum in a maths test. A later line reuses a number from an earlier line. Picture
erasing each line as soon as you finish it. You would have to redo the earlier work to get that number
back. A memory-saving trick near the end of this lesson makes exactly that trade on purpose.

Size is where the test picture falls short. Your test has a handful of lines, but a big model has a huge pile of forward numbers.

People often call these stored forward values **activations**, so this storage is called **activation
memory**.

### The branch toward the input

The multiply station also sends a slope back toward x, and that slope is `−0.1333 × 0.600 = −0.0800`.
Here x is the training example and training never changes it, so nothing uses that number.

In a deeper model, the thing feeding a layer is the output of the layer before it. That output depends
on the earlier layer's own dials, so this branch is how the backward pass reaches them.

The target gets no slope at all, because it is data and not a dial. So during the backward pass, the
lab labels it `no gradient: data`.

### Many layers: the product gets long

Recall that a real network stacks many layers. Each **layer** is a row of small stations that sit side
by side, and these stations are called **units**. In the lab, each unit takes in 16 numbers from the
layer before it. It multiplies each number by its own weight, adds them all up, and squashes the total.

The **Activation** control picks the squashing step that makes each unit's output, called its
activation. There are three choices:

- **sigmoid** is the squashing step you have already met, and its local derivative is never more than
  0.25.
- **tanh** squashes numbers from −1 to 1, and its local derivative can reach 1 in
  the middle.
- **ReLU** keeps positive numbers as they are and turns negative numbers into 0. So its local
  derivative is either exactly 1 or exactly 0. With random starting weights, about half the units get a negative total. ReLU passes nothing back through those units.

On the way back, every layer multiplies the arriving slope by two things. One is its squashing step's
local derivative, and the other is its own weights. With twelve layers, that makes a long product, and
long products can behave in extreme ways.

Picture a made-up game where you keep only a quarter of your points every round. You start with a
million points. After one round you have 250,000, and after two rounds you have 62,500. After eleven
rounds, you are left with about a quarter of one point.

Now picture the opposite: a chain message goes around, and every person forwards it to three new
friends. In the eleventh round alone, 177,147 new people receive it.

The same thing happens to slopes. A slope passing back through many layers gets multiplied once at
every layer. When most of the numbers in the chain are below 1, the product shrinks toward zero. The
earliest layers then get almost no slope and barely learn, and this is called the **vanishing
gradient**. When most of the numbers are above 1, the product blows up and gives the earliest layers
huge slopes. That is called the **exploding gradient**.

The game and the chain message use the same number every round. In a network, each layer can multiply
by a different number. What counts is whether most of them sit below 1 or above 1.

### Reading tiny and huge numbers

The **Gradient flow through depth** card prints very tiny and very huge numbers, so it uses a short
form. The number 2.6e-8 means 2.6 with the decimal point moved 8 places to the left, or 0.000000026.
In the same way, 3.5e5 means 3.5 with the point moved 5 places to the right, or 350,000.

The readouts under the bars use this form even for ordinary sizes, so 0.21 appears as 2.13e-1. The
number 1.3 appears as 1.32e+0 and 350,000 appears as 3.48e+5. In these forms, the + only means the
point moves to the right.

Each layer in the card has 16 × 16 = 256 weights, so it has 256 slopes, one for each weight. The card
sums them up as a single size called the **norm**. Here is the recipe: square every slope, add the
squares together, and take the square root.

Picture a tiny layer with only two slopes, 3 and 4. Their squares are 9 and 16, and those add up to 25.
The square root of 25 is 5, so the norm is 5. A slope of −3 would give the same square, 9, so the norm
ignores the sign.

A bigger norm means that layer's dials would get a bigger push in a training step. A norm near zero
means they would barely move.

### What the card measures

Take sigmoid units with Glorot starting weights, a setting explained below, and stack twelve layers. On
the way back, each layer multiplies the slope by about 0.24 on average. That 0.24 comes from two things
multiplied together. The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25. The layer's weights also have an effect, and
with this setting it stays close to 1.

Twelve layers have eleven steps between them, from the last layer back to Layer 1. Multiply 0.24 by
itself eleven times, and you get roughly one ten-millionth.

The card shows exactly this, since **Layer 12 norm** reads about 0.21 and **Layer 1 norm** reads 2.6e-8.
**Layer 1 ÷ last** reads 1.2e-7, or 0.00000012. So the first layer gets roughly one ten-millionth of
the slope that the last layer gets. The readouts print one extra digit, such as 2.58e-8, but the story
is the same.

Three controls change this product, and we look at them one at a time.

### The first control: Activation

Switching **Activation** to tanh raises the ceiling on each layer's local derivative from 0.25 to 1. At
twelve layers with Glorot weights, Layer 1 then reads about 0.21. That is almost the same as the last
layer's 0.23, so the slope barely shrinks on the way back.

### The second control: Initial weight scale

The second control is **Initial weight scale**. Every weight starts out as a random number before
training. How spread out those random numbers are is a choice called **weight initialization**.

The control offers four settings: `small (std 0.1)`, `Glorot`, `He` and `large (std 1.0)`. Here "std"
is short for standard deviation, a measure of how far from zero the random numbers typically land. With
0.1, a typical starting weight sits around 0.1 above or below zero.

Think of darts thrown at a bullseye, where the bullseye is zero. Tight throws land close to zero, and
wild throws land far from it. The std measures how wild the throws are. The darts picture is not exact,
though. A dart can miss in any direction across the board, but a weight lands only above or below zero.

Glorot and He are two recipes for that spread. Each is named after the first author of the paper that
proposed it. For this lab's layers of 16 units, Glorot works out to about 0.25 and He to about 0.35.

With ReLU units at twelve layers, the starting spread decides how big every layer's slope is. Here is
what **Layer 1 norm** reads:

```
small (std 0.1)     2.4e-7
Glorot              2.0e-2     which is 0.02
He                  1.3
large (std 1.0)     3.5e5      which is 350,000
```

Small starting weights shrink the signal at every layer, and large ones make it grow at every layer.
Here, the signal means the numbers going forward and the slopes coming back. Over twelve layers, that
one choice decides whether every slope is a whisper or a roar.

Why does Glorot leave Layer 1's slope at only about 0.02 here? Glorot keeps the weights' own effect
close to 1, but ReLU switches off about half the units. So each layer still weakens the signal a
little. The He recipe uses a wider spread, about 0.35 instead of 0.25, to make up for the switched-off
units. That is why He was designed for ReLU, and it keeps each layer's effect close to 1.

You might expect large weights to give Layer 1 a far bigger slope than the last layer. Yet Layer 1's
3.5e5 is only about a third of the last layer's 1.05e6.

The reason goes back to the multiply station. There, the slope on w was the slope arriving at z times
x, the number fed in. Every weight in the stack works the same way, so each weight's slope has two
parts. One is the slope arriving from the loss side. The other is the number fed in from the input
side.

With ReLU and large weights, the arriving slopes grow on the way back toward the input. Meanwhile, the
numbers fed into each layer grow on the way forward toward the output. Those two effects roughly
cancel, so every layer's slope ends up about the same size. They are all huge, or all tiny when the
weights are small.

Think of each weight's slope as the area of a rectangle, and area is length times width. The length is
the arriving slope, and the width is the number fed in. With large weights, the length is big near the
input and the width is small. Near the output, the length is small and the width is big, so the areas
come out roughly alike. The match is rough, though, and Layer 1 still ends up at about a third of the
last layer.

Sigmoid has no such balance. Its outputs always sit between 0 and 1, so the numbers fed into each layer stay a similar size. Meanwhile, the slope shrinks on the way back, and nothing makes up for it.

### The third control: Skip connections

The third control is **Skip connections**. A **skip connection** is a shortcut that adds a layer's input
straight onto its output. People also call it a residual connection, and the control turns it on or
off. With a shortcut, the numbers can hop over the layer as well as pass through it.

Picture a layer that receives 0.5 and whose own calculation gives 0.25. With a shortcut, the layer
hands on 0.5 + 0.25, or 0.75.

On the way back, the shortcut becomes an express lane. The slope arriving at a layer passes straight
back to the layer before. That happens on top of whatever comes through the layer's own weights and
squashing step.

The express lane picture misses one thing. On the way forward, the shortcut adds its numbers on top, so
the values pile up, layer after layer.

With sigmoid, Glorot and twelve layers, turning **Skip connections** on lifts Layer 1's norm from 2.6e-8
to 0.24. The fix has a price, and it shows in **Loss at the start**. That is the untrained stack's error
score on its practice examples, and it climbs from 0.90 to 7.30.

That score uses a different rule from the small graph, called **cross-entropy**. Each of the 32 made-up
examples has a yes-or-no answer, and the stack ends with a sigmoid. So its guess is the chance that the
answer is yes.

Guessing a 50 percent chance every time scores about 0.69. The score has no upper limit, because a guess
that is confident and wrong costs a great deal.

Picture a weather app that gives a 99 percent chance of sun, and then a storm rolls in. That app
deserves a much worse score than one that said 60 percent. Cross-entropy scores guesses the same way.
The app story covers one guess, but the card's score is the average over all 32 examples.

Every shortcut adds more numbers on top, so values grow larger at each layer on the way forward. This
lab has no step that shrinks them back down. By the last layer, the stack's guesses are pushed to
near-certain answers, and about half are confidently wrong.

## Why it’s here

### Every dial needs its own slope

Picture a made-up group project with four people that comes back with a low mark. The group needs to
know how much the mark would change if each person changed their part a little. With four people, you
could talk it through. Now imagine asking the same question about a billion people at once, after every
single practice test.

That is the credit assignment problem that training faces with every round of examples. The most direct
fix is to nudge one dial, rerun the model, and measure the loss. The lab's **Numeric check** does
exactly that for its single dial. In a big model, though, it would mean a separate run for every dial.

Backpropagation avoids this by working backward from the one loss at the end. Each number it works out
on the way back is shared by all the dials behind it. So it finds each shared part only once.

In the small graph, both w and x use the multiply station's slope, −0.1333. In a big model, the slope
arriving at a layer gets reused by every dial in that layer. Every layer before it reuses that slope
too.

It is like one message in a group chat. Everyone reads the same message, and nobody needs a separate
copy. Unlike a chat, though, each dial does one more multiplication of its own after reading the shared
number.

That is why one backward sweep gives every dial its slope at once. You could carry nudges forward from
one dial toward the loss instead. But that answers the question for only one dial per run.

The backward method that programs use is called **reverse-mode autodiff**. That is short for
reverse-mode automatic differentiation, and differentiation means finding derivatives.

### The weak point: long products

The multiplying that makes backpropagation cheap is also its weak point, since slopes get multiplied
along the chain. A long line of layers that each pass back only a fraction leaves almost nothing for
the earliest layers.

That vanishing-gradient problem shaped how modern models are built. The shortcuts you will meet in The
transformer block lab are largely a fix for it. Careful starting weights help too. So does a kind of
layer that rescales the numbers passing through it, called normalization.

The Gradient flow card lets you measure the first two of these fixes, but it has no normalization layer.

## How to play with it

### Walk the small graph one edge at a time

The **Interactive backpropagation computation graph** card has six circles. They are `input x`,
`weight w`, `multiply z`, `sigmoid ŷ`, `target y` and `loss L`. A circle shows `?` until the lab works
out its value.

Below the graph sits the **Backpropagation trace**, six steps you can move through with the **Advance
one edge** button. You can also click any arrow on the graph.

The trace opens on step 1, `Multiply`, with z already filled in. So your first press of **Advance one
edge** moves on to `Activate`.

1. `Multiply` multiplies the input by the weight and fills in z, giving 0.480 at the start.
2. `Activate` sends z through the sigmoid and fills in the guess ŷ, giving 0.618.
3. `Measure loss` compares the guess with the target and fills in the loss L, giving 0.0797.
4. `Loss → output` starts the backward pass by multiplying in the loss station's factor, −0.565.
5. `Output → product` multiplies in the sigmoid's factor, 0.236, and the running total becomes
   −0.1333.
6. `Product → weight` multiplies in x, or 0.800, and gives the weight its slope of −0.1066.

During the first three steps, the heading reads "Forward pass" with `values →`. From the fourth step
on, it reads "Backward pass" with `← gradients`.

Each backward step adds a pink `×` tag under an arrow, showing the factor that arrow multiplies in. A
pink pill under each circle shows its running total, and the next row of the tape fills in.

Check the tape as you go. Each pill should equal the pill to its right times the factor on the arrow
between them. At the last step, the tape reads `−0.1333 × 0.800 = −0.1066`. A small line under that
row gives the slope toward x as well.

### Break the slope on purpose

Before you touch anything, make a prediction. If the loss gets ten times bigger, should the slope on
the weight get bigger too?

**Try this:** Set **Input x** to 1.5, **Weight w** to 3, and **Target y** to 0.1. Then walk the trace
again. Now z is 1.5 × 3, or 4.5, so the sigmoid pins its guess near 1, at 0.989.

That guess is a long way from the target of 0.1. The loss jumps to 0.790, about ten times its starting
value.

Yet the slope on the weight is only 0.029. Ignoring the sign, that is less than a third of the 0.1066
you started with. It is also positive now, since the guess sits above the target and the weight should
go down.

To see where the slope went, look at the three factors. The loss station's factor is large, about
1.778, because the miss is big. The multiply station's factor, x, is 1.5, but the sigmoid's factor has
collapsed to 0.011. That means the sigmoid passes on only about one percent of any nudge. A saturated
sigmoid barely responds, and that one tiny factor shrinks the whole product:

```
∂L/∂w  =  1.778  ×  0.011  ×  1.500  =  0.029
```

That is the vanishing gradient in miniature, with one layer instead of twelve.

You can also move **Target y** on its own and watch the **Chain rule** row update straight away. First
put **Input x** back to 0.8 and **Weight w** back to 0.6. Setting the target to 0 then flips the slope
to about +0.233, so the weight should now go down. Setting the target to 0.6, close to the guess,
shrinks the slope to about 0.0067. A guess that is nearly right needs only a small correction.

### Spend the slope

A slope becomes useful only when you act on it. That is the job of the last two controls. The
**Learning rate** slider, written η and read "eta", sets how big a step to take.

The button **Apply w ← w − η∂L/∂w** takes one step. It sets the new weight to the old weight minus the
learning rate times the slope.

Picture walking downhill in thick fog. The slope tells you which way is down, and the learning rate
sets the length of your stride. Real walking differs in one way: a walker's stride stays about the same
length. Here, the step is the learning rate times the slope, so a gentler slope gives a smaller step.

Put **Target y** back to 0.9, so all three sliders are at their starting values again. Then, with a
learning rate of 0.70, one press works out like this:

```
w  ←  0.600 − 0.70 × (−0.1066)
   =  0.600 + 0.0746
   =  0.675
```

Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding, so subtracting a negative slope moves the weight
up. That is exactly what the slope said to do.

The loss falls from 0.0797 to 0.0720. On the **Loss versus weight tangent chart**, the ball slides a
little way downhill. Moving the **Learning rate** slider on its own changes nothing until you press the
button.

The chart draws the loss for every weight from −3 to 3, with a ball at the current weight. A dashed
straight line, called a tangent, touches the curve at the ball. Its steepness is the slope that the
backward pass worked out.

A green dashed line marks the weight where the guess would equal the target. There the loss would be zero.
At the start, its label reads `ŷ = y at w 2.75`.

### Check the slope by measuring

Under the chart, compare **Analytic slope** with **Numeric check**. The first comes from the chain-rule
formula, and the second comes from nudging the weight and measuring. So the lab finds the two in
completely different ways.

Picture a student who solves an equation with algebra. Then the student checks the answer a second way,
by plugging numbers back in. The **Numeric check** works in a similar spirit. It nudges the weight a
tiny bit up and a tiny bit down, and measures the loss both times. Then it divides the change in loss
by the distance between the two weights it tried.

The two checks are not quite alike, since plugging numbers back in checks an answer exactly. The nudge
has a real size, so the numeric check gives an estimate.

You can try a rougher version yourself, with a much bigger nudge. Drag **Weight w** to 0.62 and read
the **Loss** readout, about 0.07755. Then drag **Weight w** to 0.58, where the **Loss** readout shows
about 0.08182. The loss changes by about −0.00427, and dividing by 0.04, the distance between the two
weights, gives the slope:

```
loss at w = 0.62        0.0775517
loss at w = 0.58        0.0818174
change in loss         −0.0042657
divided by 0.04        −0.10664
```

Even this rough measurement lands on the chain rule's −0.1066. The lab uses a far tinier nudge of
0.00001. So its two readouts agree in every printed digit, wherever you drag **Weight w**.

### Stack the layers

Now open **Gradient flow through depth**, and it builds a stack of layers, each a row of 16 units. It
runs the stack once on 32 made-up practice examples, before any training. Then it shows how much slope
reaches each layer.

The bars run from **Layer 1 · in**, nearest the input, down to the last layer, marked `· out`. A full
bar is split into 20 equal stretches. The empty end stands for 1e-13, and the full end stands for 1e7.
Each extra stretch stands for ten times more slope.

Think of a price tag that gains a zero: $10, then $100, then $1,000. Each stretch of bar is like one
more zero. Unlike prices, though, most of this scale covers numbers below 1: 13 of the 20 stretches.

Each factor of ten is called an **order of magnitude**. So a bar three stretches shorter shows a slope
three orders of magnitude smaller. That is 10 × 10 × 10, or a thousand times smaller. A bar half as long
is therefore not half the slope, so trust the printed number beside it.

Under the bars, **Layer 1 norm**, the last layer's norm and **Layer 1 ÷ last** give the exact values.
**Loss at the start** gives the untrained stack's average cross-entropy score on its 32 examples. The
card opens at six layers, where Layer 1 reads 1.4e-4 and Layer 6 reads 2.3e-2.

Run these comparisons in order, and before each change, predict which way **Layer 1 norm** will move:

1. Set **Activation** to `sigmoid`, **Initial weight scale** to `Glorot`, and **Skip connections** to
   `off`. Then lower **Network depth** to 1 and raise it back up to 12, one step at a time.
2. Watch Layer 1's norm start at about 0.15 with one layer. It falls at every single step, down to
   2.6e-8 at twelve. The last layer's own number wobbles up and down, and at twelve layers it still
   reads about 0.21.
3. Switch **Activation** to `tanh`, and notice that Layer 1 now reads about the same as the last layer.
4. Switch to `ReLU` and keep twelve layers. Step **Initial weight scale** from `small (std 0.1)`
   through `Glorot` and `He` to `large (std 1.0)`. Every bar moves together, from tiny to enormous.
5. Switch back to `sigmoid` with `Glorot`, and turn **Skip connections** on. Then compare
   **Layer 1 ÷ last** and **Loss at the start** with your twelve-layer readings from step 2.

For more detail, open **Table of the same numbers**, and you will find two extra columns. **Mean slope
f′(z)** is the average local derivative of each layer's squashing step. Here f′(z), read "f-prime of
z", is the lab's short way to write a squashing step's local derivative.

**Output RMS**, short for root mean square, is a typical size for a layer's output numbers. To find it,
square the numbers, take the average, then take the square root. The only change from the norm is the
average in the middle. With the numbers 3 and 4, the squares are 9 and 16. Their average is 12.5, and
the square root of 12.5 is about 3.5. That is a typical size for 3 and 4.

## What to notice

- No station uses the whole formula. Each one multiplies what arrives from its right by its own local
  derivative. The running product is what carries the information back to the dial.
- The multiply station reports the other input's value. So x scales the slope on w, and w scales the
  slope toward x. That is why forward values have to stay in memory until the backward pass reaches
  them.
- The sigmoid's local derivative peaks at 0.25 when the guess is 0.5 and falls toward zero at both ends.
  A sigmoid can never pass back more than a quarter of the slope it receives.
- A large loss does not guarantee a large slope. One flat, saturated station in the middle of the chain
  can block almost all of it.
- The branch toward x gets a real slope, even though it has no dial to turn. In a deeper model, that
  same branch is how slope reaches the earlier layers.
- The target is data, not a dial, so no slope flows toward it. The graph marks it `no gradient: data`.
- **Analytic slope** and **Numeric check** agree in every printed digit. Comparing a formula's slope
  with a measured one like this is called **gradient checking**. In real code, it catches mistakes in
  derivatives that someone wrote by hand.
- The **Chain rule** row reports that match as **Numeric agreement**, a relative error. A relative error
  is the gap between the two slopes, as a share of their size. Say two slopes were 2.0 and 2.1: their
  gap of 0.1 is about 5 percent of their size. At the start, the readout shows about 9.2e-10 percent,
  less than a billionth of a percent.
- Vanishing is about the gap between the first and last layers. The starting weights set the size of
  every layer at once. With ReLU at twelve layers, Layer 1 stays within a factor of ten of the last
  layer. That holds at every starting setting.
- At twelve layers, sigmoid vanishes at every starting setting. Even its best setting leaves Layer 1
  with less than one part in 250 of the last layer's slope.
- The table's **Mean slope f′(z)** column shows why sigmoid struggles. With sigmoid, it stays below 0.25
  at every layer. So each step back starts by cutting the slope to under a quarter.
- At twelve layers, tanh with `large (std 1.0)` shows the opposite problem. Layer 1's slope ends up more
  than ten times bigger than the last layer's. So the slope grows as it travels back toward the input.
- A shortcut fixes the gap, not the growth. With sigmoid, Glorot, twelve layers and skips on, the
  **Output RMS** column climbs from about 0.55 at Layer 1. It reaches more than 6 at Layer 12. That
  swelling pushes the final guesses to near-certain answers, and about half of them are wrong. So
  **Loss at the start** rises from 0.90 to 7.30.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

The lab calculates the small graph honestly, but the graph is tiny. It has one dial, one example and
one squashing step. It also scores the guess with a squared gap, not cross-entropy, the kind of loss language models actually use. The **Gradient flow through depth** card uses cross-entropy too. No
station feeds two others, so the lab never has to combine slopes arriving from different paths.

The chart works only because the model has one dial. A real model has millions of dials, so nobody can
draw its loss curve. The tangent here stands for one slope out of millions that the same backward pass
would produce at once.

The **Gradient flow through depth** card is one random draw of the starting weights and the practice
examples. Think of a random draw as one shuffle of a deck of cards. A new shuffle deals new cards. The card uses a stack 16 units wide on 32 made-up points, measured before any training step. It
has no normalization layers, and it reports one norm for each layer's weights.

A different random draw changes the card's numbers but not the orderings this lesson describes. That is
where the shuffle picture breaks down: in a card game, a new shuffle can change who wins. Here, a new
draw leaves the orderings in place. The course's own checks re-run the card with seven different random
draws to make sure.

That card shows how big the slopes start, not whether the network would actually learn. Learning also
depends on the method that applies the slopes, the learning rate and how the error is scored. The
slopes also keep changing as training goes on.

### Where the real method runs into trouble

The assembly-line picture and the group-project story both suggest fault and intent, but there is none.
The backward pass is arithmetic about how one number responds to a nudge in another. A big slope does not mean a dial is "wrong", only that the loss is sensitive to it now.

The assembly line also stops matching in another way, since real graphs branch and this lab never shows
that. A station whose output feeds several others collects slopes from each of them. It **adds** them
together before multiplying by its own local derivative.

Picture one path sending back 0.3 and another sending back −0.1. The station's total is 0.3 + (−0.1), or
0.2. One weight can also be reused in many places. Its slope is then the sum of the pieces from every
place it appears.

The backward pass also has a hidden cost. The forward values must stay in activation memory until the
backward pass reaches them. That storage grows with the number of layers, the examples in each round
and the length of the text. For long documents and deep models, that storage often limits training more
than the arithmetic does.

Skip connections on their own make numbers grow, as the card shows. Real deep networks, such as the
transformers in later labs, pair shortcuts with normalization layers. This lab leaves those out, so the
card shows what shortcuts do alone, growth included.

**A common mix-up:** Backpropagation is not how brains learn. It needs a single error signal sent
backward through the exact same connections used going forward. Real brains do not appear to work that
way. Backpropagation is an efficient method for finding the slopes of a calculation, and nothing more.

## Going deeper

### Programs do the bookkeeping

People build models with software toolkits called frameworks, and frameworks do all of this
automatically. Every operation quietly records what it needs to report its own local derivative. So the
program builds a tape as the forward pass runs, much like the tape in this lab.

Asking for slopes then replays that tape in reverse, and that is reverse-mode autodiff in action.
Nobody has to write derivatives by hand, so trying out a new model design is cheap.

### Saving memory

The memory problem has a standard trick called gradient checkpointing. Instead of storing every forward
value, the program saves them only at a few chosen points. It recomputes the rest during the backward
pass.

That is the maths-test trade from earlier. You keep a few key lines and redo the steps between them when
you need them. Checkpointing costs roughly 30 percent more computing, but it saves a large amount of
memory.

### Slopes too small to store

Computers store numbers using bits, and a bit is a single digit that is either 0 or 1. A 16-bit number
uses 16 of them, and the usual format uses 32 bits. To save memory, training sometimes stores numbers in
a 16-bit format called fp16 instead. With fp16, a very small slope can round down to exactly zero and
be lost.

Picture a kitchen scale that reads 0 for anything under 1 gram. A pinch of salt would read 0, even
though it weighs something.

The fix is to multiply the loss by a large number before the backward pass. That scales every slope up
by the same amount, and afterward the program divides that number back out. On the kitchen scale, that
is like weighing a thousand pinches and then dividing by a thousand. Training differs on one point: it
gathers no extra pinches, and one multiplication makes every slope bigger.

A different 16-bit format, called bf16, covers the same range of sizes as the 32-bit format. So it
usually needs no scaling.

### Taming the size of slopes

Glorot and He are short formulas from research papers. The Glorot paper came out in 2010 and the He
paper in 2015.

Glorot divides 2 by the number of inputs plus the number of outputs, and then takes the square root.
This lab's layers have 16 inputs and 16 outputs, so Glorot works out 2 ÷ 32, or 0.0625. The square root
of 0.0625 is exactly 0.25.

The He formula divides 2 by the number of inputs alone, then takes the square root too. Here that is the
square root of 2 ÷ 16, or about 0.35. The He formula was designed for units like ReLU.

Sometimes a slope grows too large anyway. A trick called gradient clipping then shrinks it back whenever
its norm passes a set limit.

Normalization layers rescale the numbers inside the model as they pass through. They help keep both the
forward values and the slopes in a workable range.

### Clearing slopes between steps

In most frameworks, new slopes get added to the old ones by default instead of replacing them. So
programs clear the slopes to zero before each new step.

It is like a scoreboard that keeps adding points until someone resets it. Unlike a scoreboard, though,
this adding-up is sometimes useful on purpose. It lets a small computer gather slopes from several
rounds of examples. Then the computer takes one step, as if it had trained on one bigger round.

### Where backpropagation stops

Some steps cannot pass a slope backward at all. Picking one word from a list of possible next words is
a hard choice. So there is no smooth answer to the question "what if it moved slightly?"

Compare a dimmer with a light switch. Turn a dimmer slightly, and the light changes slightly. Push a
switch slightly, and either nothing happens or the light flips all the way. A word is a bigger choice
than a switch, though. A switch has two settings, but picking a word means choosing one from many.

Training through a step like that needs different ways to estimate slopes. That is part of why the
reinforcement stage in the Post-training lab does not look like ordinary training.

The Reinforcement learning foundations lab runs the simplest of these methods on a four-armed bandit.
That is a toy game where you keep choosing one of four slot-machine arms and see whether it pays.
