## The big picture

When you send a message to a chatbot, the program behind it is called a language model. It first
turns each word into a list of numbers. Those numbers then climb through a tall stack of steps. At the top, the model scores every piece of text that could come next.

The surprising part is that almost the whole stack is one design, copied over and over. That
repeated design is called the **transformer block**. In this module you take one block apart and
watch every piece of it work.

### Where you have met it

The language models behind today's chatbots are built from this block, stacked dozens of times. A
word lookup sits at the bottom of the stack, and a next-word scorer sits at the top. The lab names
two real models whose sizes you can check for yourself. GPT-2 small stacks 12 blocks, while Llama 2
7B stacks 32 of them.

The 7B in that name means the model holds about 7 billion adjustable numbers. You will meet those in a moment. Bigger models stack even more blocks, since Llama 2 70B uses 80 and Llama 3.1 405B uses 126.

Inside every block, the same few steps run in the same order. So one block, even the tiny one in this lab, teaches you a lot about the whole model.

### Why it matters

A block has several jobs to do at once. Each job would fail in a clear way without its part.

- First, words have to **share information**. Take "the animal did not cross the street because it was tired". The word `it` stands for the animal, so it needs information from several words back. The
  sharing step is called **attention**, and without it every word would be processed alone.
- Second, each word's information has to be **reworked**. Whatever a word has gathered has to be combined. It then has to become something more useful for the next block.
- Third, the stack has to **survive being repeated** dozens of times. In this design, each step adds
  a correction to a word's numbers instead of replacing them. Without that rule, information from the
  bottom of a deep stack would get scrambled on the way up.
- Fourth, **word order** has to count. Picture a block where every word may read every other word, with no position information added. It cannot tell "the cat sat on the mat" from "the mat sat on the cat". Yet those sentences mean very different things.

The third job also protects **training**, the long practice stage where a model guesses next words
over and over. After each guess, the model's adjustable numbers get a small nudge so the next guess
improves. Without that rule, training would stall.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these three ideas in your own words:

- how information travels through one block, from the moment a word's numbers enter
  to the moment they leave;
- what the **residual stream** is, a running list that every step adds to and no step erases. That one rule lets models stack so many blocks;
- why attention, the only step where words share information, cannot tell word order by itself. You will also see what position information adds.

### What the lab lets you do

The lab runs a real transformer block right in the page. You can switch its parts on and off while
you watch the numbers change. It has four cards, and each card shows a different side of the block.

- **Transformer block** shows one word's numbers at every stage. It has an on and off switch for each working part.
- **Residual stream** shows how each part's changes pile up for that word. Its **Input scale** slider makes the incoming numbers bigger or smaller.
- **Parameter count** counts the adjustable numbers inside a block, and training tunes those numbers. It adds them up for two real models and matches their published totals exactly.
- **Position** reorders the words of a sentence and checks whether the block's outputs change.

Nothing in the lab has been trained, so its adjustable numbers are still random. The lab shows you
the plumbing of a block, not anything a block has learned. That turns out to be enough to see why
each pipe is there.

## What it is

### Words as lists of numbers

A computer cannot do arithmetic on the letters c, a and t. So a language model swaps each word for
a list of numbers that describes it. A list like this is called a **vector**. The swap uses a **word lookup table**. It stores one starting list of numbers for every word the model knows.
Each piece of text the model handles is called a **token**. In this lab, every token is a whole word.

Real models use hundreds or thousands of numbers for each token. GPT-2 small uses 768, and Llama 2
7B uses 4,096. The lab uses only 8, so you can see every one of them. It labels those eight places
d0 to d7. The number of places is the model's width, written d, so in this lab d is 8.

The lab's sentence is "the cat sat on the mat". It makes six tokens in slots numbered 0 to 5.
The **Token** buttons at the top of the **Transformer block** card pick the word to follow. Both that card and the **Residual stream** card describe this word. The lab starts on `mat` in slot 5.

The card draws each vector as eight small bars. Positive numbers rise above a middle line, and
negative numbers drop below it. For `mat`, the first bar dips a little below the line and the second
rises well above it. The bars carry no number labels, but the lab computes them as −0.45 and 1.70.

Beside each row, the card also prints one number called the **norm**. It measures the overall size
of the whole list. You find it the way you find the long side of a right-angled triangle. Square each number, add up the squares, and take the square root.

Try a short list with the numbers 3 and 4. The squares are 9 and 16, which add up to 25, so the
norm is 5. Doing the same with `mat`'s eight starting numbers gives a norm of 2.93. The top row of
the card prints it as `norm 2.93`. If you picture a list as an arrow, its norm is the arrow's length.

Keep the two meanings of "norm" apart as you read on. This **norm** measures size, while the **Layer
norm** step you are about to meet changes the numbers.

### A conveyor belt that only adds

Picture a factory conveyor belt that carries one tray for each word. All six trays ride side by side. Each tray holds a card with that word's 8 numbers. Specialists stand at stations along the belt. None of them ever takes a card away or rubs out a number. Instead, a specialist photocopies the card and works on the copy. It writes down a change for each of the 8 numbers.

A change might say "add 0.58" or "take away 0.66". A change can be negative, so adding it can lower
a number as well as raise it. The changes are added onto the card, and the tray rolls on to the
next station. It carries everything it already had, plus the new changes.

The belt is the model's **residual stream**. It is a running list of numbers for each word, and it climbs the full height of the model. Each block is one stretch of the belt with two specialists along it. Each specialist is called a **sublayer**.

- **Self-attention** is the specialist that can look into other trays. It copies information from the cards of other words. It is the only step in the block where one word's numbers can affect
  another word's.
- The **MLP**, short for multilayer perceptron, works on one tray at a time. The name is an old one
  for a small network built in layers. The MLP cannot see that other words exist, and it reworks the
  numbers already on its card.

One path carries a card around a specialist, straight to the point where the change is added. This path is called a **skip connection**. It is the reason the belt never breaks, even when a specialist is
switched off.

Each specialist also has its own set of numbers, like the dials on a machine. These dials decide
exactly how the specialist turns a copy into a change. They are not the word's numbers. They belong to the specialist, and it uses the same ones for every word. Training tunes them, so they are
called **adjustable numbers**, or **parameters**.

### One trip through the block

The **Transformer block** card lists the stages from top to bottom, in the order the numbers meet
them. The six stages below follow `mat` along that path. Rows on the left rail are the belt itself,
while indented rows work on a copy. A Layer norm row tidies the copy. The specialist row below it writes its change back at the next plus sign.

#### Stage 1: tidy a copy (`Layer norm`)

Numbers on a card can drift very large or very small on their way up a model. A specialist works
best on input of a steady size. So before each specialist reads its copy, a tidying step resizes the
copy in two moves.

First, it subtracts the average of the eight numbers from each of them, so the new numbers average
zero. Second, it divides each number by how far the numbers typically sit from their average. That
spread is called the standard deviation. The result is eight numbers with a typical size of 1.

This two-move step is called **layer normalization**, and the lab's button shortens it to `Layer
norm`.

Here is a small example you can check by hand. Take the list 2, 2, 6 and 6, whose average is 4.
Subtracting 4 gives −2, −2, 2 and 2. Every number now sits exactly 2 away from the average, so the
spread is 2. Dividing by 2 gives −1, −1, 1 and 1.

Now make the list 8 times bigger, so it reads 16, 16, 48 and 48. The average is now 32 and the
spread is 16. The tidied copy comes out as −1, −1, 1 and 1 once again. That is the key property. However big or small the numbers on the card are, the tidied copy comes out the same size.

In the conveyor-belt picture, the tidying step is a photocopier with an automatic zoom. It always prints the copy at one standard size. For `mat`, the Layer norm row reads `(x − 0.05) ÷ 1.03`. That
means subtract an average of 0.05, then divide by a spread of 1.03. The tidied copy's norm is 2.83,
which is the square root of 8. Eight numbers with a typical size of 1 have squares that add up to
about 8.

Only the copy is tidied, so the card on the belt keeps its own size. Tidying a copy before each
specialist reads it is called **pre-norm**, and GPT-2 and Llama both use it.

#### Stage 2: share between words (`Self-attention`)

This is the step you met in the Attention lab. Here each word again makes three lists of numbers
from its tidied copy. A **query** describes what the word is looking for. A **key** advertises what
the word can offer, and a **value** holds the information it hands over.

The word compares its query with the key of every word it may read. It turns those match scores into
shares that add up to 100 percent. Then it takes a blend of the values, where each word's value
counts as much as its share.

In this block, a word may read itself and the words before it, but never the words after it. This
one-way rule is called the **causal mask**. The rule exists because the model's job is to guess the next word. Reading later words would be peeking at the answer.

The block runs two of these searches side by side. Each search is called a head, and each head has
its own adjustable numbers. In a trained model, that lets each head learn to look for a different
kind of match. One head might find the action, and another might find the thing being acted on.

The Self-attention row shows both heads' shares for the chosen word. For `mat`, head 1 gives 29
percent to `sat` and only 3 percent to `cat`. Head 2 also leans toward `sat`, giving it 31 percent.

The two blends are then combined into one list of 8 numbers. This list is the change that the
specialist writes back, and it is called the **attention write**. For `mat`, its size measured as a
norm is 1.57. The **Residual stream** card shows it as **Attention write**.

#### Stage 3: add (the first `Residual add`)

The skip connection carries the original card around the specialist. The plus sign then adds the attention write to that card. For `mat`, d1 starts at 1.70, and the attention write for d1 is
−0.66. After the addition, d1 reads 1.04. The `Residual add` rows have no switch, because removing
the addition would remove the belt itself.

#### Stage 4: tidy again (the second `Layer norm`)

The updated card is copied and tidied in exactly the same way as in stage 1. That way the next
specialist also reads numbers of a standard size.

#### Stage 5: rework each word alone (`MLP`)

The MLP is a small network built from the kind of artificial neurons you met in earlier labs. It
first widens the 8 numbers into 32. Then it passes each of the 32 through a bending rule called
**GELU**. Finally it squeezes the result back down to 8 numbers.

Each of the 32 new numbers is a mix of the 8 old ones. To make one, multiply each old number by its own adjustable number. Then add up the eight results. Squeezing back down to 8 works the same
way, with each final number a mix of the 32.

GELU passes large positive numbers through almost unchanged. It squashes negative numbers to nearly
zero, with a smooth bend in between. That bend matters, because two multiply steps in a row can
always be replaced by one. Multiplying by 3 and then by 2 is the same as multiplying by 6.

Without the bend, the widening and the squeezing would do no more than one plain mixing step. The extra room would go to waste.

For `mat`, the row reads `8 → 32 (GELU, 16 positive) → 8`. This means 16 of the 32 in-between
numbers came out positive.

The MLP widens the list by 4 times here, and that amount is called the **feed-forward expansion**.
The MLP uses the same adjustable numbers for every word, and it handles one word at a time. It never
looks at another slot. A step that works like this, separately on each slot with shared adjustable
numbers, is called **position-wise**.

Layer norm is position-wise too, because it averages across one word's 8 numbers and never across
different words. That leaves attention as the only step in the whole block that crosses between
slots.

#### Stage 6: add again (the second `Residual add`)

The MLP's change is called the **MLP write**, and it is added to the belt. The result is the block's
output for this word. For `mat`'s d1, the MLP write is 0.57. The lab does not print values for a single place like d1. Here is the arithmetic it does for the whole trip.

```text
d1 for mat:    1.70   input
             − 0.66   attention write
             + 0.57   MLP write
             = 1.61   block output
```

In a pre-norm block, all 8 numbers follow the same pattern. The output equals the input plus the
attention write plus the MLP write. Nothing was replaced along the way. The **Residual stream** card
draws exactly this sum for all eight places.

### Pre-norm and post-norm

The order above tidies a copy before each specialist, and that is pre-norm. The original Transformer
from 2017 used a different placement, called **post-norm**. Post-norm tidies the belt itself right
after each addition. In the photocopier picture, it resizes the card on the belt instead of the
copy. The specialists then read the belt directly, with no tidied copy in between.

In this one-block lab, that means attention reads the raw input card, because nothing has tidied it
yet. The **Placement** control switches between `Pre-norm` and `Post-norm`. In post-norm, the Layer
norm rows move onto the rail.

Post-norm also puts a tidying step across the belt itself. So the route from the top of a stack back
to the bottom is no longer only plus signs. That is part of why deep post-norm stacks are harder to
train, as the next section explains.

### Where order comes from

Look again at how attention works, and you will find a gap. Its match scores depend only on the
numbers being compared. Nothing on a card says which slot its tray rides in. So if every word may
read every other word, swapping two trays changes nothing about the work. Each card receives exactly the same changes as before. The outputs come out the same, only in the swapped slots.

Walk one word through both sentences to see why. Assume every word may read every other word, and
there is no position information yet. Take `sat`. In "the cat sat on the mat", it compares its query with all six cards. That includes cat in slot 1 and mat in slot 5.

In "the mat sat on the cat", `sat` meets the very same six cards. Only cat and mat have swapped
trays. The match scores are the same, so the shares and the blend are the same. Then `sat` gets
exactly the same change.

Attention cannot tell a word next door from a word five trays away. No card has the distance written on it.

The same goes for cat itself. Its card gets the same change in slot 5 as it did in slot 1. So the
six outputs are identical to before, and only their slots have swapped.

This property has a long name, **permutation equivariance**, and it makes sense once you split it in
two. A permutation is a reordering. Equivariance means the outputs change in the same way as the inputs. So reordering the words going in reorders the outputs coming out. Layer norm and the MLP
never look at slots either, so the whole block shares this property.

The fix is a **positional encoding**, which is information about each word's slot. The simplest kind
is the one this lab uses. It is a list of numbers that depends only on the slot. The model adds it to each word's vector before the first block. Other kinds work inside attention instead, as Going deeper describes.

In the conveyor-belt picture, it is like stamping each card with its seat number. The stamp is a set
of small numbers added onto the card's own 8 numbers.

This lab uses a wave pattern, called sinusoidal, whose numbers swing between −0.5 and 0.5. Slot 0's pattern is 0, 0.5, 0, 0.5 and so on. Slot 1's pattern begins 0.42 and then 0.27. The first
numbers of the pattern change quickly from slot to slot, and the later ones change slowly. Together
they give every slot its own mix.

It works like the hands of a clock. The second hand moves fast and the hour hand moves slowly. Together they give every moment of the day its own reading. In the same way, the fast-changing first numbers and the slow-changing later ones give every slot its own pattern. A clock is only a loose match, because its hands turn in smooth circles. The lab's pattern is a list of numbers.

The label on the **Input x** row says the same thing. It describes the input as the word's
embedding, meaning its starting numbers, plus the position. So `the` in slot 0 and `the` in slot 4
enter the block as two different vectors. Their first numbers are −1.48 and −1.86. They are the same
word, but the block no longer receives them as the same input.

The one-way rule gives away a little order on its own. The first word sees only itself, and the last word sees all six. To keep its test about order alone, the **Position** card switches that
rule off. Then every word reads all six words.

### What is actually running

The lab computes a real transformer block in your browser, only a very small one. Each word is a
list of 8 numbers, and attention has two heads that each work with 4 numbers. The MLP widens to 32
numbers and back. Altogether, the block has 840 adjustable numbers, which the **Parameter count**
card lists as **This lab's toy block**.

Those adjustable numbers are random rather than trained. They come from a random number generator
that always starts from the same value, called a seed. That is why you see the same numbers every
time you open the lab. The arithmetic is honest, but the numbers carry no meaning. So the lab shows
how information flows through a block, not what a trained block does with it.

## Why it’s here

Earlier labs gave you separate pieces. Attention showed how words share information, and the neuron
labs showed how a small network reworks numbers. A useful model needs both jobs done many times
over, one layer after another. The transformer block wires the two together into a single unit that
you can repeat. The hard part is making that repetition work.

### Deep stacks lose their signal

Picture a game of telephone with 32 players, where each player whispers the message to the next
one. Every whisper loses a little, so by the last player the message has turned into nonsense. Now
change one rule. The original note travels down the line too, and each player may only stick a short
correction onto it. The last player still holds the original note, plus every correction made along
the way.

A deep stack built without the belt would be like the first game. Every layer would have to pass on
a whole new version of every word. With the belt, each specialist only adds a correction. So the original information survives all the way to the top. Each specialist can also focus on one kind of correction. None of them has to rebuild the whole card.

The game stops matching in one way: real specialists add corrections to numbers, not words on a note.

The same path also helps in the other direction, during training. A correction signal travels
backward from the model's answer down through every layer. The signal tells each adjustable number
which way to move. The plus signs give it a direct route all the way down to the lowest layers.

Here is why the plus sign matters. A block's output is its input plus the changes. So if the input
rises by 1, the output rises by 1 as well, plus whatever the changes do. A message such as "move this
number up" therefore passes back through each plus sign at full strength.

The route through a specialist is different, because a specialist can shrink the message as it
passes. Picture a stack where each specialist halves it. After 10 layers, less than a thousandth is
left, and after 32 layers almost nothing. The plus-sign route still carries the message whole, and
that direct route is what made very deep stacks trainable.

Together with the tidying step, this path is what lets real models repeat their block dozens of
times. Information does not fade on the way up, and the training signal does not die on the way
down.

There is one more benefit, and the interpretability labs build on it. Those labs are about explaining
what goes on inside a model. The stream is a sum of many separate changes. So you could, in principle, split the numbers at the top into the pieces that each specialist added.

### Numbers that drift out of range

Without the tidying step, the size of the numbers drifts as they pass through layer after layer.
Attention is especially sensitive to this, because its match scores come from multiplying numbers
from two words together. You will see the result in the lab. Make the input large with Layer norm off. Then each head gives 100 percent of its share to a single word.

Where the tidying happens matters as much as whether it happens. Pre-norm is the standard choice
today because deep stacks train steadily with it. Post-norm usually needs extra care before a deep
stack will train. That care includes a slow warm-up with small training steps at the start, and
carefully chosen starting numbers.

### Order carries meaning

Order changes more than which sentence sounds sensible. Picture a model reading "the dog chased the
cat up a tree, and then the". It has to guess what comes next. To guess well, it has to know which animal did the chasing. It also has to know which one is now stuck in the tree. Only the order of the words says so.

Swap the two animals and the same words describe a different story, with a different likely next
word. Attention alone cannot see order, so a position signal has to be added. Without one, a block where every word reads every other word returns the same numbers for both stories. They are only shuffled between the slots.

### Reading a model's spec sheet

Knowing the block's shape also helps you read the published descriptions of real models. These descriptions are called model cards. Four facts almost fix how many adjustable numbers a model has. They are the width, the number of layers, the type of MLP and the size of the vocabulary. The **Parameter count** card shows how, and it does the counting for you.

## How to play with it

### Switch off the only step that crosses words

**Try this:** In the **Transformer block** card, click the `Self-attention` button so that it reads
`off`. Then read **Output shift if other words change** in the **Residual stream** card. Watch it
drop from 0.900 to exactly 0.000.

That readout comes from a small experiment the lab runs for you. It runs the block a second time
with the other five words replaced. So "the cat sat on the mat" becomes "a dog ran to a mat". Only
`mat` stays the same. The lab then measures how far the output of `mat` moved between the two runs.

With attention on, the output moves by 0.900, because `mat` reads the other words. With attention
off, nothing in the block can see another word, so the output does not move at all. The belt still
reaches the output, though. **Output norm** stays well above zero, because the card's own numbers
pass straight through.

Now switch `Self-attention` back on and pick the `the` button at slot 0. Its shift is 0.000 even with attention on. Under the one-way rule, the first word has no earlier words to read. Its
Self-attention row shows both heads giving 100 percent to the word itself.

### Make the input huge

Click the `mat` button again, turn `Layer norm` off, and drag **Input scale** from 1× up to 8×.
Watch the Self-attention row while you drag. Both heads now give 100 percent of their share to `sat`. So `mat` reads one word and ignores the rest.

Match scores come from multiplying numbers from two words together. When every number is 8 times
bigger, each score becomes 64 times bigger. Gaps between scores that were small turn huge, and the
top score takes everything.

The changes the specialists write grow about five times bigger. You can read this in the **Attention
write** as it climbs from 1.83 to 9.18. The **MLP write** climbs in the same way, from 2.25 to 11.19.
So a block without tidying lets every size run away.

Now turn `Layer norm` back on. The attention write drops to 1.57 and stays exactly there wherever you
drag the slider. Multiplying every number on every card by the same amount leaves the tidied copies unchanged. The 2, 2, 6, 6 example showed this. So attention reads exactly the same input.

The MLP's copy comes from the card after the attention write is added. That write tilts a small card more than a large one. So the MLP write drifts a little as you drag, staying between about 1.9 and
2.2.

Then look at **cos(output, input)**, where cos is short for cosine. Picture each list as an arrow
again. The cosine scores how closely the two arrows point the same way. It runs from 1 for exactly
the same direction down to −1 for opposite directions.

With Layer norm on and the input at 8×, it reads 0.995. So the output is almost the same as the input. At 0.25× it falls to 0.178, so the output is mostly the specialists' changes.

The changes are about the same size in both cases. What differs is the size of the stream they land
on. Two spoonfuls of sugar change a cup of tea a lot. In a swimming pool, you would never taste them.

### Watch the additions line up

First set **Input scale** back to 1×. In the **Residual stream** card, each of the eight rows, d0 to
d7, has three lanes. The top lane runs from zero to the input value. The middle lane adds the
attention write, starting where the top lane ended. The bottom lane adds the MLP write in the same way. A diamond marks the block's output for that place.

In pre-norm, every diamond sits exactly at the end of the bottom lane. Find the d1 row for `mat`.
The middle lane points left, because the attention write there is negative. The bottom lane points back to the right.

Now switch the `MLP` off in the **Transformer block** card, and the bottom lanes vanish. Each diamond
lands at the end of the middle lane instead. The output is now the input plus the attention write. Switch the MLP back on and compare the two writes. For `mat`, they read 1.57 for **Attention
write** and 2.00 for **MLP write**.

### Move the tidying onto the belt

In the **Transformer block** card, switch **Placement** to `Post-norm`. The Layer norm rows jump onto
the rail, because they now resize the belt itself. In the **Residual stream** card, the diamonds no longer sit at the end of the lanes. That is because post-norm resizes the output after every addition.

Watch **Output norm** while you drag **Input scale** from 0.25× to 8×. With Layer norm on, it reads
2.83 the whole time, which is the square root of 8. Every card is resized to the same standard size.
Post-norm erases the stream's own size at every addition, while pre-norm keeps it.

Now look at the Self-attention row with the slider at 8×. Both heads again give 100 percent to `sat`,
even with Layer norm on, and the **Attention write** reads 9.18. In post-norm, attention reads the
raw input card, which nothing has tidied yet. So its huge numbers reach the match scores unchanged.

### Count where the parameters live

As you saw earlier, the adjustable numbers in a model are called **parameters**. The main ones are also called weights. In the **Parameter count** card, click `GPT-2 small`. The bar splits one
block's parameters into attention, the MLP (hatched) and a thin sliver for the norms. **MLP share**
reads 66.6%, so two out of every three adjustable numbers in a block belong to the MLP.

The note under the card adds up all the blocks, the word lookup tables and the final norm. It arrives
at 124,439,808, which is exactly the published count for GPT-2 small. Click `Llama 2 7B` instead, and
the MLP share reads 66.8%. The total is 6,738,415,616, or about 6.7 billion.

Next, drag **Model width** from 768 to 1,536, which doubles d. Watch the **Block** row grow about four
times bigger. It goes from about 7.1 million to about 28.3 million parameters. A block's size grows
with d squared, and 2 squared is 4. Going deeper shows where the square comes from.

Then switch **Norm** between `LayerNorm` and `RMSNorm`, a cheaper tidying step described in Going
deeper. Notice that the total barely moves.

### Test word order

The **Position** card runs the block with the settings from the **Transformer block** and **Residual
stream** cards. So start by resetting them. Set **Placement** back to `Pre-norm`. Make sure `Layer
norm`, `Self-attention` and `MLP` are all on, and set **Input scale** to 1×.

Open the **Position** card, then set **Word order** to `Swap cat ↔ mat` and **Position information**
to `Off`. The two rows of word chips show two sentences. They read "the cat sat on the mat" and "the mat sat on the cat".
**Words whose output changed** reads 0 of 6.

Every **Output shift** in the table is 0.000, and every **Result** says "same output". The block
hands back the same six outputs, only in each other's slots. So it cannot tell the two sentences
apart.

Switch **Position information** to `On`, and all six words change output. The word cat moves by 1.565
and mat moves by 1.286. Those two move the most because their own slots changed. The four words that
stayed put move less, by about 0.4 each. Their own cards did not change, but the cat and mat cards
they read now carry different seat-number stamps.

Try `Shuffle all` next, which turns the sentence into "mat on cat the the sat". With position
information off, the result is again 0 of 6. With it on, all six change and **Largest output shift**
reads 1.391.

Finally, go back to the swap with position information on, and turn `Self-attention` off in the
**Transformer block** card. Now only cat and mat change output. Without attention, a word's output
depends only on its own input.

## What to notice

- Each specialist **adds a nudge rather than rewriting the card**. So in the **Residual stream** card,
  the pre-norm diamond lands exactly at the end of the three lanes.
- **Switching a specialist off does not cut the block.** The skip connection still carries the stream to the output. That is why blocks built around a residual stream get gradually worse when you remove a part. They do not break outright.
- **Attention is the only step where words affect each other.** With `Self-attention` off, the output shift is 0.000 for every word. With it on, the first word still reads 0.000, because it has
  no earlier words to read.
- **In pre-norm, Layer norm hides the stream's overall size from the specialists.** With it on, the attention write stays at 1.57 at every **Input scale**. That keeps their work in a sensible range
  however large the stream has grown.
- **The same change matters less on a bigger stream.** As you raise **Input scale**, the cosine
  between output and input climbs toward 1.
- **Pre-norm keeps the stream's own size, while post-norm erases it.** That is why the post-norm
  output norm reads 2.83 at every input scale.
- **Most of a block's parameters live in the MLP.** The **Parameter count** card shows about two-thirds for both presets.
- **In the Position card, only position information supplies order**, because the card lifts the
  one-way rule. With position information off, the card shows zero change at every setting of the
  other switches. Tidying, the MLP and the input size cannot supply order, because none of them looks
  at slots.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- **Nothing here has been trained**, because the adjustable numbers are random values from a fixed
  seed. The arithmetic is honest, but no vector means anything. So the lab shows the plumbing rather
  than learned skill.
- **The block is tiny**, with 8 numbers for each word where real models use hundreds or thousands.
  It also runs one block where real models run dozens.
- **One switch stands in for two**, since the single `Layer norm` button turns both tidying steps off
  together. A real block has two separate ones. Real tidying steps also end by multiplying by a
  learned scale and adding a learned shift. Here the scale is fixed at 1 and the shift at 0, so those
  last moves change nothing.
- **Some parts are missing**, since attention here has no **biases**. Those are extra adjustable
  numbers that some steps add on at the end of their arithmetic. There is also no dropout, a training
  trick that randomly blanks out some numbers.
- **Switching a part off is exact here, in one sense.** Nothing in this block learned to expect that part's changes. So no step gets an input unlike anything it saw in training. That stops being true in a trained model, as the mix-up below explains.
- **Later steps still react to a part being switched off**, though. Switch `Self-attention` off and
  the MLP reads a different card. So for `mat`, the **MLP write** changes from 2.00 to 1.89. The output
  then moves by more than the attention write alone.
- **The Position card lifts the one-way rule.** With that rule in place, the first word sees only itself and the last sees all six. So even a block with no position numbers
  leaks a little order. The last word still cannot tell in which order the five words before it came.
- **The position pattern is fixed.** It is a wave pattern of size 0.5, added to the input of an
  untrained block. The card shows that the block's outputs can depend on position. It does not show
  that the block makes any use of order.
- **The parameter counts assume every head has its own keys and values**, which Smaller attention
  below explains. Outside the two presets, the card counts only the main weights. It leaves out biases and word lookup tables.

### Where the conveyor belt stops matching

The belt picture makes a real model look tidier than it is, in three ways.

First, the picture suggests that each specialist's changes stay neat and separate on the card. In a
real model, every specialist at every level writes into the same fixed list of numbers. That list is only as long as the model's width. With hundreds of specialists, that list runs out of room. Their changes end up
crowded on top of each other. The interpretability labs return to this problem.

Second, the tidying step is not neutral bookkeeping. It has its own adjustable numbers, a scale and a
shift, which training tunes. The size it hides from each copy still matters too. The same write changes a word with large numbers less, as the sugar example showed. Trained models
make use of effects like these.

Third, the specialists are not workers who choose what to write. Each one does fixed arithmetic with
its learned numbers, and its change is whatever that arithmetic produces.

### What goes wrong in real models

**A common mix-up:** Switching a specialist off in a real, trained model is not a clean experiment.
Picture a school band that has rehearsed for months with a drummer. On concert night the drummer is
sick. The band sounds noticeably worse, but not only because the drum part is missing. The other
players were counting on the beat to keep time, so they stumble too.

Judging the drummer's job by how bad the band sounds would overstate it. A trained model works the
same way, because every later layer learned to expect that specialist's changes. Removing it hands those layers inputs they never saw in training. Nothing retrains to make up for the gap.

The damage you measure therefore mixes the specialist's own job with the shock to everything after
it. Removing a part like this is called **ablation**. Getting an honest answer from it takes the more
careful methods in the circuits lab.

Position has real-world limits as well, because adding position information is necessary but not enough. A trained model still has to learn to use it. How well a model copes with texts longer than its training texts also depends on its position scheme.

Finally, for most models the parameter count tracks two things. One is the memory a model needs, and the other is the work it does for each word. Some designs separate the two. They hold many MLPs per block but run only a few for each word. Going deeper explains how.

## Going deeper

### Counting a block from its shape

You can work out a model's size from its shape. Most of its adjustable numbers sit in grids that turn one list into another. Each new number is a mix of all d old ones. Each mix needs d
adjustable numbers of its own, one for each old number. So a grid that turns d numbers into d new numbers needs d × d entries. We write that as d².

Attention needs four of these grids. Three make queries, keys and values, and one combines the
heads' results. That is the **Attention** row's `4 · d²`. With d = 768, one grid holds 589,824
numbers, so the four of them together hold 2,359,296.

The MLP needs two bigger grids, one that widens the list to 4d and one that squeezes it back. For
GPT-2 small the widened list has 3,072 numbers, which is 4 times 768. So the **MLP** row reads `2 ·
d · 3,072`, which comes to 4,718,592. That is twice the attention count.

The MLP's two grids hold 8 · d² between them. Adding attention's 4 · d² gives about 12 · d² for a
whole block. That is why the **Block** row compares the total with this rule of thumb. It is also why two-thirds of every block is MLP.

The two tidying steps add only a scale and a shift for each of the d places. At d = 768 they make up
about 0.04% of a block. So choosing between LayerNorm and RMSNorm affects speed and steadiness rather
than size. The count grows with d², so doubling the width makes each block four times bigger.

Load **GPT-2 small**, and the lab adds its 12 blocks to a word lookup table for 50,257 tokens. It
also counts a table of positions, the biases and a final norm. It reproduces the published
124,439,808 exactly.

### Newer MLPs

Newer designs mostly change the MLP rather than attention. One such design is **SwiGLU**, and it
splits the widening step into two branches that are multiplied together. One branch acts a little
like a dimmer switch on the other. SwiGLU uses three grids instead of two, so it narrows the middle
to about 8/3 of the width. That keeps its total close to the classic MLP's.

It is the `SwiGLU ≈8/3×` choice in the **MLP** control. **Llama 2 7B** uses it with a middle
width of 11,008. With it, the preset still adds up to Llama 2 7B's published 6,738,415,616.

A **mixture of experts** goes further. It keeps many MLPs in each block but sends each word to only a few of them. A model can then have an enormous total size. Each word still pays only a small bill in work.

### Smaller attention

**Grouped-query attention**, covered in **Modern attention at scale**, lets several heads share the
same keys and values. That shrinks two of attention's four grids. So real attention often costs less
than `4 · d²`.

### A cheaper tidying step

**RMSNorm** skips subtracting the average and has no learned shift. It only divides by the typical
size of the numbers and then applies a learned scale. It is cheaper to compute and works about as
well in practice. It is the standard in recent models such as Llama.

### Other ways to supply order

Real models handle order in several ways. Some add a learned list of position numbers for each slot.
Others add a fixed wave pattern, as this lab does. Still others lower the match score between two
words the farther apart they sit, a scheme called ALiBi.

Many current models, Llama among them, add nothing at the input at all. Instead, they twist each
query and key by an angle that depends on its slot. This scheme is called rotary embeddings.
**Modern attention at scale** shows it at work, and the **Attention** lab lines the schemes up.

A model that only reads earlier words can pick up a little order from that rule alone. Even so, most
models still supply position information on purpose, either at the input or inside attention.

### Peeking partway up

At the very top of the stack sit one more tidying step and a word-scoring layer. They turn each word's list of numbers into odds for the next token. You can also apply that scoring layer to the residual stream
partway up the stack. That gives a rough peek at which next token the model was leaning toward at
that depth. Researchers call this tool the logit lens.

### Specialists that read and write in the same space

Every specialist only adds to a shared stream. So you can picture each attention part and each MLP neuron as reading certain mixes of its numbers. Each part also writes its own
mix back. Two parts at different depths work together when what one writes overlaps with what the
other reads. The circuits lab is built on that way of looking at a model.
