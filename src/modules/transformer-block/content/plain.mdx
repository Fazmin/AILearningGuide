## The big picture

When you send a message to a chatbot, the program behind it, called a language model, first turns
each word into a list of numbers. Those numbers then climb through a tall stack of steps, and at the
top the model scores every piece of text that could come next. The surprising part is that almost
the whole stack is one design, copied over and over again. That repeated design is called the
**transformer block**, and in this module you take one apart and watch every piece of it work.

### Where you have met it

The language models behind today’s chatbots are built from this block, stacked dozens of times, with
a word lookup at the bottom and a next-word scorer at the top. The lab names two real models whose
sizes you can check for yourself: GPT-2 small stacks 12 blocks, while Llama 2 7B stacks 32 of them.
The 7B in that name means the model holds about 7 billion adjustable numbers, which you will meet in
a moment. Bigger models stack even more, since Llama 2 70B uses 80 blocks and Llama 3.1 405B uses
126.

Inside every one of those blocks, the same few steps run in the same order. That is why
understanding one block, even the tiny one in this lab, takes you a long way toward understanding
the whole model.

### Why it matters

A block has several jobs to do at once, and each job would fail in a clear way without its part:

- First, words have to **share information**. In “the animal did not cross the street because it was
  tired”, the word `it` stands for the animal, so that word needs information from several words
  back. Without a sharing step, called **attention**, every word would be processed on its own and
  could never pick anything up from its neighbours.
- Second, each word’s information has to be **reworked**, because whatever a word has gathered still
  has to be combined and turned into something more useful for the next block.
- Third, the stack has to **survive being repeated** dozens of times. In this design, each step adds
  a correction to a word’s numbers instead of replacing them. Without that rule, information from
  the bottom of a deep stack would get scrambled on the way up. **Training** would stall too. That
  is the long practice stage where a model guesses next words over and over. After each guess, its
  adjustable numbers are nudged a little so that the next guesses improve.
- Fourth, **word order** has to count. Picture a block in which every word may read every other
  word. Without extra position information, it cannot tell “the cat sat on the mat” from “the mat
  sat on the cat”, even though they mean very different things.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these three ideas in your own words:

- how information travels through one block, step by step, from the moment a word’s numbers enter
  to the moment they leave;
- what the **residual stream** is, a running list of numbers that every step adds to and no step
  erases, and why that one rule lets models stack so many blocks;
- why attention, the only step where words share information, cannot tell word order by itself, and
  what adding position information changes.

### What the lab lets you do

off while you watch the numbers change. It is organised into four cards, and each one shows a
different side of the block:

- **Transformer block** shows one word’s numbers at every stage of the block, with an on and off

- **Residual stream** shows how each part’s changes pile up for that word, and its **Input scale**

## What it is

### Words as lists of numbers

A computer cannot do arithmetic on the letters c, a and t, so a language model starts by swapping
each word for numbers. It uses a list of numbers that describes the word, and a list like this is
called a **vector**. The swap uses a **word lookup table**, which stores one starting list of
numbers for every word the model knows. Each piece of text the model handles is called a **token**,
and in this lab every token is a whole word.

Real models use hundreds or thousands of numbers for each token: 768 in GPT-2 small and 4,096 in
Llama 2 7B. The lab uses just 8, so that you can see every one of them. The lab labels those
eight places d0 to d7, and the number of places is the model’s width, written d, so in this lab d
is 8.

The lab’s sentence is “the cat sat on the mat”, six tokens sitting in slots numbered 0 to 5. The
**Token** buttons at the top of the **Transformer block** card choose which word that card and the
**Residual stream** card describe. The lab starts on `mat` in slot 5.

The card draws each vector as
eight small bars, with positive numbers rising above a middle line and negative numbers dropping
below it. For `mat`, the first bar dips a little below the line and the second rises well above it.
The bars carry no number labels, but the lab computes them as −0.45 and 1.70.

Beside each row, the card also prints a single number called the **norm**, which measures the
overall size of the whole list. You find it the way you find the long side of a right-angled
triangle: square each number, add up the squares, then take the square root. For a short list such
as 3 and 4, that gives 9 plus 16, which is 25, so the norm is 5.

Doing the same with `mat`’s eight
starting numbers gives a norm of 2.93, which the top row of the card prints as `norm 2.93`. If you
picture a list as an arrow, its norm is the arrow’s length.

Keep the two meanings of “norm” apart as you read on, because this **norm** measures size, while
the **Layer norm** step you are about to meet changes the numbers.

### A conveyor belt that only adds

Picture a factory conveyor belt that carries one tray for each word, with all six trays riding side
by side. In each tray sits a card with that word’s 8 numbers, and specialists stand at stations
along the belt. A specialist never takes a card away and never rubs out a number. Instead, it
photocopies the card, works on the copy, and writes down a change for each of the 8 numbers.

A change might say “add 0.58” or “take away 0.66”, and since a change can be negative, adding it can
lower a number as well as raise it. The changes are added onto the card, and the tray rolls on to
the next station carrying everything it already had plus the new changes.

The belt is the model’s **residual stream**: one running list of numbers for each word, travelling
the full height of the model. Each block is one stretch of the belt with two specialists along it,
and each specialist is called a **sublayer**:

- **Self-attention** is the specialist that can look into other trays, copying information from the
  cards of other words. It is the only step in the block where one word’s numbers can affect another
  word’s.
- The **MLP**, short for multilayer perceptron, an old name for a small network built in layers,
  works on one tray at a time. It has no way of seeing that other words exist, and it reworks the
  numbers already on that card.

The path that carries a card around a specialist, straight to the point where the change is added,
is called a **skip connection**. It is the reason the belt never breaks, even when a specialist is
switched off.

Each specialist also has its own set of numbers, like the dials on a machine, which decide
exactly how it turns a copy into a change. These are not the word’s numbers. They belong to the
specialist, and it uses the same ones for every word. Training tunes them, so they are called
**adjustable numbers**, or **parameters**.

### One trip through the block

The **Transformer block** card lists the stages from top to bottom in the order the numbers meet
them, and the six stages below follow `mat` along that path. Rows on the left rail are the belt
itself, while indented rows work on a copy. A Layer norm row tidies the copy, and the specialist row
below it writes its change back at the next plus sign.

#### Stage 1: tidy a copy (`Layer norm`)

Numbers on a card can drift very large or very small on their way up a model, but a specialist
works best on input of a steady size. So, before each specialist reads its copy, a tidying step
resizes the copy in two moves. First, it subtracts the average of the eight numbers from each of
them, so that the new numbers average zero.

Second, it divides each number by how far the numbers typically sit from their average, a kind of
spread called the standard deviation. That leaves eight numbers whose typical size is 1, and this
two-move step is called **layer normalization**, which the lab’s button shortens to `Layer norm`.

Here is a small example you can check by hand, using the list 2, 2, 6 and 6, whose average is 4.
Subtracting 4 gives −2, −2, 2 and 2, and since every number now sits exactly 2 away from the
average, the spread is 2. Dividing by 2 then gives −1, −1, 1 and 1.

Now make the list 8 times bigger, so that it reads 16, 16, 48 and 48. The average is now 32 and the
spread is 16, so the tidied copy comes out as −1, −1, 1 and 1 once again. That is the key property:
however big or small the numbers on the card are, the tidied copy comes out at the same size.

In the conveyor-belt picture, the tidying step is a photocopier with an automatic zoom that always
prints the copy at one standard size. For `mat`, the Layer norm row reads `(x − 0.05) ÷ 1.03`, which
means subtract an average of 0.05 and then divide by a spread of 1.03. The tidied copy’s norm is
2.83, the square root of 8, because eight numbers with a typical size of 1 have squares that add up
to about 8.

Only the copy is tidied, so the card on the belt keeps its own size. Tidying a copy before each
specialist reads it is called **pre-norm**, and it is the placement that GPT-2 and Llama use.

#### Stage 2: share between words (`Self-attention`)

This is the step you met in the Attention lab, and here each word again makes three lists of numbers
from its tidied copy. A **query** describes what the word is looking for, a **key** advertises what
it can offer, and a **value** holds the information it hands over.

The word compares its query with the key of every word it may read, and it turns those match scores
into shares that add up to 100 percent. It then takes a blend of their values, where each word’s
value counts as much as its share. In this block, a word may read itself and the words before it but
never the words after it, a one-way rule called the **causal mask**. The rule is there because the
model’s job is to guess the next word, and reading later words would be peeking at the answer.

The block runs two of these searches side by side, and each search is called a head and has its own
adjustable numbers. In a trained model, that lets each head learn to look for a different kind of
match, such as one finding the action and another the thing being acted on. The Self-attention row
shows both heads’ shares for the chosen word, and for `mat`, head 1 gives 29 percent to `sat` and
only 3 percent to `cat`. Head 2 also leans toward `sat`, giving it 31 percent.

The two blends are then combined into one list of 8 numbers, which is the change this specialist
writes back, called the **attention write**. For `mat`, its size measured as a norm is 1.57, which
the **Residual stream** card shows as **Attention write**.

#### Stage 3: add (the first `Residual add`)

The skip connection carries the original card around the specialist, and at the plus sign the
attention write is added to it. For `mat`, d1 starts at 1.70 and the attention write for d1 is
−0.66, so after the addition d1 reads 1.04. The `Residual add` rows have no switch, because removing
the addition would remove the belt itself.

#### Stage 4: tidy again (the second `Layer norm`)

The updated card is copied and tidied in exactly the same way as in stage 1, so that the next
specialist also reads numbers of a standard size.

#### Stage 5: rework each word alone (`MLP`)

The MLP is a small network built from the kind of artificial neurons you met in earlier labs. It
first widens the 8 numbers into 32, then passes each of the 32 through a bending rule called
**GELU**, and finally squeezes the result back down to 8 numbers.

Each of the 32 new numbers is a mix of the 8 old ones. To make one, multiply each old number by its
own adjustable number, then add up the eight results. Squeezing back down to 8 works the same way,
with each final number a mix of the 32.

GELU passes large positive numbers through almost unchanged and squashes negative numbers to nearly
zero, with a smooth bend in between. That bend matters, because two multiply steps in a row can
always be replaced by one. Multiplying by 3 and then by 2 is the same as multiplying by 6. Without
the bend in between, the widening and the squeezing would do no more than one plain mixing step, and
the extra room would be wasted.

For `mat`, the row reads `8 → 32 (GELU, 16 positive) → 8`, meaning
16 of the 32 in-between numbers came out positive.

How much the MLP widens the list, 4 times here, is called the **feed-forward expansion**. The MLP
uses the same adjustable numbers for every word, handling one word at a time, and it never looks at
another slot. A step that works like this, separately on each slot with shared adjustable numbers,
is called **position-wise**.

Layer norm is position-wise too, because it averages across one word’s 8 numbers and never across
different words. That leaves attention as the only step in the whole block that crosses between
slots.

#### Stage 6: add again (the second `Residual add`)

The MLP’s change, called the **MLP write**, is added to the belt, and the result is the block’s
output for this word. For `mat`’s d1, the MLP write is 0.57. The lab does not print values for a
single place like d1, but here is the arithmetic it does for the whole trip:

```text
d1 for mat:    1.70   input
             − 0.66   attention write
             + 0.57   MLP write
             = 1.61   block output
```

In a pre-norm block, every one of the 8 numbers follows the same pattern: the output equals the
input plus the attention write plus the MLP write. Nothing was replaced along the way, and the
**Residual stream** card draws exactly this sum for all eight places.

### Pre-norm and post-norm

The order above, which tidies a copy before each specialist, is pre-norm, but the original
Transformer from 2017 used a different placement, called **post-norm**. Post-norm tidies the belt
itself right after each addition, so in the photocopier picture it resizes the card on the belt
instead of the copy. The specialists then read the belt directly, with no tidied copy in between.

In this one-block lab, that means attention reads the raw input card, because nothing has tidied it
yet. The **Placement** control switches between `Pre-norm` and `Post-norm`, and in post-norm the
Layer norm rows move onto the rail.

Post-norm also puts a tidying step across the belt itself, so the route from the top of a stack
back to the bottom is no longer only plus signs. That is part of why deep post-norm stacks are
harder to train, as the next section explains.

### Where order comes from

Look again at how attention works, and you will find a gap. Its match scores depend only on the
numbers being compared, and nothing on a card says which slot its tray rides in. So, when every word
may read every other word, swapping two trays changes nothing about the work. Each card receives
exactly the same changes as before, and the outputs come out the same, only in the swapped slots.

Walk one word through both sentences to see why, with every word allowed to read every other word
and no position information yet. Take `sat`. In “the cat sat on the mat”, it compares its query
with all six cards, including cat in slot 1 and mat in slot 5.

In “the mat sat on the cat”, `sat` meets the very same six cards, only cat and mat have swapped
trays. The match scores are the same, so the shares and the blend are the same, and `sat` gets
exactly the same change. Attention cannot tell a word next door from a word five trays away,
because distance is written on no card.

The same goes for cat itself. Its card gets the same change in slot 5 as it did in slot 1, so the
six outputs are identical to before, and only their slots have swapped.

This property has a long name, **permutation equivariance**, which makes sense once you split it in
two. A permutation is a reordering, and equivariance means the outputs change in the same way as the
inputs, so reordering the words going in reorders the outputs coming out. Layer norm and the MLP
never look at slots either, so the whole block shares this property.

The fix is a **positional encoding**: information about each word’s slot. The simplest kind, used in
this lab, is a list of numbers that depends only on the slot, added to each word’s vector before the
first block. Other kinds work inside attention instead, as Going deeper describes. In the
conveyor-belt picture, it is like stamping each card
with its seat number, where the stamp is a set of small numbers added onto the card’s own 8 numbers.

This lab uses a wave pattern, called sinusoidal, whose numbers swing between −0.5 and 0.5. Slot 0’s
pattern is 0, 0.5, 0, 0.5 and so on, while slot 1’s pattern begins 0.42 and then 0.27. The first
numbers of the pattern change quickly from slot to slot while the later ones change slowly, so every
slot gets its own mix.

It works like the hands of a clock. The second hand moves fast and the hour hand moves slowly, so
together they give every moment of the day its own reading. In the same way, the fast-changing first
numbers and the slow-changing later ones give every slot its own pattern.

The label on the **Input x** row says the same thing, describing the input as the word’s embedding,
meaning its starting numbers, plus the position. So `the` in slot 0 and `the` in slot 4 enter the
block as two different vectors, with first numbers of −1.48 and −1.86. They are the same word, but
the block no longer receives them as the same input.

The one-way rule gives away a little order on its own, because the first word sees only itself and
the last word sees all six. To keep its test about order alone, the **Position** card switches that
rule off, so that every word reads all six words.

### What is actually running

The lab computes a real transformer block in your browser, only a very small one. Each word is a
list of 8 numbers, attention has two heads that each work with 4 numbers, and the MLP widens to 32
numbers and back. Altogether, the block has 840 adjustable numbers, which the **Parameter count**
card lists as **This lab’s toy block**.

Those adjustable numbers are random rather than trained, because they come from a random number
generator that always starts from the same value, called a seed. That is why you see the same
numbers every time you open the lab. The arithmetic is honest, but the numbers carry no meaning, so
the lab shows how information flows through a block, not what a trained block does with it.

## Why it’s here

Earlier labs gave you separate pieces: attention showed how words share information, and the neuron
labs showed how a small network reworks numbers. A useful model needs both jobs done many times
over, one layer after another. The transformer block wires the two together into a single unit that
you can repeat, and the hard part is making that repetition work.

### Deep stacks lose their signal

Picture a game of telephone with 32 players, where each player whispers the message to the next
one. Every whisper loses a little, so by the last player the message has turned into nonsense. Now
change one rule: the original note travels down the line too, and each player may only stick a short
correction onto it. The last player still holds the original note, plus every correction made along
the way.

A deep stack built without the belt would be like the first game, because every layer would have to
pass on a whole new version of every word. With the belt, each specialist only adds a correction, so
the original information survives all the way to the top. It also lets each specialist focus on one
kind of correction, since none of them has to rebuild the whole card.

The same path also helps in the other direction, during training, when a correction signal travels
backward from the model’s answer down through every layer. The signal tells each adjustable number
which way to move, and the plus signs give it a direct route all the way down to the lowest layers.

Here is why the plus sign matters. A block’s output is its input plus the changes, so if the input
rises by 1, the output rises by 1 as well, plus whatever the changes do. A message such as “move
this number up” therefore passes back through each plus sign at full strength.

The route through a specialist is different, because a specialist can shrink the message as it
passes. Picture a stack where each specialist halves it: after 10 layers, less than a thousandth is
left, and after 32 layers almost nothing. The plus-sign route still carries the message whole, and
that direct route is what made very deep stacks trainable.

Together with the tidying step described next, this path is what lets real models repeat their
block dozens of times. Information does not fade on the way up, and the training signal does not
die on the way down.

There is one more benefit, which the interpretability labs, about explaining what goes on inside a
model, build on. Because the stream is a sum of many separate changes, the numbers at the top can,
in principle, be pulled apart into the pieces that each specialist added.

### Numbers that drift out of range

Without the tidying step, the size of the numbers drifts as they pass through layer after layer.
Attention is especially sensitive to this, because its match scores come from multiplying numbers
from two words together. You will see the result in the lab: make the input large with Layer norm
off, and each head gives 100 percent of its share to a single word.

Where the tidying happens matters as much as whether it happens, and pre-norm is the standard choice
today because deep stacks train steadily with it. Post-norm usually needs extra care before a deep
stack will train, such as a slow warm-up with small training steps at the start and carefully chosen
starting numbers.

### Order carries meaning

Order changes more than which sentence sounds sensible. Picture a model reading “the dog chased the
cat up a tree, and then the” and guessing what comes next. To guess well, it has to know which
animal did the chasing and which one is now stuck in the tree, and only the order of the words says
so.

Swap the two animals and the same words describe a different story, with a different likely next
word. Since attention alone cannot see order, a position signal has to be added. Without one, a
block where every word reads every other word hands back the same numbers for both stories, only
shuffled between the slots.

### Reading a model’s spec sheet

Knowing the block’s shape also helps you read the short descriptions that are published for real
models, called model cards. The width, the number of layers, the type of MLP and the size of the
vocabulary almost fix how many adjustable numbers a model has. The **Parameter count** card shows
how, and it does the counting for you.

## How to play with it

### Switch off the only step that crosses words

**Try this:** In the **Transformer block** card, click the `Self-attention` button so that it reads
`off`. Then read **Output shift if other words change** in the **Residual stream** card, and watch
it drop from 0.900 to exactly 0.000.

That readout comes from a small experiment the lab runs for you. It runs the block a second time
with the other five words replaced, so “the cat sat on the mat” becomes “a dog ran to a mat”. Only
`mat` stays the same, and the lab measures how far its output moved between the two runs.

With attention on, the output moves by 0.900, because `mat` reads the other words. With attention
off, nothing else in the block can see another word, so the output does not move at all. The belt
still reaches the output, though, and **Output norm** stays well above zero, because the card’s own
numbers pass straight through.

Now switch `Self-attention` back on and pick the `the` button at slot 0. Its shift is 0.000 even
with attention on, because under the one-way rule the first word has no earlier words to read. Its
Self-attention row shows both heads giving 100 percent to the word itself.

### Make the input huge

Click the `mat` button again, turn `Layer norm` off, and then drag **Input scale** from 1× up to 8×
while you watch the Self-attention row. Both heads now give 100 percent of their share to `sat`, so
`mat` reads one word and ignores the rest.

Match scores come from multiplying numbers from two words together, so when every number is 8 times
bigger, each score becomes 64 times bigger. Gaps between scores that were small turn huge, and the
top score takes everything.

The changes the specialists write grow about five times bigger, which you can read in the
**Attention write** as it climbs from 1.83 to 9.18. The **MLP write** climbs in the same way, from
2.25 to 11.19, so a block without tidying lets every size run away.

Now turn `Layer norm` back on, and the attention write drops to 1.57 and stays exactly there
wherever you drag the slider. Making every number on every card the same amount bigger leaves the
tidied copies unchanged, as the 2, 2, 6, 6 example showed, so attention reads exactly the same
input.

The MLP’s copy is taken after the attention write has been added, and that write tilts a small card
more than a large one. So the MLP write drifts a little as you drag, staying between about 1.9 and
2.2.

Then look at **cos(output, input)**, where cos is short for cosine. Picturing each list as an arrow
again, the cosine scores how closely the two arrows point the same way. It runs from 1 for exactly
the same direction down to −1 for opposite directions. With Layer norm on and the input at 8×, it
reads 0.995, so the output is almost the same as the input. At 0.25× it falls to 0.178, so the
output is mostly the specialists’ changes.

The changes are about the same size in both cases, so what differs is the size of the stream they
land on. Two spoonfuls of sugar change a cup of tea a lot, while in a swimming pool you would never
taste them.

### Watch the additions line up

First set **Input scale** back to 1×. In the **Residual stream** card, each of the eight rows, d0 to
d7, has three lanes. The top lane runs
from zero to the input value, and the middle lane adds the attention write, starting where the top
lane ended. The bottom lane adds the MLP write in the same way, and a diamond marks the block’s
output for that place.

In pre-norm, every diamond sits exactly at the end of the bottom lane. Find the d1 row for `mat`,
where the middle lane points left, because the attention write there is negative, and the bottom
lane points back to the right.

Now switch the `MLP` off in the **Transformer block** card, and the bottom lanes vanish. Each
diamond lands at the end of the middle lane instead, because the output is now the input plus the
attention write. Switch the MLP back on and compare the two writes, which for `mat` read 1.57 for
**Attention write** and 2.00 for **MLP write**.

### Move the tidying onto the belt

In the **Transformer block** card, switch **Placement** to `Post-norm`, and the Layer norm rows jump
onto the rail, because they now resize the belt itself. In the **Residual stream** card, the
diamonds no longer sit at the end of the lanes, since the output is resized after every addition.

Watch **Output norm** while you drag **Input scale** from 0.25× to 8×. With Layer norm on, it reads
2.83 the whole time, the square root of 8, because every card is resized to the same standard size.
Post-norm erases the stream’s own size at every addition, while pre-norm keeps it.

Now look at the Self-attention row with the slider at 8×. Both heads again give 100 percent to
`sat`, even with Layer norm on, and the **Attention write** reads 9.18. In post-norm, attention
reads the raw input card, which nothing has tidied yet, so its huge numbers reach the match scores
unchanged.

### Count where the parameters live

As you saw earlier, the adjustable numbers in a model are called **parameters**, and the main ones
are also called weights. In the **Parameter count** card, click `GPT-2 small`, and the bar splits
one block’s parameters into attention, the MLP (hatched) and a thin sliver for the norms. **MLP
share** reads 66.6%, so two out of every three adjustable numbers in a block belong to the MLP.

The note under the card adds up all the blocks, the word lookup tables and the final norm. It
arrives at 124,439,808, which is exactly the published count for GPT-2 small. Click `Llama 2 7B`
instead, and the MLP share reads 66.8%, with a total of 6,738,415,616, or about 6.7 billion.

Next, drag **Model width** from 768 to 1,536, which doubles d, and watch the **Block** row grow
about four times bigger. It goes from about 7.1 million to about 28.3 million parameters, because a
block’s size grows with d squared, and 2 squared is 4. Going deeper shows where the square comes
from.

Then switch **Norm** between `LayerNorm` and `RMSNorm`, a cheaper tidying step described in Going
deeper, and notice that the total barely moves.

### Test word order

The **Position** card runs the block with the settings from the **Transformer block** and
**Residual stream** cards, so start by resetting them. Set **Placement** back to `Pre-norm`, make
sure `Layer norm`, `Self-attention` and `MLP` are all on, and set **Input scale** to 1×.

Open the **Position** card, then set **Word order** to `Swap cat ↔ mat` and **Position information**
to `Off`. The two rows of word chips show “the cat sat on the mat” and “the mat sat on the cat”, and
**Words whose output changed** reads 0 of 6.

Every **Output shift** in the table is 0.000, and every **Result** says “same output”. The block
hands back the same six outputs, only in each other’s slots, so it cannot tell the two sentences
apart.

Switch **Position information** to `On`, and all six words change output, with cat moving by 1.565
and mat by 1.286. Those two move the most because their own slots changed. The four words that
stayed put move less, by about 0.4 each. Their own cards did not change, but the cat and mat cards
they read now carry different seat-number stamps.

Try `Shuffle all` next, which turns the sentence into “mat on cat the the sat”. With position
information off, the result is again 0 of 6, while with it on all six change and **Largest output
shift** reads 1.391.

Finally, go back to the swap with position information on, and turn `Self-attention` off in the
**Transformer block** card. Now only cat and mat change output, because without attention a word’s
output depends only on its own input.

## What to notice

- Each specialist **adds a nudge rather than rewriting the card**, so in the **Residual stream**
  card the pre-norm diamond lands exactly at the end of the three lanes.
- **Switching a specialist off does not cut the block**, because the skip connection still carries
  the stream to the output. That is why blocks built around a residual stream get gradually worse
  when a part is removed, instead of breaking outright.
- **Attention is the only step where words affect each other**, so with `Self-attention` off the
  output shift is 0.000 for every word. With it on, the first word still reads 0.000, because it has
  no earlier words to read.
- **In pre-norm, Layer norm hides the stream’s overall size from the specialists**, so with it on
  the attention write stays at 1.57 at every **Input scale**. That keeps their work in a sensible
  range however large the stream has grown.
- **The same change matters less on a bigger stream**, so as you raise **Input scale**, the cosine
  between output and input climbs toward 1.
- **Pre-norm keeps the stream’s own size, while post-norm erases it**, which is why the post-norm
  output norm reads 2.83 at every input scale.
- **Most of a block’s parameters live in the MLP**, which the **Parameter count** card shows as
  about two-thirds for both presets.
- **In the Position card, only position information supplies order**, because the card lifts the
  one-way rule. With position information off, the card shows zero change at every setting of the
  other switches. Tidying, the MLP and the input size cannot supply order, because none of them
  looks at slots.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

- **Nothing here has been trained**, because the adjustable numbers are random values from a fixed
  seed. The arithmetic is honest, but no vector means anything, so the lab shows the plumbing rather
  than learned skill.
- **The block is tiny**, with 8 numbers for each word where real models use hundreds or thousands, and it runs
  one block where real models run dozens.
- **One switch stands in for two**, since the single `Layer norm` button turns both tidying steps
  off together, while a real block has two separate ones. Real tidying steps also end by multiplying
  by a learned scale and adding a learned shift. Here the scale is fixed at 1 and the shift at 0, so
  those last moves change nothing.
- **Some parts are missing**, since attention here has no **biases**, the extra adjustable numbers
  some steps add on at the end of their arithmetic. There is also no dropout, a training trick that
  randomly blanks out some numbers.
- **Switching a part off is exact here, in one sense**: nothing later in this block was trained to
  expect that part’s changes. So no step is thrown off by an input unlike anything it saw in
  training, which stops being true in a trained model, as the mix-up below explains.
- **Later steps still react to a part being switched off**, though. Switch `Self-attention` off and
  the MLP reads a different card, so for `mat` the **MLP write** changes from 2.00 to 1.89. The
  output then moves by more than the attention write alone.
- **The Position card lifts the one-way rule**, which matters because with the rule in place, the
  first word sees only itself and the last sees all six. So even a block with no position numbers
  leaks a little order, although the last word still cannot tell in which order the five words
  before it came.
- **The position pattern is fixed**: it is a wave pattern of size 0.5 added to the input of an
  untrained block. The card shows that the block’s outputs can depend on position, not that the
  block makes any use of order.
- **The parameter counts assume every head has its own keys and values**, which Smaller attention
  below explains. Outside the two presets, the card also counts the main weights only, with no
  biases and no word lookup tables.

### Where the conveyor belt stops matching

The belt picture makes a real model look tidier than it is, in three ways. First, the picture
suggests that each specialist’s changes stay neat and separate on the card. In a real model, every
specialist at every level writes into the same fixed list of numbers, as long as the model’s width.

With hundreds of specialists, that list runs out of room, so their changes end up crowded on top of
each other. The interpretability labs return to this problem.

Second, the tidying step is not neutral bookkeeping. It has its own adjustable numbers, a scale and
a shift, which training tunes. And the size it hides from each copy still matters. A word with large
numbers on the belt is changed less by the same write, as the sugar example showed. Trained models
make use of effects like these.

Third, the specialists are not workers who choose what to write, since each one does fixed
arithmetic with its learned numbers, and its change is whatever that arithmetic produces.

### What goes wrong in real models

**A common mix-up:** Switching a specialist off in a real, trained model is not a clean experiment.
Picture a school band that has rehearsed for months with a drummer, and on concert night the drummer
is sick. The band sounds noticeably worse, but not only because the drum part is missing, since the other
players were counting on the beat to keep time and they stumble too.

Judging the drummer’s job by how bad the band sounds would overstate it. A trained model works the
same way, because every later layer learned to expect that specialist’s changes. Removing it hands
those layers inputs they never saw in training, and nothing retrains to make up for the gap.

The damage you measure therefore mixes the specialist’s own job with the shock to everything after
it. Removing a part like this is called **ablation**, and getting an honest answer from it takes the
more careful methods in the circuits lab.

Position has real-world limits as well, because adding position information is necessary but not
enough, and a trained model still has to learn to use it. How well a model copes with texts longer
than the ones it trained on also depends on which position scheme it uses.

Finally, for most models the parameter count tracks both the memory a model needs and the work it
does for each word. Some designs separate the two, because they hold many MLPs per block but run
only a few of them for each word, as Going deeper explains.

## Going deeper

### Counting a block from its shape

You can work out a model’s size from its shape, because most of its adjustable numbers sit in grids
that turn one list into another. Each new number is a mix of all d old ones, and each mix needs d
adjustable numbers of its own, one for each old number. So a grid that turns a list of d numbers
into a new list of d numbers needs d × d entries, written d².

Attention needs four of these grids, one each to make queries, keys and values and one to combine
the heads’ results, which is the **Attention** row’s `4 · d²`. With d = 768, one grid holds 589,824
numbers, so the four of them together hold 2,359,296.

The MLP needs two bigger grids, one that widens the list to 4d and one that squeezes it back. For
GPT-2 small the widened list has 3,072 numbers, which is 4 times 768. So the **MLP** row reads `2 ·
d · 3,072`, which comes to 4,718,592, twice the attention count.

The MLP’s two grids hold 8 · d² between them, so adding attention’s 4 · d² gives about 12 · d² for a
whole block. That is why the **Block** row compares the total with this rule of thumb, and why
two-thirds of every block is MLP.

The two tidying steps add only a scale and a shift for each of the d places. At d = 768 they make up
about 0.04% of a block, so choosing between LayerNorm and RMSNorm affects speed and steadiness
rather than size. Because the count grows with d², doubling the width makes each block four times
bigger.

Load **GPT-2 small**, and the lab adds its 12 blocks to a word lookup table for 50,257 tokens. It
also counts a table of positions, the biases and a final norm, and it reproduces the published
124,439,808 exactly.

### Newer MLPs

Newer designs mostly change the MLP rather than attention, and one such design, **SwiGLU**, splits
the widening step into two branches that are multiplied together. One branch acts a little like a
dimmer switch on the other. Since SwiGLU uses three grids instead of two, it narrows the middle to
about 8/3 of the width, which keeps its total close to the classic MLP’s.

It is the `SwiGLU ≈8/3×` choice in the **MLP** control, and **Llama 2 7B** uses it with a middle
width of 11,008. With it, the preset still adds up to Llama 2 7B’s published 6,738,415,616.

A **mixture of experts** goes further by keeping many MLPs in each block but sending each word to
only a few of them. A model can then have an enormous total size while each word still pays only a
small bill in work.

### Smaller attention

**Grouped-query attention**, covered in **Modern attention at scale**, lets several heads share the
same keys and values. That shrinks two of attention’s four grids, so real attention often costs less
than `4 · d²`.

### A cheaper tidying step

**RMSNorm** skips subtracting the average and has no learned shift, so it only divides by the
typical size of the numbers and then applies a learned scale. It is cheaper to compute, works about
as well in practice, and is the standard in recent models such as Llama.

### Other ways to supply order

Real models handle order in several ways, and some of them add a learned list of position numbers
for each slot. Others add a fixed wave pattern, as this lab does, while others lower the match score
between two words the farther apart they sit, a scheme called ALiBi.

Many current models, Llama among them, add nothing at the input at all. Instead, they twist each
query and key by an angle that depends on its slot, a scheme called rotary embeddings. **Modern
attention at scale** shows it at work, and the **Attention** lab lines the schemes up.

A model that only reads earlier words can pick up a little order from that rule alone. Even so,
most models still supply position information on purpose, either at the input or inside attention.

### Peeking partway up

At the very top of the stack, one more tidying step and a word-scoring layer turn each word’s list
of numbers into odds for the next token. You can also apply that scoring layer to the residual
stream partway up the stack. That gives a rough peek at which next token the model was leaning
toward at that depth, a tool researchers call the logit lens.

### Specialists that read and write in the same space

Because every specialist only adds to a shared stream, you can picture each part of attention, and
each neuron in the MLP, as reading certain mixes of the stream’s numbers. Each part also writes its
own mix back, and two parts at different depths work together when what one writes overlaps with
what the other reads. The circuits lab is built on that way of looking at a model.
