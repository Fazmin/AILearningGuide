## The big picture

This module is about **direct preference optimization (DPO)**: training a language model on
comparisons, each a prompt with a preferred and a rejected completion, through one
classification-style loss. It asks what a successful-looking run has actually changed: which
completions moved, how far the model drifted from its starting point, and whether the preference
holds on comparisons it never trained on.

It follows **Post-training**, which fitted a reward model to comparisons and optimized against it
under a KL penalty, a cost on drifting from a reference distribution. **Instruction tuning &
templates** supplies the response-only loss that builds this lab's optional **SFT checkpoint**, a
stand-in for the supervised fine-tuned model DPO normally starts from.

### Where it shows up

- **After supervised fine-tuning.** Production DPO runs on an SFT checkpoint, which is normally
  also the frozen reference. Without one, Rafailov et al. (2023) build the reference by maximizing
  the likelihood of the preferred completions, which is how this lab's `SFT checkpoint` is built.
- **On off-policy data.** Pairs are usually collected from some other model's outputs.
- **As a family of objectives.** Length-normalized and reference-free variants (SimPO, ORPO), an
  added likelihood term on the preferred side, and online variants that sample fresh completions
  from the model being trained each target one of DPO's known limits.
- **In run monitoring.** Production diagnostics match the lab's readouts: both sides of the margin
  plotted apart, the share of pairs ordered correctly, drift, and a check on unseen pairs.

### Why it matters

DPO turns comparisons into gradients with no reward model, no sampling and no
reinforcement-learning loop. The KL penalty survives inside the loss: every reward is measured
against a frozen **reference model**, and **beta** sets how strongly the **policy**, the model
being trained, is held near it. Without that anchor, nothing in the loss would keep the policy
close to a working model.

The cost is a result that is easy to misread. The loss sees only the margin between the two
completions, can be satisfied mostly by making the rejected one unlikely, and grades only the
training pairs. Without separate readouts for each side, for drift and for unseen pairs, a falling
loss looks like a learned preference whether or not it is one.

### What you will be able to explain

- Run DPO on comparisons you write, and say why the loss reads ln 2 before the first step whatever
  the pairs contain.
- Read an **implicit reward margin** correctly: the gap between two completions of one prompt, each
  scored by beta times its log-probability gain over the reference, and never a quality score.
- Explain what **beta** trades away: separation between the completions against drift from the
  reference, and why, under this lab's plain gradient descent, that trade depends on the step
  budget.
- Judge whether a preference gain carries over, by scoring **held-out pairs** the optimizer never
  saw.

### What the lab shows

Every loss, gradient, reward and KL value is genuinely computed; the model, the data and the
optimizer are deliberately simple. The model conditions on one preceding character through 900
logits, trains on the pairs you write (three by default), and updates by plain full-batch gradient
descent with no sampling. **Reference model** chooses `Base model`, a table trained 50 epochs on
the Harbor and Recipes corpora, or `SFT checkpoint`, that base fine-tuned here on your preferred
completions.

In the **Preference pair editor**, write comparisons in **Preference pairs** as
`prompt | preferred | rejected`, then set **DPO steps**, **Beta** and **DPO learning rate**. Each
change retrains from the reference.

**Implicit reward margin during training** plots the margin and both of its sides, averaged over
the training pairs, at every step, and the held-out margin at evenly spaced step counts along the
same run. **Where each pair sits on the loss** places each pair's measured log-ratio margin
m, the margin with beta divided out, on the closed-form loss curve for the current beta, beside the
curves for half and double beta.

**Drift from the reference model** plots KL from the reference, averaged evenly over 30 context
rows, at three betas. **Per-pair rewards** shows both rewards for every training pair and for the
held-out pairs `H1` to `H3`, three fixed comparisons written before any run.

The key experiment raises **DPO steps** from 0 and compares the training pairs' `ordered correctly`
badge with **Held-out pairs ordered correctly**. At the defaults every training pair ends ordered
correctly, but only one of the three held-out pairs does.

## What it is

**DPO** trains on comparisons directly. There is no reward model, no sampling, and no
reinforcement-learning loop — just one loss over pairs of completions:

`L = −log sigmoid(beta · ((logp_chosen − logp_chosen_ref) − (logp_rejected − logp_rejected_ref)))`

The **implicit reward** of a completion is `beta` times how much more log-probability the
**policy** — the model being trained — gives it than the frozen **reference model** does. The **margin** is the chosen completion's
implicit reward minus the rejected one's. The loss is a decreasing function of the margin and of
nothing else.

By default the reference model is a real checkpoint: 50 epochs on the Harbor and Recipes corpora,
a base table. Production DPO starts from an **SFT checkpoint** instead. **Reference model** in the
pair editor can switch to one: the same base, fine-tuned here on your preferred completions with a
response-only loss, as in *Instruction tuning & templates*. That lab's own weights are never loaded.

The policy starts as an exact copy of the reference. Set **DPO steps** to 0 and **DPO loss** shows
0.6931, because every margin is exactly zero and `−log sigmoid(0)` is `ln 2`.

### What the comparisons look like

The three default pairs in **Preference pair editor** use `prompt | preferred | rejected`, and
every rejected completion is **degenerate repetition** — "fog fog fog fog fog." against "fog
settles over the harbor.". Looping on a phrase is a genuinely high-probability failure for a small
model, and in all three pairs the reference model assigns the loop the higher raw probability.

## Why it’s here

**Post-training** made the case for learning from comparisons: ranking two replies is faster
and more reliable than writing an ideal one, and DPO reaches the RLHF objective without a separate
scorer. It described DPO but never ran it.

The margin needs care precisely because it is the quantity being optimized. That makes it the last
quantity that can serve as evidence the optimization did what you wanted.

## How to play with it

Raise **DPO steps** from 0. At the defaults — three pairs, **Beta** 0.4, 40 steps, **DPO learning
rate** 0.35 — the loss falls from 0.6931 to 0.0317, the β-scaled margin rises to 3.497, the
**Log-ratio gap** reads 8.74 nats, and all three pairs end correctly ordered.

Now read the two dashed and dotted lines. The chosen reward ends at +0.556, the rejected at −2.940.
On **Where each pair sits on the loss**, the numbered pairs have slid right to m = 9.50, 8.85 and
7.88, where each still pulls at only 2 to 4% of full strength.

Then sweep **Beta** and read the three drift curves. The legend gives each run's gap and its final
KL. Try it at 40 steps and again at 4.

### Held-out pairs

Under **Per-pair rewards**, below the training pairs, sit three **held-out pairs**, `H1` to `H3`:
comparisons of the same kind that no step trains on, fixed before any run. At the defaults all
three training pairs end ordered correctly and only `H3` does. `H1` and `H2` end at margins of
−0.54 and −1.12. The held-out line in the first chart ends at +0.42, against +3.50 for the training
pairs.

### Change the reference

Switch **Reference model** to `SFT checkpoint`. The reference now already prefers the chosen
completion on two of the three training pairs, where the base preferred none, yet **DPO loss** at 0
steps still reads 0.6931. After 40 steps the held-out margins are −1.48, −1.76 and +2.70.

## What to notice

- The margin is bought lopsidedly. The rejected side falls about five times further than the chosen
  side rises. Most of the gap comes from making the bad completion unlikely.
- That is not a bug, and both sides falling would not be one either. The loss only ever sees the
  difference.
- Per pair, the chosen rewards are +0.18, +0.51 and +0.97; the rejected ones −3.61, −3.03 and −2.18.
  The rejected column moves further in every row.
- **Beta** plays two roles. It sets where the loss flattens: loss 0.1 needs m = 11.26 at β 0.2, 5.63
  at 0.4 and 2.82 at 0.8. It also multiplies every gradient step.
- So its effect on drift depends on the budget. At 40 steps, β 0.2, 0.4 and 0.8 end at KL 0.0133,
  0.0102 and 0.0053, with gaps of 10.2, 8.7 and 6.2 nats. At 4 steps the order flips: 0.0005,
  0.0013 and 0.0017.
- The β-scaled margin rises with β (2.045, 3.497, 4.949) while the gap it measures shrinks. Compare
  runs with the gap, not the scaled margin.
- The training margin and the held-out margin part ways. The training badge reads 100% and the
  held-out badge 1 of 3: `H1` and `H2` are ordered backwards and `H3` forwards.
- More steps make `H1` and `H2` worse, not better. Raise **DPO steps** and the held-out line stays
  near zero while the training margin climbs. At the default beta and learning rate, no step count
  orders more than one held-out pair.
- `H3` carries over because its loop, "pasta and the pasta and the pasta.", shares ` and the ` with
  training pair 2's loop. Train on pair 2 alone and `H3` ends at +2.25; on pair 1 alone, at +0.28.
- With the SFT checkpoint the reference already ranks pairs 2 and 3 correctly, and the lab still
  reports a loss of 0.6931 at 0 steps. Margins are measured from the reference, whatever it prefers.
  Drift at β 0.40 rises from 0.0102 to 0.0161, because it is now measured from that checkpoint.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** The objective, the gradients, the rewards and the KL are all real; the model is
not. It conditions on one preceding character through 900 logits, so a completion is a chain of
character transitions. Three pairs, full-batch plain gradient descent, and no sampling. Drift is an
even average over the 30 context rows, not weighted by how often each occurs.

The transitions inside "fog fog fog" are the same ones ordinary harbor text needs, so pushing the
rejected completion down necessarily takes probability from useful text. A model with real context
can learn that a phrase is fine once and bad five times in a row; this one cannot.

With Adam, which rescales steps per parameter, beta's step-size role largely disappears and its
saturating role dominates. The early-steps flip above is a property of plain gradient descent.

The held-out set is three fixed comparisons of one kind, a corpus sentence against a loop. It shows
that ordering does not carry over automatically, not how often it does. The SFT checkpoint is
trained on the same preferred completions the pairs contain, so it prefers them by construction; a
real SFT set is separate and far larger.

**In real systems.** A rising margin is evidence about the margin. **Reward hacking** — the measured
objective improving while human-judged quality stalls — survives the removal of the reward model,
because the margin is now the exploitable quantity.

**Length bias** is the concrete case. Raw total log-probability always favours the shorter
completion, and preference data in which longer answers tend to win teaches the policy to write
longer. Pairs are also usually collected off-policy, from some other model's outputs, so the policy
is taught about text it would not have produced.

## Going deeper

The closed form comes from **RLHF**. Maximizing reward minus β times the KL to the reference has a
known optimal policy, `πref · exp(r / β) / Z(x)`. Inverting it writes the reward as
`β · log(π / πref)` plus a per-prompt constant, and that constant cancels inside the **Bradley-Terry**
likelihood for a pair. The KL constraint is not dropped — it is absorbed, which is why beta is both
the reward scale and the constraint strength.

The variants each attack one limit above: length-normalized and reference-free objectives (SimPO,
ORPO) for length bias and the second model's memory, an added likelihood term on the chosen side
for the "both sides fell" outcome, and online variants that sample fresh completions from the
current policy and label those.

DPO is run on a supervised fine-tuned checkpoint, and that checkpoint is normally the reference.
Rafailov et al. (2023, arXiv 2305.18290) initialize the reference from the SFT model when one
exists and, when it does not, by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions. That
fallback is what **Reference model** `SFT checkpoint` does here. The default is still the base
table, so the lineage is only followed once you switch.

The production diagnostics are the ones on screen here: both sides plotted apart, the share of
pairs ordered correctly, drift, and a check on pairs the optimizer never trained on.
