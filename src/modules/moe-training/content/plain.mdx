## The big picture

Most AI models do all of their arithmetic on every piece of text they read, so every stored number, called a **weight**, takes part in every prediction. A **mixture of experts** works differently. It keeps several separate small networks, called **experts**, together with a little decision-maker, called the **router**, that sends each piece of text to only one or two of them.

The model stores every expert, but each piece of text only runs through the experts the router picked. That means a model can hold far more weights than it actually uses on any one piece of text, which is the whole appeal of the design. This module trains a small mixture of experts for real and shows the problem that comes with it.

Left to itself, the router ends up sending every piece of text to the same single expert, while the other experts never learn anything at all. Fixing that is possible, but every fix has a cost, and the lab lets you measure each cost yourself.

### Where you have met it

Several well-known AI models are built this way, and their published numbers show how big the gap between stored and used weights can get.

- Mixtral 8x7B is built from stacked stages called layers, and it has 8 experts in every layer. It sends each piece of text to 2 of them, so it stores 46.7 billion weights but uses only 12.9 billion for each piece of text.
- DeepSeek-V3 has 256 experts in each of these layers, plus 1 shared expert that every piece of text uses, and it picks 8 of the 256 each time. It stores 671 billion weights and uses 37 billion for each piece of text.
- OLMoE, a model whose code and training data are fully public, picks 8 of its 64 experts each time. It stores 7 billion weights and uses about 1 billion for each piece of text.
- Switch Transformer, an earlier design from Google, sends each piece of text to just one expert.

The idea itself is older than any of these models. In 1991, researchers built a system of several small networks plus a "gating" network that learned which of them should handle each example.

### Why it matters

When nothing stops it, the router tends to pick the same few experts over and over. The researchers who built one of the first large mixtures of experts, in 2017, described this as self-reinforcing, because the experts that get picked a lot learn faster and so get picked even more. The unused experts still take up memory, and memory is one of the most expensive parts of running a large model.

There are fixes, but none of them is free. One fix adds a penalty that competes with the model's main goal of guessing well. Another puts a limit on each expert, which throws some text away. And because big models spread their experts across many computer chips, a lopsided router leaves some chips idle while others are swamped with work.

- how two different fixes, an extra balancing penalty and a bonus number for each expert, push the router back toward sharing, and what each one costs;

### What the lab lets you do

Every loss, share and count of dropped text in the lab is measured from real training. The weight counts come from simple formulas, and one row of published figures about Mixtral is typed in. The lab trains a small mixture of experts on 1,181 pairs of neighbouring characters, taken from three short practice texts, and its job is to guess each character from the one before it.

Each piece of text a model reads at one time is called a **token**. Real models use whole words or parts of words as tokens, but in this lab every token is a single character, which is why the lab's readouts talk about tokens.

By default the model trains for 30 rounds, where each round is one pass through all of the pairs. It looks at 16 pairs at a time and nudges its weights after each group of 16, which adds up to 2,220 nudges over the whole run.

The lab has six cards, and each one shows a different part of the story:

- **Router and experts** holds the main settings, **Experts**, **Top-k**, **Epochs** and **Seed**, and shows how the trained router treats one **Example character**.
- **Expert load over training** draws how much of the text each expert received after every round, with the readouts **Effective experts**, **Loss** and **Dense baseline**.
- **Balancing** turns on one of the two fixes and compares the result with an identical copy that has no fix.
- **Capacity and dropped tokens** gives each expert a limited number of seats and counts the text that gets thrown away.
- **Specialisation** shows which characters, and which of the three texts, each expert receives.
- **Total vs active parameters** counts the weights stored against the weights used. Its numbers for this lab come from simple formulas, and its Mixtral row is a published figure that the lab did not measure.

Every time you move a control, the lab retrains three models from scratch: yours, the same one with no fix, and a plain one-expert model to compare against.

## What it is

### A help desk for text

Picture a homework help centre with four tutors and one person at the front desk. Each student walks up, the desk glances at them and sends them to one tutor, who works out an answer. The desk then passes that answer on, louder or quieter depending on how much it trusts that tutor for this particular student.

In the lab, each student is one character of text, the tutors are the experts, and the front desk is the router. The answer is a guess about which character comes next.

The picture stops matching in two places. Real tutors arrive already knowing things, but every expert here starts out as random numbers and learns only from the text it is sent. And the desk's trust is not a feeling, because it is a number worked out from the router's scores, as the next part explains.

### The router and its gates

The model reads one character and guesses the next one. First it turns the character into a short list of five numbers, called **h**. Four of them are learned numbers that describe that character, and the fifth one is always 1.

Then three things happen, one after another.

- The **router** gives every expert a score by multiplying the five numbers by its own weights and adding up the results. A higher score means the router thinks that expert suits this character better.
- The scores are turned into shares that add up to 1, where a higher score always gets a bigger share. Each expert's share is called its **gate value**.
- Only the experts with the highest scores actually run. Picking the top one or two like this is called **top-k routing**, where k is how many experts run, and the lab's **Top-k** control sets it to 1 or 2.

Each expert is a small table of weights that turns the five numbers into a score for each of the 30 possible next characters. The model adds up the chosen experts' scores, each one multiplied by its gate value, so an expert with a gate of 0.9 counts nine times as much as one with a gate of 0.1.

Here is a worked example from the lab's starting settings. For the letter t, the router gives expert E3 a score of 4.74, while the other three experts all score below −1.6. Turned into shares, that gives E3 a gate value of 1.00 and leaves almost nothing for the rest, so only E3 runs. Its answer puts h first, with a 39% chance, which fits English well.

There is one more number, called a **bias**, which the router adds to each expert's score only at the moment it picks experts. It stays at 0 unless you turn on one of the fixes, and it never changes a gate value.

### Why one expert wins

The fifth number in h, the one that is always 1, matters a lot. It gives every expert a starting guess that it uses for every character it receives, and it gives the router a starting score for each expert that applies to every character as well.

Together these two make a loop. An expert that happens to win a few more characters early on practises on more of them, so its starting guess improves. Because it now lowers the error more whenever it is chosen, the router learns to raise that expert's starting score, which wins it even more characters, and the loop goes round again.

You can watch this happen in the lab. At seed 1, before any training at all, the router's choices are split 12%, 23%, 42% and 23% across the four experts. Just one round later, expert E3 receives 100% of them. That is called **expert collapse**.

The lab describes the split in two ways. An expert's **expert load** is its share of the router's choices. **Effective experts** turns all the shares into one number, which is how many experts are really in use. Four equal shares read 4.00, one expert taking everything reads 1.00, and the 12-23-42-23 split before training reads 3.66, because it is uneven but uses all four.

## Why it’s here

In an ordinary model, often called a dense model, every weight does work for every piece of text, so making the model bigger also makes it slower on every word it reads. A mixture of experts breaks that link. Adding experts adds stored weights, but each piece of text still only runs through k of them, so the work for each piece of text stays about the same.

Researchers who study how models improve as they grow treat the number of weights and the amount of work for each piece of text as two separate dials for exactly this reason. With a mixture of experts, you can turn up the first dial without turning up the second.

Training a mixture of experts brings a problem that a dense model never has, because the router decides which weights get to learn. If it collapses onto one expert, the other experts are just memory that does nothing. So a mixture of experts needs three extra things: a way to keep the router sharing, a limit on the work each expert takes, and a plan for spreading experts across chips.

This lab picks up where The architecture zoo left off. Its Sparse experts card had a router that never trained, while here the router trains, and you can watch what training does to it.

## How to play with it

### 1. Watch it collapse

Leave **Balancing** on None, **Experts** at 4 and **Top-k** at 1. On **Expert load over training**, the line for E3 jumps to 100% after the first round and stays there for the rest of the run, while **Effective experts** reads 1.00.

Now compare **Loss**, which measures how wrong the guesses are, with **Dense baseline**, which is the same training with just one expert. Lower is better for both, and a model that knew nothing would score 3.401. **Loss** reads 2.197 and **Dense baseline** reads 2.199, so the two are almost exactly the same.

That makes sense once you think about it, because the collapsed model is really a one-expert model carrying three spare experts that never run. Collapse wastes memory, not accuracy. It happens in every one of the six seeds the lab offers, whether you use 2, 4 or 8 experts, and the loss always lands within 0.04 of the dense baseline.

### 2. Add a balancing loss

Switch **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss. Training normally works by lowering one number, the loss. This fix adds a second, extra number to the loss, called an **auxiliary load-balancing loss**, which is smallest when the experts share the work evenly, so lowering the total now means lowering both.

For each group of 16 pairs, the extra number looks at two things for every expert: its share of the router's first choices, and its average gate value. It multiplies those two together for each expert, adds up the results and multiplies by the number of experts. The lab shows the answer as **Balance term N·Σ f·P**, where N is the number of experts, f is the share of first choices, and P is the average gate.

That answer is 1 when the work is shared evenly, and it equals the number of experts when one expert takes everything. For the collapsed run at seed 1, E3 gets every first choice, so the sum is 4 × 1 × E3's average gate, which comes to 3.75. **Aux weight** sets how much this extra number counts in the total.

- At **Aux weight** 0.01, the value Switch Transformer used, the router still collapses, with 1.00 effective experts and a loss of 2.196. It collapses fully in five of the six seeds.
- At 0.1 the work spreads out to 3.89 effective experts, and the loss drops to 2.131, which beats the dense baseline. In all six seeds it ends between 3.59 and 3.96 effective experts, and below its own dense baseline.
- At 1 the work stays shared, at 3.84 effective experts, but the loss rises to 2.216, which is worse than the dense baseline. **Mean gate**, the average gate of the experts that ran, falls from 0.84 to 0.57.

That last result is the price of this fix. At weight 1 the push to share is so strong that the router spreads its trust out evenly across all the experts, so each chosen expert's answer gets turned down. In all six seeds, weight 1 ends worse than weight 0.1.

**Try this:** set **Aux weight** to 0.1, then move **Seed** through all six of its values. Watch **Effective experts** and **Loss** in the **Balancing** table, and check that the balanced run beats **Dense baseline** every single time.

### 3. Nudge with a bias

Switch **Balancing** to Bias update. This fix adds no extra loss at all, which is why it is called **auxiliary-loss-free balancing**. Instead, each expert gets its bias, a bonus number that is added to its score only when the router picks experts.

After every group of 16 pairs, each bias moves by a small fixed amount, which you set with **Bias step**. An expert that got fewer than its fair share of choices has its bias raised, and one that got more than its share has its bias lowered. Its gate value still comes from the score alone, without the bias.

In the help-desk picture, the bias works like a sticky note on each tutor's door that says "send me a few more" or "send me a few fewer". The desk reads the note when it chooses a tutor, but the note does not change how much the desk trusts that tutor's answer.

- At 0.001, the value DeepSeek-V3 used, the bias moves far too slowly for a run of 2,220 nudges. The run stays on one expert from round 1 to round 21 and ends at 1.63 effective experts, with a loss of 2.275. That is worse than the dense baseline, because the expert that finally receives some characters has had only a few rounds to learn them.
- At 0.03 the work spreads out to 4.00 effective experts, and the loss is 2.131. The bias bars end at +3.30, +1.59, +2.52 and +3.87. All four are positive, but only the gaps between them matter, since adding the same amount to every expert would not change any choice.
- **Mean gate** stays at 0.89, because the bias never touches a gate.

### 4. Cap each expert

Set **Capacity factor** to 1.25. Now each expert has a fixed number of seats in every group of 16 pairs. With 4 experts and top-1, a perfectly fair share would be 16 ÷ 4 = 4 seats each, and a factor of 1.25 raises that to 5 seats. The lab writes the sum as 1.25 × 16 × 1 ÷ 4, rounded up, which is 5.

Every character's first choice gets a seat before any second choice does, in the order the characters arrive. A choice that finds its expert already full becomes a **dropped token**, which means that expert never runs for it. A character that loses every expert it chose gets no answer at all, so the model guesses all 30 characters equally, which costs a loss of 3.401.

- With **Balancing** on None, 40% of choices are dropped at seed 1, and **Loss** jumps to 2.651. If one expert took every character, it could serve only 5 of every 16, so 11 of every 16 would be dropped, which is 69%. Seed 4 does exactly that, and across the six seeds the share runs from 40% to 69%.
- With Auxiliary loss at 0.1, only 11% of choices are dropped and **Loss** is 2.306. Bias update at 0.03 drops 6%, with a loss of 2.210.

**A common mix-up:** a seat limit does not balance the router. When an expert is full, the dropped character is not sent to another expert, because it is simply skipped. The limit stops one expert from doing all the work, but the router never learns to share, so the waste shows up as dropped text instead of idle experts.

### 5. Read the specialists

Set **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss at 0.1 and look at **Specialisation**. With **Grid** on By previous character, each row is one character, the number beside it is how many pairs start with that character, and the shading shows which expert receives it. Every row has a single dark square.

At seed 1, E2 takes the vowels a, e, o and u, plus w and z, while E4 takes the space between words and nothing else. E1 takes b, c, d, f, h, m, p, s, t, v, y and the full stop, and E3 takes g, i, k, l, n, q and r.

Now switch **Grid** to By corpus, where a corpus is one of the three practice texts. Each text's split across the experts lands within ±0.01 of the overall split, which means the three texts are shared out almost identically.

Neither of these results is a discovery about the experts. The router only ever sees the previous character, so every copy of a character has to go the same way, and a text can only follow the mix of characters it contains. In the help-desk picture, the desk sees only the first letter of each student's name, so it cannot possibly sort students by school subject.

### 6. Count what runs

Raise **Experts** from 1 to 8 with **Top-k** at 1, then set **Top-k** to 2. **Total parameters**, which counts every weight stored, goes from 275 to 1,360. **Active per token**, which counts the weights one character actually uses, goes from 275 to 310, and then to 460 at top-2. Those used weights are called **active parameters**.

Here is where the counts come from at the starting settings. The four learned numbers for each of the 30 characters make 120 weights, the router has 5 weights for each of the 4 experts, which is 20, and each expert has 5 × 30 = 150. Storing all four experts gives 120 + 20 + 600 = 740, while one character uses only 120 + 20 + 150 = 290.

With **Balancing** on Auxiliary loss at 0.1, **Loss** is 2.170 with 8 experts and 2.123 at top-2, compared with 2.131 for 4 experts.

The **Mixtral 8x7B** row shows the same split at an enormous scale: 46.7 billion weights stored and 12.9 billion used for each piece of text. In other words, each piece of text uses 28% of the model.

## What to notice

- Collapse is fast. At seed 1 it takes a single round, which is 74 nudges.
- A collapsed model guesses about as well as the one-expert model, because the waste is memory, held by experts that never run.
- **Balance term N·Σ f·P** reads 3.75 when the router has collapsed at seed 1, and 1.04 at Aux weight 0.1, which is close to the even value of 1.
- The balancing weight has a window that works. At 0.01 the router collapses, at 0.1 it shares and guesses better, and at 1 it shares but guesses worse, so here the window starts about 10 times higher than Switch's 0.01.
- The bias fix balances without touching the gates. Across all six seeds its average loss at Bias step 0.03 is 2.138, against 2.129 at Aux weight 0.1. That gap is smaller than the difference between seeds, so this lab cannot say which fix is better.
- More experts is not automatically better. At Aux weight 0.1, 8 experts end worse than 4 in five of six seeds, and with Bias step 0.03 they do in all six. Each expert now sees only about an eighth of the pairs, so it gets less practice, which researchers call the shrinking batch problem.
- At seed 1, top-2 with 8 experts guesses slightly better than top-1 with 4, but it runs twice as many expert weights for every character.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The lab trains a real model, but that model is much simpler than a real one in several ways.

- Each expert is a single small table of weights rather than a full network, and there is only one layer, with none of the other parts a real model has. Because of that, a dropped character gets no answer and costs a loss of 3.401, where a real model would pass it along unchanged.
- The router sees only the previous character, so the clean rows on **Specialisation** are guaranteed, and the even spread across the three texts is forced by the design rather than discovered.
- **Loss** is measured on the same 1,181 pairs the model trained on, so it shows how well the model fits this text, not how well it would do on new text.
- The gate values here are shares of all the experts' scores. DeepSeek-V3 works them out a different way, and Mixtral shares them out between only its two chosen experts.
- This lab needs a balancing weight 10 to 30 times bigger than Switch's 0.01, and a bias step 30 times bigger than DeepSeek-V3's 0.001. That is because it trains for 2,220 nudges rather than hundreds of thousands.
- How completely the lab collapses depends on its starting numbers. If the four learned numbers for each character start twice as large, a router with no fix keeps 1.5 to 1.9 effective experts in five of six seeds.
- The bias fix does not beat the balancing loss here. Its average loss over six seeds is 2.138 against 2.129, and it wins in only two of them.
- The researchers who invented the bias fix found the opposite in a model with a billion weights. On a measure called perplexity, where lower is better, it scored 9.50 against 9.56 for the balancing loss, and it spread the work far more evenly.
- The Mixtral row is a published number typed into the lab, not something the lab measured.

### In the real mechanism

Real mixtures of experts have their own limits, which are separate from this lab's limits.

- A real router sees what the surrounding text means, not just one character, so it can sort text in ways this one cannot. Even so, Mixtral's researchers found no obvious sorting by topic, because its choices followed grammar more than subject.
- Another study, ST-MoE, found experts for punctuation, verbs and names in one half of its model but not in the other half. OLMoE found an expert that takes nearly all the text from scientific papers posted on the arXiv website.
- Memory has to hold every expert. Switch-Base, a model with 7.4 billion weights, needs 8 to 10 computer chips just to hold its weights during training, while a 0.74 billion-weight dense model fits on a single chip.
- Big models spread their experts across many chips, which is called **expert parallelism**. Each piece of text travels to the chip that holds its chosen experts and then back again, so a lopsided router leaves some chips idle while others are swamped.
- Large routers can also become unstable, with scores that grow too big, and ST-MoE adds another small extra loss to keep them in check.

## Going deeper

### Many small experts

Newer designs split each expert into several smaller ones and pick more of them for each piece of text. They also set aside a few shared experts that every piece of text uses, which leaves the other experts free to specialise. DeepSeek-V3 picks 8 of its 256 experts plus its 1 shared expert in every one of these layers. It balances with a bias step of 0.001 for its first 14.3 trillion tokens, and it never drops any text.

### Training without dropping

Seat limits exist because computer chips work fastest when every expert gets the same amount of work. A system called MegaBlocks rewrites the expert arithmetic so that experts can take uneven amounts of work without wasting space or dropping text. OLMoE also trains without dropping any text, using a balancing weight of 0.01.
