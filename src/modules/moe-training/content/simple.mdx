## The big picture

Most AI models do all their maths on every piece of text they read. Their stored numbers are called **weights**, and every weight helps with every guess.

A **mixture of experts** works in a different way. It keeps several small networks, called **experts**, side by side. A small decision-maker, called the **router**, sends each piece of text to just one or two of them.

The model stores every expert, but each piece of text only runs through the chosen ones. So the model can hold many more weights than it uses on any one piece of text. That is the whole appeal of the design.

This module trains a small mixture of experts for real. It also shows the big problem that comes with it. Left alone, the router sends every piece of text to the same single expert. The other experts never learn anything at all.

You can fix that, but every fix has a cost. The lab lets you measure each cost for yourself.

### Where you have met it

Several well-known AI models are built this way. Their published numbers show how big the gap can get.

- Mixtral 8x7B is built from stacked stages called layers, with 8 experts in every layer.
- Mixtral sends each piece of text to 2 of its 8 experts.
- It stores 46.7 billion weights but uses only 12.9 billion for each piece of text.
- DeepSeek-V3 has 256 experts in each of these layers, plus 1 shared expert that all text uses.
- It picks 8 of the 256 each time, using 37 billion of its 671 billion weights.
- OLMoE shares all its code and data, and it picks 8 of its 64 experts each time.
- It stores 7 billion weights and uses about 1 billion for each piece of text.
- Switch Transformer, an earlier design from Google, sends each piece of text to just one expert.

The idea is older than all of these. In 1991, researchers built a set of small networks with a "gating" network. The gating network learned which small network should handle each example.

### Why it matters

When nothing stops it, the router picks the same few experts again and again. Researchers built an early big mixture of experts in 2017. They said this problem feeds itself. The experts that get picked a lot learn faster, so they get picked even more.

The unused experts still take up memory. Memory is one of the most costly parts of running a big model.

There are fixes, but none of them is free. One fix adds a penalty that fights with the model's main goal. Another fix limits each expert, and that throws some text away. Big models also spread their experts across many computer chips. A lopsided router leaves some chips idle while others are swamped.

## What it is

### A help desk for text

Picture a homework help centre with four tutors and one person at the front desk. Each student walks up, and the desk sends them to one tutor. That tutor works out an answer to the student's question.

The desk then passes the answer on, louder or quieter. How loud depends on how much the desk trusts that tutor for this student.

In the lab, each student is one character of text. The tutors are the experts, and the front desk is the router. The answer is a guess about which character comes next.

The picture stops matching in two places. Real tutors already know things, but every expert here starts as random numbers. An expert learns only from the text it is sent.

Also, the desk's trust is not a feeling. It is a number worked out from the router's scores, as the next part explains.

### The router and its gates

The model reads one character and guesses the next one. First it turns the character into a short list of five numbers, called **h**. Four of them are learned numbers that describe the character. The fifth number is always 1.

Then three things happen in order:

- The **router** gives every expert a score, using its own weights and the five numbers.
- A higher score means the router thinks that expert suits this character better.
- Next, the scores become shares that add up to 1, and higher scores get bigger shares.
- Each expert's share is called its **gate value**.
- Last, only the experts with the top scores run, and the rest are skipped.
- Picking the top one or two like this is called **top-k routing**.
- Here k is how many experts run, and the lab's **Top-k** control sets it to 1 or 2.

Each expert is a small table of weights. It turns the five numbers into a score for each of the 30 possible next characters. The model adds up the chosen experts' scores, each one times its gate value.

So an expert with a gate of 0.9 counts nine times as much as one with 0.1.

Here is an example from the lab's starting settings. For the letter t, the router gives expert E3 a score of 4.74. The other three experts all score below −1.6.

Turned into shares, that gives E3 a gate value of 1.00, and almost nothing for the rest. So only E3 runs for t. Its top guess for the next letter is h, with a 39% chance. That fits English well.

There is one more number, called a **bias**. The router adds it to each expert's score, but only while picking experts. It stays at 0 unless you turn on a fix, and it never changes a gate value.

### Why one expert wins

The fifth number in h, the one that is always 1, matters a lot. It gives every expert a starting guess that it uses for every character it gets. It also gives the router a starting score for each expert, which counts for every character.

Together these two make a loop, a bit like a snowball rolling downhill:

- An expert that wins a few more characters early on practises on more of them.
- With that practice, its starting guess gets better.
- Now it cuts the error more whenever it is chosen.
- So the router learns to raise that expert's starting score.
- The higher score wins it even more characters, and the loop goes round again.

You can watch this happen in the lab. At seed 1, before any training, the router splits its choices 12%, 23%, 42% and 23%. Just one round later, expert E3 gets 100% of them. This is called **expert collapse**.

The lab describes the split in two ways. An expert's **expert load** is its share of the router's choices.

**Effective experts** turns all the shares into one number: how many experts are really in use. Four equal shares read 4.00, and one expert taking everything reads 1.00. The uneven 12-23-42-23 split before training reads 3.66.

## Why it’s here

In an ordinary model, often called a dense model, every weight works on every piece of text. So a bigger model is also slower on every word it reads.

A mixture of experts breaks that link. More experts means more stored weights. But each piece of text still runs through just k of them. So the work for each piece of text stays about the same.

Researchers who study how models grow treat these as two separate dials. One dial is the number of weights. The other is the work done for each piece of text. A mixture of experts lets you turn up the first dial without the second.

Training a mixture of experts brings a problem that a dense model never has. The router decides which weights get to learn. If it collapses onto one expert, the other experts are memory that does nothing.

So a mixture of experts needs three extra things. It needs a way to keep the router sharing, and a limit on each expert's work. It also needs a plan for spreading experts across chips.

This lab picks up where The architecture zoo left off. Its Sparse experts card had a router that never trained. Here the router trains, so you can watch what training does to it.

## How to play with it

### 1. Watch it collapse

Leave **Balancing** on None, **Experts** at 4 and **Top-k** at 1. Look at **Expert load over training**. The line for E3 jumps to 100% after the first round and stays there. **Effective experts** reads 1.00.

Now compare **Loss** with **Dense baseline**. **Loss** measures how wrong the guesses are, so lower is better. **Dense baseline** is the same training with just one expert. A model that knew nothing at all would score 3.401.

**Loss** reads 2.197 and **Dense baseline** reads 2.199, so they are almost the same. That makes sense when you think about it. The collapsed model is really a one-expert model with three spare experts that never run.

Collapse wastes memory, not accuracy. It happens in all six seeds the lab offers, with 2, 4 or 8 experts. Each time, the loss lands within 0.04 of the dense baseline.

### 2. Add a balancing loss

Switch **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss. Normally, training works by lowering one number, the loss. This fix adds a second, extra number to it. That extra number is called an **auxiliary load-balancing loss**.

The extra number is smallest when the experts share the work evenly. So lowering the total now means lowering both numbers.

Here is how the extra number works for each group of 16 pairs:

- For every expert, it takes the expert's share of the router's first choices.
- It multiplies that share by the expert's average gate value.
- It adds those results up for all the experts.
- Then it multiplies the total by the number of experts.

The lab shows the answer as **Balance term N·Σ f·P**. It reads 1 when the work is shared evenly. It equals the number of experts when one expert takes everything.

For the collapsed run at seed 1, E3 gets every first choice. So the sum is 4 times 1 times E3's average gate, which comes to 3.75.

**Aux weight** sets how much this extra number counts. Step it through three values:

- At **Aux weight** 0.01, the value Switch Transformer used, the router still collapses.
- It reads 1.00 effective experts and a loss of 2.196, and it collapses fully in five of six seeds.
- At 0.1 the work spreads out to 3.89 effective experts.
- The loss drops to 2.131, which beats the dense baseline.
- In all six seeds, 0.1 ends between 3.59 and 3.96 effective experts and beats its dense baseline.
- At 1 the work stays shared at 3.84 effective experts, but the loss rises to 2.216.
- That is worse than the dense baseline, and **Mean gate** falls from 0.84 to 0.57.

**Mean gate** is the average gate of the experts that ran. Its fall shows the price of this fix. At weight 1 the push to share is so strong that the router spreads its trust evenly. So each chosen expert's answer gets turned down.

In all six seeds, weight 1 ends worse than weight 0.1.

**Try this:** set **Aux weight** to 0.1, then move **Seed** through all six of its values. Watch **Effective experts** and **Loss** in the **Balancing** table. Check that the balanced run beats **Dense baseline** every time.

### 3. Nudge with a bias

Switch **Balancing** to Bias update. This fix adds no extra loss at all. That is why it is called **auxiliary-loss-free balancing**.

Instead, each expert gets a bias, a bonus number added to its score. The router uses the bonus only while picking experts.

After every group of 16 pairs, each bias moves by a small fixed amount. You set that amount with **Bias step**. An expert that got fewer than its fair share has its bias raised. An expert that got more has its bias lowered. Its gate value still comes from the score alone, without the bias.

In the help-desk picture, the bias is like a sticky note on each tutor's door. It might say "send me a few more" or "send me a few fewer". The desk reads the note when it picks a tutor. But the note does not change how much the desk trusts that tutor's answer.

Now step **Bias step** from slow to fast:

- At 0.001, the value DeepSeek-V3 used, the bias moves far too slowly for 2,220 nudges.
- The run stays on one expert from round 1 to round 21.
- It ends at 1.63 effective experts, with a loss of 2.275.
- That is worse than the dense baseline.
- The expert that finally gets some characters has had only a few rounds to learn them.
- At 0.03 the work spreads out to 4.00 effective experts, and the loss is 2.131.
- The bias bars end at +3.30, +1.59, +2.52 and +3.87.
- All four are positive, but only the gaps between them matter.
- Adding the same amount to every expert would not change any choice.
- **Mean gate** stays at 0.89, because the bias never touches a gate.

### 4. Cap each expert

Set **Capacity factor** to 1.25. Now each expert has a fixed number of seats in every group of 16 pairs.

With 4 experts and top-1, a fair share is 16 ÷ 4 = 4 seats each. A factor of 1.25 raises that to 5 seats. The lab writes the sum as 1.25 × 16 × 1 ÷ 4, rounded up, which gives 5.

Every character's first choice gets a seat before any second choice does. They take seats in the order they arrive.

A choice that finds its expert full becomes a **dropped token**. That expert never runs for it. A character that loses every expert it chose gets no answer at all. Then the model guesses all 30 characters equally, which costs a loss of 3.401.

Here is what the cap does at seed 1:

- With **Balancing** on None, 40% of choices are dropped, and **Loss** jumps to 2.651.
- If one expert took every character, it could serve only 5 of every 16.
- Then 11 of every 16 would be dropped, which is 69%.
- Seed 4 does exactly that, and across the six seeds the share runs from 40% to 69%.
- With Auxiliary loss at 0.1, only 11% are dropped, and **Loss** is 2.306.
- Bias update at 0.03 drops 6%, with a loss of 2.210.

**A common mix-up:** a seat limit does not balance the router. When an expert is full, the dropped character is not sent to another expert. It is simply skipped. The limit stops one expert from doing all the work. But the router never learns to share, so the waste shows up as dropped text.

### 5. Read the specialists

Set **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss at 0.1, then look at **Specialisation**. Set **Grid** to By previous character. Each row is one character, and the number beside it counts its pairs. The shading shows which expert gets that character, and every row has one dark square.

At seed 1, the experts split the characters like this:

- E2 takes the vowels a, e, o and u, plus w and z.
- E4 takes the space between words and nothing else.
- E1 takes b, c, d, f, h, m, p, s, t, v, y and the full stop.
- E3 takes g, i, k, l, n, q and r.

Now switch **Grid** to By corpus. A corpus is one of the three practice texts. Each text's split across the experts lands within ±0.01 of the overall split. So the three texts are shared out almost the same way.

Neither result is a real discovery about the experts. The router only ever sees the previous character. So every copy of a character must go the same way. A text can only follow the mix of characters it contains.

In the help-desk picture, the desk sees only the first letter of each student's name. So it cannot possibly sort students by school subject.

### 6. Count what runs

Raise **Experts** from 1 to 8 with **Top-k** at 1. Then set **Top-k** to 2.

**Total parameters** counts every weight stored, and it goes from 275 to 1,360. **Active per token** counts the weights one character actually uses. It goes from 275 to 310, then to 460 at top-2. Those used weights are called **active parameters**.

Here is where the counts come from at the starting settings:

- The four learned numbers for each of the 30 characters make 120 weights.
- The router has 5 weights for each of the 4 experts, which is 20.
- Each expert has 5 × 30 = 150 weights.
- Storing all four experts gives 120 + 20 + 600 = 740 weights in total.
- One character uses only 120 + 20 + 150 = 290 of them.

Set **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss at 0.1 and compare **Loss**. It is 2.170 with 8 experts and 2.123 at top-2. With 4 experts it is 2.131.

The **Mixtral 8x7B** row shows the same split at a huge scale. Mixtral stores 46.7 billion weights and uses 12.9 billion for each piece of text. So each piece of text uses 28% of the model.

## What to notice

- Collapse is fast: at seed 1 it takes a single round, which is 74 nudges.
- A collapsed model guesses about as well as the one-expert model does.
- The waste is memory, held by the experts that never run at all.
- **Balance term N·Σ f·P** reads 3.75 when the router has collapsed at seed 1.
- At Aux weight 0.1 it reads 1.04, which is close to the even value of 1.
- The balancing weight only works inside a window of values, and the window is narrow.
- At 0.01 the router collapses, and at 0.1 it shares and guesses better.
- At 1 it still shares the work, but now it guesses worse than the dense baseline.
- Here the window starts about 10 times higher than Switch's value of 0.01.
- The bias fix balances the work without ever touching the gate values.
- Over all six seeds, its average loss at Bias step 0.03 is 2.138.
- At Aux weight 0.1 the average is 2.129, which is a very small gap.
- The gap is smaller than the change from seed to seed, so the lab cannot pick a winner.
- More experts is not always better, even when every expert gets some of the work.
- At Aux weight 0.1, 8 experts end worse than 4 in five of six seeds.
- With Bias step 0.03, 8 experts end worse than 4 in all six seeds.
- Each expert now sees only about an eighth of the pairs, so it gets less practice.
- Researchers call this the shrinking batch problem, because each expert's batch of text shrinks.
- At seed 1, top-2 with 8 experts guesses a little better than top-1 with 4.
- But it pays for that by running twice as many expert weights for every character.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The lab trains a real model, but that model is much simpler than a real one:

- Each expert is a small table of weights, not a full network.
- There is only one layer, without the other parts a real model has.
- So a dropped character gets no answer and costs a loss of 3.401.
- A real model would pass that text along unchanged.
- The router sees only the previous character, so the clean rows on **Specialisation** are guaranteed.
- The even spread across the three texts is forced by the design, not discovered.
- **Loss** is measured on the same 1,181 pairs the model trained on.
- So it shows how well the model fits this text, not how it does on new text.
- The gate values here are shares of all the experts' scores.
- DeepSeek-V3 works them out a different way.
- Mixtral shares them out between only its two chosen experts.
- This lab needs a balancing weight 10 to 30 times bigger than Switch's 0.01.
- It also needs a bias step 30 times bigger than DeepSeek-V3's 0.001.
- That is because it trains for 2,220 nudges, not hundreds of thousands.
- How fully the lab collapses depends on its starting numbers.
- Say each character's four learned numbers start twice as large.
- Then a router with no fix keeps 1.5 to 1.9 effective experts in five of six seeds.
- The bias fix does not beat the balancing loss here.
- Its average loss over six seeds is 2.138, against 2.129, and it wins in only two seeds.
- The bias fix's inventors found the opposite in a model with a billion weights.
- On a score called perplexity, where lower is better, the bias fix scored 9.50.
- The balancing loss scored 9.56, and the bias fix also spread the work far more evenly.
- The Mixtral row is a published number typed into the lab, not something it measured.

### In the real mechanism

Real mixtures of experts have their own limits, separate from this lab's:

- A real router sees what the nearby text means, not just one character.
- So it can sort text in ways this router cannot.
- Even so, Mixtral's researchers found no clear sorting by topic.
- Mixtral's choices followed grammar more than subject.
- Another study, ST-MoE, found experts for punctuation, verbs and names.
- It found them in one half of its model, but not in the other half.
- OLMoE found one expert that takes nearly all the text from science papers on arXiv.
- Memory has to hold every expert, even the ones that sit idle most of the time.
- Switch-Base has 7.4 billion weights and needs 8 to 10 chips just to hold them in training.
- A dense model with 0.74 billion weights fits on a single chip.
- Big models spread their experts across many chips, which is called **expert parallelism**.
- Each piece of text travels to the chip holding its chosen experts, then back again.
- A lopsided router leaves some chips idle while others are swamped.
- Big routers can also become unstable, with scores that grow too large.
- ST-MoE adds another small extra loss to keep those scores in check.

## Going deeper

### Many small experts

Newer designs split each expert into several smaller ones. They pick more of them for each piece of text.

They also keep a few shared experts that every piece of text uses. That leaves the other experts free to specialise.

DeepSeek-V3 picks 8 of its 256 experts, plus its 1 shared expert, in every one of these layers. It balances with a bias step of 0.001 for its first 14.3 trillion tokens. It never drops any text.

### Training without dropping

Seat limits exist because chips work fastest when every expert gets the same amount of work. A system called MegaBlocks rewrites the expert maths in a new way. Then experts can take uneven amounts of work without wasting space or dropping text.

OLMoE also trains without dropping any text, and it uses a balancing weight of 0.01.
