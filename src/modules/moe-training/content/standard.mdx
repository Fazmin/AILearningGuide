## A big model that runs like a small one

Mixtral 8x7B stores 46.7B parameters but uses only 12.9B on any one token (Mistral AI, 2023; Jiang et al., 2024). It sends each token to 2 of its 8 experts in every layer: the weights of a big model, the compute of a small one.

That design is a **mixture of experts**: several copies of one block, the experts, and a small **router** that sends each token to only a few of them. Other examples:

- DeepSeek-V3: 256 routed experts and 1 shared expert per MoE layer, 8 routed per token, 671B parameters in total and 37B active (DeepSeek-AI, 2024).
- OLMoE-1B-7B, fully open: 8 of 64 experts, 7B in total, about 1B per token (Muennighoff et al., 2024).
- Switch Transformer: one expert per token (Fedus et al., 2022). The idea goes back to a gating network that learns which expert handles each training case (Jacobs et al., 1991).

### Why it matters

In a dense model every weight runs on every token. A mixture of experts breaks that link: total parameters grow with the number of experts, while compute per token grows only with `k`. Clark et al. (2022) treat parameter count and compute as two separate axes and fit scaling laws over both, for routed models with up to hundreds of experts.

Now the catch: the router decides which weights get to learn. In this lab, within one epoch, it wrecks the design it belongs to. So treat this lesson as a case file: we'll watch it fail, find the cause, try three repairs and read each one's price tag.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end you will be able to:

- explain top-k routing: how router scores pick k experts and how gate values weight their outputs;
- recognise expert collapse as a rich-get-richer loop, and spot it in the load readouts;
- compare an auxiliary balancing loss with bias-update balancing, including what each costs in loss;
- predict the share of tokens a capacity factor drops, and why a cap does not balance the router;
- read what the experts specialise in, and what the router's input lets it tell apart;
- count total against active parameters, and connect them to memory and expert parallelism.

### What the lab trains

The lab trains a small mixture-of-experts layer on all 1,181 character pairs of the three tiny corpora, predicting each character from the one before. It uses plain SGD with learning rate 0.5 and batches of 16; the default 30 epochs are 2,220 steps, and **Seed** picks the starting weights. Every change retrains three runs: yours, the same with no balancing, and a one-expert dense baseline.

Every loss, load share and drop rate is measured from those runs; parameter counts come from formulas, and the Mixtral 8x7B row is a published figure.

## Meet the router

First, how does a router choose? Briefly: it's the easy part.

### Scores, gates and top-k

The model reads one previous character `x`. Its input is `h = [E[x], 1]`: four learned numbers for the character, plus the constant 1. Hold on to that 1; it comes back.

- The **router** scores every expert: `z = h · R`, with `R` a 5 × N matrix.
- The **gate values** are `p = softmax(z)`.
- **Top-k routing** runs only the `k` experts with the highest `z + b`, where `b` is a balancing bias that stays 0 unless you switch it on.
- Each expert `W_e` is a 5 × 30 map from `h` to 30 next-character scores. The output sums `p_e · (h · W_e)` over the chosen experts.

So the bias picks experts but never changes a gate. Here the gates are a softmax over all N scores, as in Switch Transformer. Mixtral renormalises the softmax over its two chosen experts, and DeepSeek-V3 uses sigmoid scores.

On **Router and experts**, set **Example character** to t. At the defaults E3 scores 4.74 and the others score below −1.6, so E3's gate is 1.00 and only E3 runs, guessing h next at 39%. That gate looks like confidence. Keep it in mind.

Scores pick, gates weigh, only `k` experts work. Now train it.

## Press train and watch it fall apart

### Watch it collapse

Leave **Balancing** on None, **Experts** at 4 and **Top-k** at 1, and open **Expert load over training**. Each line is one expert's **expert load**, its share of the router's choices, at every epoch. At seed 1 the starting split is 12, 23, 42 and 23 percent.

**Effective experts** reads a split as one number, `exp(−Σ s ln s)` over the shares: 1.00 for one expert, N for an even split. The start reads 3.66, nearly all four in use.

**Pause and predict:** one epoch is 74 steps. After it, does the split stay spread out, even out, or tip toward one expert?

It tips all the way. After one epoch E3 has 100%, and it keeps it to the end: **Effective experts** reads 1.00. This is **expert collapse**, and that gate of 1.00 for t was its symptom.

Now the surprise. **Loss** reads 2.197, beside a **Dense baseline** of 2.199. The layer has become a dense model with three idle experts, so collapse costs memory, not fit. Only the load readouts warn you.

**How sure is this?** In all six seeds the run collapses onto one expert with 2, 4 or 8 experts, and its loss stays within 0.04 of the dense baseline.

In 74 steps, one expert took everything. Why that one, and why so fast?

### Why one expert wins

Back to the constant 1 in `h`. It gives each expert a next-character bias that all of its tokens train, and gives the router a bias for each expert. Together they make a loop.

An expert that wins a few more tokens trains its bias on them, so it predicts better whenever it is chosen. The gate gradient then raises its router score, and through the router's bias that applies to every character. It wins more tokens, and the loop turns again. E3 began with the largest share, 42%, and finished with all of it.

Shazeer et al. (2017) called this imbalance self-reinforcing: favoured experts train faster and are chosen even more. The start was fairly even; the winner is made by training.

Collapse is a feedback loop, not bad luck, so a repair has to push against it.

## Three repairs, and what each one costs

Each repair fixes something real, and each one charges for it.

### Repair 1: a penalty for lopsided work

The first idea: make imbalance cost something. On the **Balancing** card, set **Balancing** to Auxiliary loss. The **auxiliary load-balancing loss** adds `α · N · Σ f_e · P_e` to each batch's loss, where `f_e` is expert e's share of the batch's first choices, held fixed, and `P_e` is its mean gate (Fedus et al., 2022).

Its gradient reaches the router through `P`. The term is 1 for an even split and N when one expert takes everything; **Balance term N·Σ f·P** reads 3.75 for the collapsed run at seed 1.

**Pause and predict:** Switch Transformer used an **Aux weight** of 0.01. Is that enough to break the loop here? What about 0.1?

At 0.01 the router still collapses: 1.00 effective experts, loss 2.196. At 0.1 it works: 3.89 effective experts, a balance term of 1.04, and a loss of 2.131, below dense.

So the weight is a dial with a window, and here the window starts about 10 times above Switch's 0.01. If 0.1 helps, does more help more?

**How sure is this?** At 0.01 the run collapses fully in five of six seeds. At 0.1 all six seeds end between 3.59 and 3.96 effective experts, and below their dense baseline.

### Too much of a good thing

**Pause and predict:** set **Aux weight** to 1. The split will stay even. What happens to **Loss**?

It stays even, 3.84, but **Loss** rises to 2.216, above dense, and **Mean gate** falls from 0.84 to 0.57. The balancing pull now outweighs the language-model loss and spreads the gates, which shrinks each chosen expert's output. The router is balanced and worse at its real job.

**How sure is this?** Weight 1 ends worse than 0.1 in all six seeds.

**Price tag:** the auxiliary loss competes with the main loss. Too little and the router collapses; too much and it flattens the gates and costs fit.

### Repair 2: a thumb on the scale

The second idea nudges the choice instead of the loss. Switch **Balancing** to Bias update, which is **auxiliary-loss-free balancing** (Wang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2024). After each batch every expert's bias moves by **Bias step** γ: up if it got fewer than its even share of `16 · k / N` choices, down if it got more.

**Pause and predict:** DeepSeek-V3 uses a bias step of 0.001, and this run is 2,220 steps. Will 0.001 rescue the router in time?

It won't. The run sits on one expert from epoch 1 to epoch 21 and ends at 1.63 effective experts, with a loss of 2.275, worse than dense. The expert it finally hands tokens to has had little time to learn them.

At 0.03, 30 times DeepSeek-V3's value, the run reaches 4.00 effective experts and a loss of 2.131. The bias bars end at +3.30, +1.59, +2.52 and +3.87, all drifted upward. Only their differences matter: adding the same amount to every expert changes no choice.

**Mean gate** stays at 0.89, because the bias never enters a gate. That is what weight 1 couldn't do: balance without flattening.

**How sure is this?** Over seeds 1 to 6, Bias step 0.03 averages a loss of 2.138 against 2.129 for Aux weight 0.1, winning in two. The gap is smaller than the seed spread. Wang et al. (2024) report the opposite at 1B parameters, a validation perplexity of 9.50 against 9.56 with far better balance; this toy does not reproduce it.

**Price tag:** the bias needs time. A step sized for hundreds of thousands of steps leaves a short run collapsed for most of training.

### Repair 3: a seat limit

The third idea caps each expert's work. On **Capacity and dropped tokens**, set **Capacity factor** to 1.25. Each expert gets `⌈1.25 × 16 × k ÷ N⌉ = 5` seats in a batch of 16 (Fedus et al., 2022). First choices are seated before second choices, each in batch order, as in GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021). A choice that finds its expert full is a **dropped token**.

**Pause and predict:** with **Balancing** on None, a fully collapsed router sends all 16 tokens to one 5-seat expert. What share drops?

It would drop 11 of every 16, 69%. Seed 1 keeps two experts busy and drops 40%, with a **Loss** of 2.651. Balancing shrinks the drops: Auxiliary loss at 0.1 drops 11% (loss 2.306), and Bias update at 0.03 drops 6% (loss 2.210).

The cap never sends a token to another expert. It bounds the work, which limits the damage from imbalance, but it teaches the router nothing.

**How sure is this?** With no balancing, the dropped share runs from 40% to 69% across six seeds; seed 4 collapses fully and drops exactly 69%.

**Price tag:** the cap throws tokens away. A dropped choice adds nothing to the token's output and no gradient to that expert.

### The ledger

- **Auxiliary loss.** Fixes the collapse. Costs fit outside its window, since it competes with the main loss. Used by Switch Transformer and OLMoE, both at 0.01.
- **Bias update.** Fixes the collapse without touching the loss or the gates. Costs time: the step must suit the run. Used by DeepSeek-V3 at 0.001, with a tiny sequence-level auxiliary loss as a backstop.
- **Capacity cap.** Bounds each expert's work. Costs dropped tokens, and balances nothing. Used by Switch Transformer and GShard, which pass an overflowed token on through the residual connection.

The router now spreads its work. But did the experts learn anything worth spreading it for?

## So what did the experts learn?

### Who gets which letters

Keep **Balancing** on Auxiliary loss at 0.1 and open **Specialisation**, with **Grid** on By previous character.

**Pause and predict:** will a character's tokens spread across experts, or go to one?

Every row is one dark cell: **Top expert's share per character** reads 100%. At seed 1, E2 takes a, e, o and u, plus w and z; E4 takes space alone; E1 takes b, c, d, f, h, m, p, s, t, v, y and the full stop; E3 takes g, i, k, l, n, q and r. Vowels together, space alone: it looks like a discovery.

Now switch **Grid** to By corpus. Each corpus splits within ±0.01 of the overall split. No expert for recipes, none for proverbs.

### What the router could see

Before believing either, ask what the router could see: only the previous character. So every occurrence of a character must take the same route, and the clean rows are guaranteed. A corpus can only inherit the mix of its characters, so the even corpus split is forced too. Neither result is a discovery about the experts.

What training does decide is which characters share an expert. Change **Seed** and the grouping changes.

Real routers read a contextual hidden state, so for them it is an open question:

- Mixtral's routing analysis finds no obvious specialisation by topic; choices follow syntax more than domain (Jiang et al., 2024).
- ST-MoE finds encoder experts for punctuation, verbs and proper names, but not in the decoder (Zoph et al., 2022).
- OLMoE finds an expert that takes nearly all arXiv tokens (Muennighoff et al., 2024).

So check a router's input before you read its grid. That leaves the bill.

## Counting the bill

### Stored against used

On **Total vs active parameters**, set **Experts** to 1 at **Top-k** 1. **Pause and predict:** raise **Experts** to 8. How much do stored weights grow, and how much does each token's work grow?

**Total parameters** climbs from 275 to 1,360; **Active per token**, the **active parameters** one token runs, moves from 275 to 310. Set **Top-k** to 2 and it rises to 460: `k`, not N, sets the work. The **Mixtral 8x7B** row is the same split at scale: 46.7B stored, 12.9B used per token, an active share of 28%.

### More experts isn't free

Does storing more buy a better fit? With **Balancing** on Auxiliary loss at 0.1, **Loss** is 2.170 at 8 experts, against 2.131 at 4. Each of the 8 sees only about an eighth of the pairs; Shazeer et al. (2017) call this the shrinking batch problem.

Two experts per token helps: at seed 1, top-2 over 8 reaches 2.123, edging out top-1 over 4, at the price of running twice the expert weights per token.

**How sure is this?** 8 experts end worse than 4 in five of six seeds at Aux weight 0.1, and in all six at Bias step 0.03.

### Memory follows the total

Stored weights must live somewhere. Switch-Base, at 7.4B parameters, needs 8 to 10 GPUs just to hold the model for training, where the 0.74B T5-Large fits on one (Rajbhandari et al., 2022).

So large mixtures use **expert parallelism**: different experts on different GPUs, with each token sent to the device holding its experts and back (Lepikhin et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2024). Here collapse returns as a hardware bill: uneven load leaves some GPUs idle while others queue. **Splitting training across GPUs** picks up from there.

## Where the toy and the real thing part ways

Each limit changes something you just saw.

### In this lab

- Each expert is one 5 × 30 linear map, a rank-4 pattern plus a bias, not a feed-forward network, in a single layer with no attention and no residual stream. A dropped token therefore predicts uniformly, a loss of ln 30 = 3.401, where a real model would pass it on unchanged. Drops cost far more loss here.
- The router sees only the previous character, so the clean rows and even corpus split on **Specialisation** are forced, not found.
- **Loss** is measured on the same 1,181 pairs the run trained on: every comparison above is about fit, not generalisation.
- The gates are a softmax over all experts; DeepSeek-V3 uses sigmoid scores normalised over the chosen experts, so the **Mean gate** readings hold for this gating only.
- The toy needs an aux weight 10 to 30 times Switch's 0.01, and a bias step 30 times DeepSeek-V3's 0.001, because a run here is 2,220 steps rather than hundreds of thousands.
- How completely the toy collapses depends on its starting scale. Start the embeddings twice as large and no balancing leaves 1.5 to 1.9 effective experts in five of six seeds. The loop is real; the clean 1.00 is partly the toy's.
- The bias method does not beat the auxiliary loss here, the reverse of Wang et al.'s 1B result: a fact about this toy, not the method.
- The Mixtral row is an authored constant from Mistral AI's announcement, not a measurement.

### In the real mechanism

- A contextual router can specialise in ways this one cannot.
- Real runs measure load per layer and per batch, and a router can drift after it looks balanced. An even split counts choices, not quality: experts can still learn nothing distinct.
- Spread over GPUs, a collapse that matches dense loss here would leave real hardware idle.
- Large routers can be numerically unstable; ST-MoE adds a router z-loss to keep their scores small (Zoph et al., 2022). This lab has none.

## Where this goes next

### Many small experts

DeepSeekMoE splits each expert into several smaller ones, routes to more of them, and sets a few shared experts aside that every token uses, so the routed experts can specialise (Dai et al., 2024). DeepSeek-V3 runs 8 of 256 routed experts plus one shared expert, balances with a bias step of 0.001 for its first 14.3T tokens, and drops no tokens (DeepSeek-AI, 2024).

### Dropless training

Capacity exists because fixed-size expert batches suit the hardware. MegaBlocks rewrites the expert computation as block-sparse matrix products, so experts can take uneven batches without padding or dropping (Gale et al., 2023). OLMoE trains dropless with an auxiliary loss weight of 0.01 (Muennighoff et al., 2024).
