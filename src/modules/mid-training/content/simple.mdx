## The big picture

A language model learns by reading a huge pile of text. That text comes from many sources, like web pages, code and math.

The total amount of reading is fixed before training starts. So the team must decide how much of each source the model reads, a plan called a **data mixture**.

Near the end of training, many teams change the plan on purpose. They give the model much more of the text they care about most, like math or code. They still mix in normal text, so the model keeps what it learned. That late change is called **mid-training**.

This module shows why mid-training works and what it costs.

### Where you have met it

Each chatbot you have used was trained on a mixture, and a few teams publish theirs. Meta wrote a report on its Llama 3 models. By the end, about half of their text was general knowledge.

About a quarter was math and reasoning, 17% was code, and 8% was in other languages. In the last 40 million tokens, its learning rate shrank to zero. The mix also gave extra weight to its very best sources.

OLMo 2 is an open model, and its team trained it in two stages. They call the shorter second stage mid-training, and it used 5 to 10 percent of the computing work. Its text leaned toward carefully filtered writing and math.

### Why it matters

The amount of reading is fixed, so more reading for one source means less for the others.

The text a team wants most is often the text it has least of. So reading more of it means reading the same pages again.

The order matters too, because the last pages a model reads have a big say in its learning.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- How a mixture splits a fixed budget into shares, and how a share turns into a number of reads.
- Why reading scarce text again helps at first, then stops helping, and then starts to hurt.
- Why teams switch to a mix heavy in the text they care about, as training slows at the end.
- How mid-training on a mix differs from training on the target text alone, in what each one costs.
- What a score on one source's text can tell you about a skill, and what it cannot.

### What the lab lets you do

Every score in this lab comes from real training that runs live as you move the controls. Five cards each show one part of the story:

- **Data mixture** splits one fixed amount of training among four sources and scores each one.
- **Arithmetic weight sweep** runs that training nine times, with more math in each run.
- **Answer probe** checks if the trained model can answer the math questions it was scored on.
- **Repeated data** trains on math alone, and you choose how many sentences it can use.
- **Late phase and annealing** changes the mix for the last fifth of training.

Three of the four sources are short texts used across this course. The fourth one, Arithmetic, is made up from three sentence patterns, like “four plus eight is twelve.”

The chart of the learning rate is drawn from a formula. It is the only picture here that is not measured.

## What it is

### The model and how it is scored

The model is the tiny one from *Train one from scratch*. It reads one character and guesses the next one. It looks up its guess in a table of 900 numbers, called **weights**.

Training nudges those weights a little after each small batch of examples, so its guesses get better. Each example is a pair of characters: one character and the one after it. Each batch here holds 16 pairs.

The lab scores the model by its **loss**, which is the average penalty for its guesses. The loss is measured in units called nats per character, and lower is better. A table that knows nothing gives each character the same chance. It scores 3.401, so that is the number to beat.

The most important rule is that test sentences are never used for training. Each source keeps the last quarter of its sentences out of training, rounded to whole sentences. These held-out sentences are what the lab scores the model on.

### Four domains

A source of text with its own style is called a **domain**, and this lab has four of them:

- Harbor has eight sentences of weather notes, and 2 of them are held out.
- Recipes has eight cooking steps, and 2 of them are held out.
- Proverbs has ten short sayings, and 3 of them are held out.
- Arithmetic has 160 made-up math facts, and 40 of them are held out.

The tiny model has no digits, only letters, spaces and full stops. So each number in a math fact is spelled out in words.

The first three domains together are called General. A score on one domain's held-out sentences is a **per-domain held-out loss**. General's score is the average of the first three, and each one counts once.

### Shares of one budget

Each run on the mix cards takes 600 steps of 16 pairs, which makes 9,600 pairs in total. This fixed total is the **token budget**, since a token here is one character.

You set the split with four sliders, and each one holds a **mixture weight** from 0 to 8. Only the ratio between the weights counts.

The lab divides each weight by the sum of all four, and that gives the domain's share. Then each slot in a batch picks a domain, with a chance equal to its share.

Here is a worked example with the default setting, called General-heavy. Its weights are 3, 3, 3 and 1, and they add up to 10.

So Harbor, Recipes and Proverbs each get 3 out of 10, which is 30% of each batch. Arithmetic gets 1 out of 10, which is 10%.

### From shares to epochs

One full pass through a domain's training sentences is called an epoch. The lab divides the pairs it drew from a domain by the pairs that domain holds. The result, the domain's **effective epochs**, tells you how many times the budget read that domain.

The domains have very different sizes, so equal shares do not give equal epochs. Harbor's six training sentences hold 378 pairs, and Recipes' six hold 292. Proverbs' seven hold 195, and Arithmetic's 120 hold 3,417.

So under General-heavy, a 30% share reads Proverbs 14.72 times, but a 10% share reads Arithmetic only 0.28 times.

Arithmetic is 80% of all the training text, but General-heavy gives it one slot in ten. Giving a domain a bigger share of the budget than its share of the text is called **upsampling**. So this setting upsamples the three small General domains.

### The schedule and the late phase

The learning rate is the size of each nudge to the weights. This lab changes it during the run, with a plan called **warmup-stable-decay**.

The rate climbs from almost zero over the first 5% of steps. Then it stays at 3 for most of the run. Over the last 20%, starting at step 480, it shrinks to zero.

That last shrinking stretch is called **annealing**, and the **Late phase** control can change the mix right there:

- **Same mix** keeps the same shares all the way to the end of the run.
- **Target mix** makes half of each late batch Arithmetic and gives the other half to General.
- **Target only** makes each late batch Arithmetic, so General is not read after step 480.

Target mix has the shape of mid-training, and a plan that changes the mix over time is a **curriculum**. One switch near the end is the simplest curriculum there is.

### A study plan for a test

Here is a made-up picture of the whole idea. Picture a student with 600 short practice sessions before a math test. They also have four subjects to keep up, and the total time is fixed.

In the last few days, they spend half of each session on math. They still review the other subjects, and that is mid-training.

If they dropped all but math, the other subjects would slip, and that is called **forgetting**.

The picture stops matching in a few places. The model does not choose its plan, because each batch is drawn at random from the shares you set. It also does not understand math at all, as the Answer probe will show. For the model, studying math only means reading more math sentences.

## Why it’s here

### The data you want most is scarce

The text a team wants most is a small part of all it can collect. That includes math, code and carefully checked writing. The training budget is far bigger than that text.

So a mix that gives this text a big share must read it more than once. Training with more budget than new text is called **data-constrained training**.

A team led by Niklas Muennighoff studied this problem. Reading the same data up to 4 times was almost as good as new data. With much more repeating, extra training stopped adding anything. The Repeated data card asks the tiny model the same question.

### Why the end of the run matters

Each batch nudges the same table of weights, so the order of the data changes where they end up. The last batches have the final say.

The shrinking learning rate also matters, since it decides how far those last nudges can move the weights.

Teams use this on purpose. The team behind a model called MiniCPM came up with the warmup-stable-decay plan. They mixed their best data into the decay. It worked better than adding the same data only during fine-tuning, the training step that comes after.

Llama 3's team tried a final stretch on small amounts of top-quality code and math. Their 8-billion-weight model did 24.0% better on a math test called GSM8k. It did 6.4% better on a harder test called MATH. For their biggest model, the gain was close to nothing.

OLMo 2's 7-billion-weight model scored 24.1 on GSM8K before mid-training and 67.5 after it.

The warmup-stable-decay plan also makes this cheap to try. Its long middle stretch does not depend on how long the run will be. So a team can save the model just before the decay. Then they can run a few different endings from that one point.

### Mid-training or fine-tuning

What if the late phase drops the General text? Then training only scores the model on math text, and nothing holds the other domains in place.

So the shared weights drift toward math, and the General score gets worse. Keeping some General text in the late mix is how mid-training limits that forgetting.

Training on the target text alone is close to fine-tuning, which the next lab studies. An older study, led by Suchin Gururangan, tried a second round of training on text from one field. It made the model better at tasks in that field. Target only is that idea, placed at the end of a run. A team led by Adam Ibrahim found that new data hurts the model on older data.

They also tried mixing older data back in, and they raised and lowered the learning rate again. That did as well as training from scratch.

## How to play with it

### Step 1: set the mixture

Under **Mixture preset**, press **Uniform**. It sets all four weights to 1, so each domain gets a quarter of each batch.

The Epochs column reads 6.16 for Harbor and 8.34 for Recipes. It reads 12.48 for Proverbs and 0.70 for Arithmetic. Equal weights did not give equal epochs.

The held-out scores read 2.197 for Harbor and 2.498 for Recipes. Proverbs reads 2.400 and Arithmetic reads 1.967. General, the average of the first three, reads 2.365.

Now press **General-heavy**, and Harbor rises to 7.42 epochs as its score improves to 2.097. Recipes rises to 10.21 epochs, and its score improves to 2.427.

Proverbs rises to 14.72 epochs, but its score moves from 2.400 to 2.408. After more than 12 passes over seven short sayings, extra passes buy nothing.

Arithmetic pays for it and gets worse, from 1.967 to 2.348, but General improves from 2.365 to 2.311. The budget is fixed, so each gain on this card is paid for somewhere else.

### Step 2: upweight the scarce domain

In this mix, Arithmetic is scarce in its share of the budget, but it is not short of text.

Keep the other weights at 3 and drag **Arithmetic weight** from 0 up to 8. On **Arithmetic weight sweep**, the Arithmetic score falls from 2.832 to 1.706. At the same time, General's score rises from 2.320 to 2.515.

The **Gain per extra epoch** column shows what each extra pass through Arithmetic bought, in nats per epoch. It falls from 1.75 between weights 0 and 1, to 1.04 between weights 1 and 2. It ends at 0.25 between weights 7 and 8.

Even at weight 8, Arithmetic gets only 1.32 epochs. So this shrinking gain is the learning slowing down. It is not the cost of reading the same text again.

At weight 0, the model never reads a math fact. Yet Arithmetic scores 2.832, well below 3.401. Letter habits from the other domains carry over, like where spaces go.

### Step 3: repeat or find more

On **Repeated data**, keep **Budget** at 8,000 pairs and lower **Unique sentences** from 120. The bars keep the budget fixed and halve the number of sentences each time:

- 120 sentences are each read 2.3 times, and the held-out score is 1.388.
- 60 sentences are each read 4.7 times, and the held-out score is 1.400.
- 30 sentences are each read 9.4 times, and the held-out score is 1.458.
- 15 sentences are each read 18.8 times, and the held-out score is 1.577.
- 8 sentences are each read 34.6 times, and the held-out score is 1.731.
- 4 sentences are each read 70.8 times, and the held-out score is 1.961.
- 2 sentences are each read 135.6 times, and the held-out score is 2.542.

**Try this:** Set **Unique sentences** to 2 and watch the dashed line drop and then turn back up. Meanwhile, the solid line for all 120 sentences levels off at its lowest.

**Lowest along the run** reads 2.217 at 27.1 epochs. So each pass after that point made the score worse.

### Step 4: anneal on the target mix

Set **Late phase** to **Target mix**, so half of each batch is Arithmetic from step 480. The table now compares three runs:

- **Switched at decay**, this run, scores 1.911 on Arithmetic and 2.398 on General.
- Its Forgetting reads +0.060, and it read 1,721 Arithmetic pairs.
- **Spread evenly** reads the same amount of each domain on average, in one fixed mix.
- It scores 2.110 on Arithmetic and 2.339 on General, with Forgetting at −0.027 after 1,707 pairs.
- **No switch**, the default run, scores 2.348 on Arithmetic and 2.311 on General, with Forgetting at −0.027.

The first two runs read almost the same amount of math. So the gap between them comes from timing alone. In the last 120 steps of the switched run, Arithmetic falls from 2.356 to 1.911.

### Step 5: mid-train or fine-tune?

Now switch **Late phase** to **Target only**, so each late batch is Arithmetic. Switched at decay reads 1.645 on Arithmetic and 2.691 on General.

Forgetting climbs to +0.353, compared with +0.060 for Target mix. Spread evenly reads 1.935 and 2.375.

So dropping General from the late phase bought 0.266 more on Arithmetic but cost 0.293 on General. Keeping some General text in the late mix is what kept that second number small.

## What to notice

- The budget is fixed, so on **Data mixture** each score that improves is paid for by another.
- Equal weights give unequal epochs, so the smallest domain is the first to run out of new text.
- More budget for one domain buys less each time, as Gain per extra epoch falls from 1.75 to 0.25.
- At 8,000 pairs, repeating is nearly free at first: **Versus 120 unique** reads +0.011 for 60 sentences.
- Then it gets costly, since **Versus 120 unique** reads +0.069 for 30 sentences.
- With very little text, extra passes hurt, as two sentences bottom out at 2.217 and end at 2.542.
- Timing matters even when the data does not change much.
- The switched and spread runs read 1,721 and 1,707 Arithmetic pairs, yet end 0.199 apart.
- Forgetting follows how much General text the late phase keeps.
- It reads −0.027 with no switch, +0.060 with Target mix, and +0.353 with Target only.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The domains are tiny: three are fixed texts of 8 to 10 sentences, and Arithmetic comes from three patterns. Their sizes decide when repeating starts, not their real value.

The model is tiny too, and it guesses each character from only the one before it. So to this model, two domains differ only in which letter tends to follow which.

Each run uses one seed, the number that fixes the random order of the batches. At the default mix, seeds 1 to 8 move the Arithmetic score by up to 0.07. They move General's score by about 0.01, so small gaps between runs may be luck.

Some choices are fixed in the code, and General is a plain average of three scores. The switch always comes at step 480, and Target mix is always half Arithmetic. The warmup is always 5% of the steps.

The learning-rate curve and the Arithmetic share curve come from the settings by formula, not from measuring.

### What the loss misses

On **Answer probe**, the default model gets 0 of 40 held-out math facts right. It gives the same answer, ten, to each question. And ten is never the answer to a held-out fact here.

The model only sees the space before the answer. So it cannot read the numbers in the question at all.

Now set **Arithmetic weight** to 0, and the Arithmetic score gets worse, rising to 2.832. Yet the probe now gets 3 of 40 right!

That is because the model answers one to each question, and three held-out answers happen to be one. The score improved by learning how number words are spelled, not by learning how to add.

**A common mix-up:** A lower score on math text does not mean the model got better at math. A per-domain score shows how well the model predicts that kind of text. A skill needs its own test, with real questions the model has never seen.

### Where the toy disagrees

Published results say the decay itself makes late, high-quality data count, but this toy does not show that.

Try Target mix with **Schedule** on **Constant rate**. Arithmetic ends even lower, at 1.785 instead of 1.911. But General ends worse, at 2.462 instead of 2.398. Forgetting doubles, from +0.060 to +0.127.

In this toy, most of the late gain comes from simply being last. Big steps at the end pull the weights toward the latest batches, but a shrinking rate holds them back. So here, the decay buys a way to keep more of General.

The decay also does little on its own in this toy. With Same mix, General improves by 0.027 over the last 20% of steps with the decay. Without the decay, it improves by 0.022. The MiniCPM team saw a steep drop once their decay started.

Repeating also hurts this model in a different way than it hurts large ones. This model cannot memorize a sentence, so repeating text hurts it by narrowing which letter pairs it sees.

Large models can memorize, and one study repeated 0.1% of the training data 100 times. A model with 800 million weights then did as badly as one half its size.

### In real systems

A lower score on math text does not make a better mathematician. That is why OLMo 2's team reports GSM8K, a test of math word problems the model never trained on.

Real mixtures cover dozens of sources and trillions of tokens. Teams choose the shares from runs of small practice models. A share that wins at a small size may not win at full size.

Real forgetting is checked on whole sets of tests, not on one average score.

## Going deeper

### Choosing the weights

Guessing weights by hand is slow, so researchers built methods to choose them. One method, called DoReMi, trains a small helper model and reads the weights from it.

On a large public dataset called The Pile, it improved the score on each domain. That held even for domains it gave less weight to. It raised accuracy on a set of tests by 6.5 points. It also reached the old accuracy in 2.6 times fewer training steps.

Another approach fits a formula to small runs that predicts the score of a mix before anyone trains it. That is the **Arithmetic weight sweep** turned into a formula.

### Annealing as a cheap test

The ending matters so much that it also works as a cheap test for new data. Llama 3's team took a model halfway through training and annealed it on 40 billion tokens.

They gave 30% of the weight to a dataset they were testing, and 70% to their usual mix.

A team led by Cody Blakeney gave more weight to special data near the end of training. Their model had 7 billion weights, and it gained up to 6.90 points on a knowledge test called MMLU.

It also gained 8.26 points on the GSM8K math test and 6.17 points on a coding test called HumanEval. They found that a final stretch of 10 to 20 percent of training gave the best balance.

### More than one switch

Real curricula often have more than one switch. SmolLM2 is a small open model that trained in several stages. Each stage mixed web text with math, code and instruction text.

Its team changed each stage's mix by hand, based on how the stage before it went. OLMo 2's team ran mid-training a few times with the data in different orders, then averaged the results.

Llama 3's team also averaged several saved copies of the model from its final stretch.

### Toy versus real

- **Budget:** this lab reads 9,600 character pairs, but OLMo 2's 7-billion-weight model read about 4 trillion tokens.
- **Length of the ending:** the late phase here is 20% of the steps.
- The MiniCPM team found a decay over about 10% of the text was enough.
- OLMo 2's mid-training used 5 to 10 percent of the computing work.
- **Mixture:** General-heavy is 30, 30, 30 and 10 percent of four tiny domains.
- Llama 3's final mix was about 50, 25, 17 and 8 percent of trillions of tokens.
- **Testing:** this lab reads per-domain scores and one probe. Real mid-training is judged on tests of new problems.
