## The big picture

A language model learns from a huge pile of text that comes from many different sources, such as web
pages, computer code and math problems. Because the total amount of reading is fixed before training
starts, the team has to decide how much of each source the model will read, and that plan is called a
**data mixture**.

Near the end of training, many teams change the plan on purpose. They give the model much more of the
text they care about most, such as math or code, while still mixing in ordinary text so that it keeps
what it already learned. That late change is called **mid-training**, and this module is about why it
works and what it costs.

### Where you have met it

Every chatbot you have used was trained on a mixture, and a few teams publish theirs. Meta's report on
its Llama 3 models says that by the end, the training text was roughly half general knowledge, a
quarter math and reasoning, 17% computer code and 8% other languages. In the last 40 million tokens of
that training, the learning rate shrank to zero while the mix gave extra weight to the very best
sources.

OLMo 2 is an open model whose team trained it in two stages and calls the shorter second stage
mid-training. That stage used 5 to 10 percent of the total computing work, and its text leaned toward
carefully filtered writing and math.

### Why it matters

The amount of reading is fixed, so giving more of it to one source always means giving less to the
others. The text a team wants most is often the text it has least of, which means that reading more of
it usually means reading the same pages again. The order matters as well, because the last pages a
model reads have a big say in where its learning ends up.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain each of these in your own words:

- how a mixture works as shares of a fixed budget, and how each share turns into the number of times
  the model reads that source;
- why reading scarce text again helps at first, then stops helping, and with very little text starts
  to make things worse;
- why teams switch to a mixture that is heavy in the text they care about while training slows down at
  the very end;
- how mid-training on a mix differs from training on the target text alone, judged by what each one
  costs the other sources;
- what a score on one source's text can tell you about a skill, and what it cannot tell you.

### What the lab lets you do

Every score in this lab comes from real training that runs live as you move the controls, and five
cards each show one part of the story:

- **Data mixture** splits one fixed amount of training among four sources and scores each source on
  sentences it never trained on.
- **Arithmetic weight sweep** reruns that training nine times, giving the math source a bigger share
  each time.
- **Answer probe** checks whether the trained model can actually answer the math questions it was
  scored on.
- **Repeated data** trains on math alone and lets you choose how many different sentences the budget is
  spread over.
- **Late phase and annealing** changes the mixture for the last fifth of training and compares that
  with two other runs.

Three of the four sources are short paragraphs used throughout this course, while the fourth,
Arithmetic, is made up from three sentence patterns, such as “four plus eight is twelve.” The chart of
the learning rate across the run is drawn from a formula, so it is the only picture here that is not
measured.

## What it is

### The model and how it is scored

The model is the tiny one from *Train one from scratch*, which reads one character and guesses the next
one by looking it up in a table of 900 numbers called **weights**. Training nudges those weights a
little after every small batch of examples so that later guesses improve. Each example is a pair of
characters, one character and the one that follows it, and every batch in this lab holds 16 pairs.

The lab scores the model by its **loss**, the average penalty for its guesses, which is measured in
units called nats per character, and lower is better. A table that knows nothing gives every character
the same chance and scores 3.401, so that is the number every run has to beat.

The most important rule of scoring is that the test sentences are never used for training. Each source
keeps the last quarter of its sentences, rounded to whole sentences, out of training, and every score in
the lab is measured on those held-out sentences.

### Four domains

A source of text with its own style is called a **domain**, and this lab has four of them. Harbor has
eight sentences of weather notes with 2 held out, and Recipes has eight cooking steps with 2 held out.
Proverbs has ten short sayings with 3 held out, and Arithmetic has 160 made-up math facts with 40 held
out.

The tiny model has no digit symbols, only letters, spaces and full stops, so every number in a math fact
is spelled out in words. The first three domains together are called General. A score on one domain's
held-out sentences is a **per-domain held-out loss**, and General's score is the average of the first
three, with each one counted once.

### Shares of one budget

Every run on the mixture cards takes 600 steps of 16 pairs, which comes to 9,600 pairs in total. That
fixed total is the **token budget**, since a token here is a single character.

You set the split with four sliders, one **mixture weight** per domain, and each weight runs from 0 to
8. Only the ratio between the weights counts. The lab divides each weight by the sum of all four to get
that domain's share, and each slot in a batch then picks a domain with a chance equal to its share.

Here is a worked example with the default setting, which is called General-heavy. Its weights are 3, 3,
3 and 1, which add up to 10. So Harbor, Recipes and Proverbs each get 3 out of 10, or 30% of every batch,
while Arithmetic gets 1 out of 10, or 10%.

### From shares to epochs

One full pass through a domain's training sentences is called an epoch. The lab divides the pairs it
drew from a domain by the pairs that domain holds. The result is the domain's **effective epochs**,
which tells you how many times the budget read that domain over.

The domains are very different sizes, which is why equal shares do not give equal epochs. Harbor's six
training sentences hold 378 pairs, Recipes' six hold 292, Proverbs' seven hold 195, and Arithmetic's 120
hold 3,417. So under General-heavy, a 30% share reads Proverbs 14.72 times, while a 10% share reads
Arithmetic only 0.28 times.

Arithmetic is 80% of all the training text, but General-heavy gives it only one slot in ten. Giving a
domain a bigger share of the budget than its share of the text is called **upsampling**, so this setting
upsamples the three small General domains and shrinks Arithmetic's share.

### The schedule and the late phase

The learning rate is the size of each nudge to the weights, and this lab changes it during the run with
a plan called **warmup-stable-decay**. The rate climbs from almost zero over the first 5% of steps and
stays at 3 for most of the run. Over the last 20%, starting at step 480, it shrinks steadily to zero.

That final shrinking stretch is called **annealing**, and the **Late phase** control can change the
mixture right where it starts:

- **Same mix** keeps the same shares all the way to the end of the run.
- **Target mix** makes half of every late batch Arithmetic and gives the other half to the three General
  domains in their usual proportions, which is the shape of mid-training.
- **Target only** makes every late batch Arithmetic, so the General domains are not read at all after
  step 480.

An order for presenting data, instead of one fixed mix for the whole run, is called a **curriculum**,
and a single switch near the end is the simplest curriculum there is.

### A study plan for a test

Here is a made-up picture of the whole idea. Picture a student with 600 short practice sessions before a
math test and four subjects to keep up, where the total time is fixed, so more math means less of
everything else.

In the last few days, the student spends half of each session on math while still reviewing the other
subjects, and that is mid-training. If they dropped everything except math, the other subjects would
start to slip, and that slipping is called **forgetting**.

The picture stops matching in a few places. The model does not choose its plan, because every batch is
drawn at random from the shares you set. It also does not understand math at all, as the Answer probe
will show, so “studying math” only means reading more math sentences.

## Why it’s here

### The data you want most is scarce

The text a team wants most, such as math, code and carefully checked writing, is a small part of
everything it can collect, while the training budget is far bigger. So a mixture that gives that text a
big share has to read it more than once, and training with more budget than new text is called
**data-constrained training**.

A research team led by Niklas Muennighoff studied exactly this problem. They found that reading the same
data up to 4 times was almost as good as reading new data, but with much more repeating, extra training
eventually stopped adding anything. The Repeated data card asks the tiny model the same question.

### Why the end of the run matters

Every batch nudges the same table of weights, so the order of the data changes where those weights end
up, and the last batches have the final say. A shrinking learning rate also decides how far those last
nudges are able to move things.

Teams use this on purpose. The team behind a model called MiniCPM, who introduced the
warmup-stable-decay plan, mixed their best data into the decay. That worked better than adding the same
data only during fine-tuning, the training step that comes afterward. Llama 3's team found that a final
stretch on small amounts of top-quality code and math lifted its 8-billion-weight model by 24.0% on a
math test called GSM8k.

The same stretch lifted that model by 6.4% on a harder test called MATH, although for their largest
model the gain was close to nothing. For OLMo 2's 7-billion-weight model, the GSM8K score rose from 24.1
before mid-training to 67.5 after it.

The warmup-stable-decay plan also makes this cheap to try, because the long middle stretch does not
depend on how long the run will be. A team can save the model just before the decay and run several
different endings from that one point.

### Mid-training or fine-tuning

If the late phase drops the General text completely, training only scores the model on math text. Then
nothing holds the other domains in place, so the shared weights drift toward math and the General score
gets worse. Keeping some General text in the late mix is how mid-training limits that forgetting.

Training on the target text alone is close to fine-tuning, which the next lab studies. An older
study led by Suchin Gururangan found that a second round of training on text from one field improves
the model's tasks in that field. Target only is that same idea, placed at the end of a run. A research team
led by Adam Ibrahim found that new data hurts performance on earlier data. They also found that mixing
earlier data back in, while raising and lowering the learning rate again, did as well as training from
scratch.

## How to play with it

### Step 1: set the mixture

Under **Mixture preset**, press **Uniform**, which sets all four weights to 1 so each domain gets a
quarter of every batch. The Epochs column reads 6.16 for Harbor, 8.34 for Recipes, 12.48 for Proverbs and
0.70 for Arithmetic, so equal weights did not give equal epochs.

The held-out scores read 2.197 for Harbor, 2.498 for Recipes, 2.400 for Proverbs and 1.967 for
Arithmetic, and General, the average of the first three, reads 2.365.

Now press **General-heavy**. Harbor rises to 7.42 epochs and its score improves to 2.097, while Recipes
rises to 10.21 epochs and improves to 2.427. Proverbs rises to 14.72 epochs, but its score moves from
2.400 to 2.408, because after more than 12 passes over seven short sayings, extra passes buy nothing.

Arithmetic pays for the change and gets worse, going from 1.967 to 2.348, while General improves from
2.365 to 2.311. The budget is fixed, so every gain on this card is paid for somewhere else.

### Step 2: upweight the scarce domain

In this mix, Arithmetic is scarce in its share of the budget, not in its amount of text. Keep the other
weights at 3 and drag **Arithmetic weight** from 0 up to 8. On **Arithmetic weight sweep**, Arithmetic's
held-out score falls from 2.832 to 1.706, while General's rises from 2.320 to 2.515.

The **Gain per extra epoch** column shows how much each extra pass through Arithmetic bought, in nats
per epoch. It falls from 1.75 between weights 0 and 1 to 1.04 between weights 1 and 2, and down to 0.25
between weights 7 and 8.

Even at weight 8, Arithmetic gets only 1.32 epochs, so this shrinking gain is the learning slowing down,
not the cost of repeated reading. At weight 0 the model never reads a math fact, yet Arithmetic scores
2.832, well below 3.401, because letter habits learned from the other domains, such as where spaces go,
carry over.

### Step 3: repeat or find more

On **Repeated data**, keep **Budget** at 8,000 pairs and lower **Unique sentences** from 120. The bars
keep the budget fixed and halve the number of different sentences each time:

- 120 different sentences are each read 2.3 times, and the held-out score is 1.388.
- 60 different sentences are each read 4.7 times, and the held-out score is 1.400.
- 30 different sentences are each read 9.4 times, and the held-out score is 1.458.
- 15 different sentences are each read 18.8 times, and the held-out score is 1.577.
- 8 different sentences are each read 34.6 times, and the held-out score is 1.731.
- 4 different sentences are each read 70.8 times, and the held-out score is 1.961.
- 2 different sentences are each read 135.6 times, and the held-out score is 2.542.

**Try this:** Set **Unique sentences** to 2 and watch the dashed line drop and then turn back up, while
the solid line for all 120 sentences levels off at its lowest. **Lowest along the run** reads 2.217 at 27.1 epochs,
which means every pass after that point made the score worse.

### Step 4: anneal on the target mix

Set **Late phase** to **Target mix**, so that from step 480, half of every batch is Arithmetic. The
table now compares three runs:

- **Switched at decay**, this run, scores 1.911 on Arithmetic and 2.398 on General, with Forgetting at
  +0.060 after reading 1,721 Arithmetic pairs.
- **Spread evenly** reads the same amount of each domain on average, but in one fixed mix for the whole
  run. It scores 2.110 on Arithmetic and 2.339 on General, with Forgetting at −0.027 after 1,707 pairs.
- **No switch** is the default run, which scores 2.348 on Arithmetic and 2.311 on General, with
  Forgetting at −0.027.

The first two runs read almost the same amount of math, so the gap between them comes from timing alone.
In the last 120 steps of the switched run, Arithmetic falls from 2.356 to 1.911.

### Step 5: mid-train or fine-tune?

Now switch **Late phase** to **Target only**, so that every late batch is Arithmetic. Switched at decay
reads 1.645 on Arithmetic and 2.691 on General, and Forgetting climbs to +0.353, compared with +0.060
for Target mix. Spread evenly reads 1.935 and 2.375.

So dropping General from the late phase bought 0.266 more on Arithmetic and cost 0.293 on General.
Keeping some General text in the late mix is what kept that second number small.

## What to notice

- The budget is fixed, so on **Data mixture** every score that improves is paid for by a score that
  gets worse somewhere else.
- Equal weights give unequal epochs, which is why the smallest domain is the first to run out of new
  text.
- More budget for one domain buys less each time, as the Gain per extra epoch column falls from 1.75 to
  0.25.
- Repeating text is nearly free at first and costly later, since at 8,000 pairs, **Versus 120 unique**
  reads +0.011 for 60 sentences and +0.069 for 30.
- With very little text, extra passes make the score worse, as two sentences bottom out at 2.217 and end
  at 2.542.
- Timing matters even when the data does not change, because the switched and evenly spread runs read
  1,721 and 1,707 Arithmetic pairs yet end 0.199 apart on Arithmetic.
- Forgetting follows how much General text the late phase keeps, reading −0.027 with no switch, +0.060
  with Target mix, and +0.353 with Target only.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

The domains are tiny, since three are fixed paragraphs of 8 to 10 sentences and Arithmetic is built
from three sentence patterns. Their sizes, rather than their real value, decide when repeating starts.

The model is tiny too, and it guesses each character from only the one before it, so to this model two
domains differ only in which letter tends to follow which.

Every run uses one seed, the number that fixes the random order of the batches. At the default mixture,
trying seeds 1 to 8 moves Arithmetic's score by up to 0.07 and General's by about 0.01, so small gaps
between runs may be luck.

Several choices are fixed in the code. General is a plain average of three scores, the switch always
comes at step 480, Target mix is always half Arithmetic, and the warmup is always 5% of the steps. The
learning-rate curve and the Arithmetic share curve are drawn from the settings by formula, not measured.

### What the loss misses

On **Answer probe**, the default model gets 0 of 40 held-out math facts right. It gives the same answer,
ten, to every question, and ten is never the answer to a held-out fact here. The model only sees
the space before the answer, so it cannot read the numbers in the question at all.

Set **Arithmetic weight** to 0 and Arithmetic's score gets worse, rising to 2.832, yet the probe now gets
3 of 40 right. That is because the model answers one to everything, and three held-out answers happen to
be one. The score improved by learning how number words are spelled, not by learning how to add.

**A common mix-up:** A lower score on math text does not mean the model got better at math. A
per-domain score measures how well the model predicts that kind of text, while a skill needs its own
test with real questions the model has never seen.

### Where the toy disagrees

Published results credit the decay itself with making late, high-quality data count, but this toy does
not show that. With Target mix and **Schedule** on **Constant rate**, Arithmetic ends even lower, at
1.785 instead of 1.911, but General ends worse, at 2.462 instead of 2.398, and Forgetting doubles from
+0.060 to +0.127.

In this toy, most of the late gain comes from simply being last. Full-size steps at the end pull the
weights furthest toward the latest batches, and the shrinking rate holds them back, so what the decay
buys here is keeping more of General.

The decay also does little on its own in this toy. With Same mix, General improves by 0.027 over the
last 20% of steps with the decay and by 0.022 without it, while the MiniCPM team reports a steep drop
once their decay starts.

Repeating also hurts this model differently from large ones. This model cannot memorize a sentence, so
repeating text hurts it by narrowing which letter pairs it sees. Large models can memorize, and one study
found that repeating 0.1% of the training data 100 times made a model with 800 million weights perform
like one half its size.

### In real systems

A lower score on math text does not make a better mathematician, which is why OLMo 2's team reports
GSM8K, a test of math word problems the model never trained on. Real mixtures cover dozens of sources
and trillions of tokens, and teams choose the shares from runs of small practice models. A share that
wins at small scale may not win at full size, and forgetting is checked on whole sets of tests rather
than on one average score.

## Going deeper

### Choosing the weights

Guessing weights by hand is slow, so researchers have built methods that choose them. One method, called
DoReMi, trains a small helper model and reads the weights from it. On a large public dataset called The
Pile, it improved scores on every domain, even the ones it gave less weight to.

DoReMi also raised accuracy on a set of tests by 6.5 points and reached the old accuracy in 2.6 times
fewer training steps. Another approach fits a formula to small runs that predicts the score of a mixture
before anyone trains it, which is the **Arithmetic weight sweep** turned into a formula.

### Annealing as a cheap test

Because the ending matters so much, it also works as a cheap way to test new data. Llama 3's team took a
model halfway through training and annealed it on 40 billion tokens, giving 30% of the weight to a
dataset they were testing and 70% to their usual mix.

A team led by Cody Blakeney gave more weight to special data near the end of training a model with 7
billion weights. It gained up to 6.90 points on a general knowledge test called MMLU, 8.26 on the GSM8K
math test, and 6.17 on a coding test called HumanEval. They found that a final stretch of 10 to 20
percent of training gave the best balance against general skill.

### More than one switch

Real curricula often have more than one switch. SmolLM2, a small open model, trained in several stages
that mixed web text with math, code and instruction text. Its team adjusted each stage's mix by hand from
how the stage before it went.

OLMo 2's team ran mid-training several times with the data in different orders and averaged the results,
while Llama 3's team averaged several saved copies of the model from its final stretch.

The other labs in the course connect here. *Hyperparameters & schedules* covers learning-rate plans,

### Toy versus real

- **Budget:** this lab reads 9,600 character pairs, while OLMo 2's 7-billion-weight model read about 4
  trillion tokens.
- **Length of the ending:** the late phase here is 20% of the steps. The MiniCPM team found a decay over
  about 10% of the text was enough, and OLMo 2's mid-training used 5 to 10 percent of the work.
- **Mixture:** General-heavy is 30, 30, 30 and 10 percent of four tiny domains, while Llama 3's final
  mix was roughly 50, 25, 17 and 8 percent of trillions of tokens.
- **Testing:** this lab reads per-domain scores and one probe, while real mid-training is judged on tests
  of problems the model never saw.
