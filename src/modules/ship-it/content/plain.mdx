## The big picture

This module is the last stop on the training path, where everything the earlier labs taught comes
together in one place. Each earlier lab studied one step on its own, such as teaching a model a new
job or squeezing it into a smaller file. Here you carry one small model through all of those steps
in a row. At the end you make a single decision about it: is it ready to go out, or should it wait?
Releasing a finished model so that people can use it is called **shipping** it, which is where the
module gets its name.

The steps always come in the same order, and each one changes the model in its own way. First the
model learns to write one kind of text, which becomes its old job, and next it learns a second kind
of text, its new job. Then it is tuned toward the kind of answers people prefer, and after that it
is squeezed into a smaller form. Last of all, you check whether a machine has enough memory to run
it for many people at the same time.

The catch is that any step can have a price, and some of those prices hide in places where nobody is
looking. A step studied on its own can look almost free, but when you chain the steps together,
their costs add up. This lab turns each of those costs into a number that you can read and compare.

### Where you have met it

This app can download a program called **llama.cpp** and use it to run a small model on your own
computer as its guide. In this lab you will pick a format for storing the model’s numbers, with
names such as `Q4_K_M`. These are formats that llama.cpp uses to fit a model into a smaller file.

Every other step in this lab also has a full-size version that people use on real models, and the
last section of this lesson names those tools. Real releases make the same ship-or-wait decision
that you make here, but with much bigger tests and more care afterwards. They test with thousands of
questions across many topics, give the new model to a small share of users first, and keep watching
the numbers after launch.

### Why it matters

Without a careful check at the end, a change that helps a model with its new job can quietly hurt it
at its old one. Nobody notices, because nobody tested the old job again. The starting settings in
this lab make exactly that kind of change, and here it is the gate’s check on the old job that
catches it.

A model can also pass every quality test and still run out of memory on its first busy day. So a
release decision has to check both quality and memory, and it has to check the version that will
actually go out.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- how one model, once it has had its first training, moves through four more stages, called
  adapting, aligning, compressing and serving, and what each of those stages costs it;
- how to decide on a release using scores on text the model never trained on, for both its new job
  and an old job you meant to keep;
- why a quiz with only six questions cannot settle a close call between two versions of a model;
- why the exact version you would release has to fit in a machine’s memory, with as many people
  using it as you expect, before you can call it ready.

### What the lab shows

The lab really trains a tiny model while you watch, and it trains the whole thing again from the
start whenever you move a setting. Every quality score you see is measured on that tiny model. Its
texts, its pairs of better and worse answers, and its quiz questions are fixed, so the same settings always give
exactly the same numbers. The lab lays all of this out across five cards:

- **Training recipe** holds the settings for the first three stages, which train the model and then
  adapt and tune it.
- **Stage by stage** shows the model’s scores after each stage, so you can see what every step did
  to it.
- **Eval gate** runs two pass-or-fail checks on the finished model, with pass marks that you can
  move yourself.
- **Compress and serve** shrinks the model and then works out whether it fits in a machine’s memory.
- **Release report** gives the final answer, ship or hold, along with the reasons behind it.

The memory check works differently from everything else, because it is arithmetic about an imagined
model of about 8 billion numbers. That imagined model runs on one imagined machine, so none of the
memory figures come from real hardware.

## What it is

### A picture to hold on to

Picture a small studio getting ready to release an update to a game that people already play. The
update adds a new level, gets polished using players’ votes on which of two versions they like
better, and is shrunk so the download takes less space. Before it goes out, the team works through a
checklist that was written in advance. The new level has to be clearly better, the old levels must
still play as well as before, and the servers must cope with the crowd on launch day. If any one
item fails, the update waits, however good the rest of it looks.

This lab follows the same plan with a model, and each part of the update has a match:

- the old levels are the model’s first job, which is writing harbor weather notes;
- the new level is the second job you want to add, which is writing recipe steps;
- the polish is the preference step, and the smaller download is the compression step;
- launch day is serving many people at once, and the checklist is the release gate.

The picture stops matching in one important way, because in a game, programmers change the code on
purpose and can read exactly what they changed. Training nudges many of a model’s numbers at once,
and nobody chooses any single number, so the only way to find out what got worse is to measure it. A
broken level is also usually plainly broken, while a model slips by degrees, which is why the gate
uses scores and pass marks instead of a quick play-through.

### The model: a table of 900 numbers

The model is the same kind of tiny model as in the Train one from scratch lab, and its only job is
to guess the next **character**. Here a character means a letter, a space or a full stop, and the
model works with 30 of them in all. Those are the 26 letters, the space, the full stop and two
special marks that never appear in its text.

For each character, the model keeps a row of 30 scores, one for every character that might come
next, and turns that row into chances that add up to 100%. That gives 30 rows of 30 scores, or 900
numbers in all. The numbers a model learns are called its **weights**, so this is the 900-weight
table that the lab keeps mentioning.

Because each row belongs to one character, the model only ever looks at the single character just
before the one it is guessing. The lab calls this tiny model the **toy**, which is why some of its
readouts begin with the word Toy.

After its first training, for example, the row for t makes h by far the likeliest next character,
because the word “the” turns up in every sentence of both texts. That one row serves both texts at
once, which will matter a great deal later on.

### Two jobs: harbor text and recipe text

The lab uses two short texts with eight sentences each, and the model learns them one after the
other. The first is harbor weather notes, such as “the morning fog settles over the harbor and the
air feels cool.” The second is recipe steps, such as “warm the pan over medium heat and add a spoon
of oil.” Because the model learns harbor text first, that is its old job, and recipe text is the new
job you want to add.

The last two sentences of each text are kept away from every stage of training, and text kept back
like this is called **held-out** text. For recipes, the held-out text is “drain the pasta and toss
it with the warm sauce. finish the dish with fresh basil and a little cheese.” The lab writes all of
its text in lowercase, which is why “finish” has no capital letter. Testing on sentences the model
trained on would reward it for repeating them, while held-out text shows how it does on writing it
never trained on.

### Scoring the model: held-out loss

To score the model, the lab walks through a held-out sentence one character at a time and looks at
each of the model’s guesses. At each step it checks the chance the model gave to the character that
really came next. A big chance means little surprise and a small chance means a lot, and the average
surprise over every character is the model’s **loss**. So a lower loss is better, because it means
the model was less surprised by real text.

The unit of surprise is called the **nat**, which is why the lab reports loss in nats per character.
You will not need the formula, only one rule of thumb: every time the chance given to the right
character halves, the surprise goes up by about 0.69 nats. That gives a few landmarks to read the
numbers by:

- a model that was always certain and always right would score exactly 0;
- a model that gave the right character a 1-in-8 chance every time would score about 2.08. Going
  from a sure thing to 1 in 8 takes three halvings, and 3 × 0.69 is about 2.08;
- a blind guess, which gives all 30 characters the same 1-in-30 chance, scores 3.401.

Small changes in loss read almost like percentages. When the loss drops by 0.03, the model is
giving the right characters roughly 3% more chance on average. When it rises by 0.10, it is giving
them roughly 10% less.

At the start, the base model scores 2.098 on held-out harbor text, which is about as surprised as a
steady 1-in-8 guess. On held-out recipe text it scores 2.540, nearer a 1-in-13 guess, because it has
never trained on a single recipe. Loss measured on held-out text like this is called **held-out
loss**, and both of the gate’s quality checks run on it.

### The five stages

The lab runs one model through five stages. Stage 1 builds the starting model, and the four stages
after it are the ones whose costs this lesson tracks. The **Stage by stage** table has a row for the
model after each of the first four stages. The fifth, serving, is worked out on the **Compress and
serve** card instead. The **Training recipe** card holds the settings for the first three
stages, and the **Compress and serve** card holds the rest.

#### Stage 1: pretrain

The empty table trains on the six harbor training sentences, and the result is the row called **Base
model**. **Base training epochs** sets how many times it reads through those sentences, where one
full pass through them is called an **epoch**.

#### Stage 2: adapt

The model then learns recipe text through a small add-on called an **adapter**. It uses a method
called **LoRA**, short for low-rank adaptation, which the LoRA & adapters lab covers. Rather than
retrain all 900 numbers freely, the adapter builds a correction to the table out of a few simple
patterns, which keeps the add-on small.

Each pattern is one list of 30 numbers, one for each row, and another list of 30, one for each
column. Every cell of the pattern is the number for its row times the number for its column.
**LoRA rank** sets how many patterns get added together, from 1 to 12. At rank 4 the adapter learns
4 × 60 = 240 numbers instead of 900, so it can only change the table in a limited, regular way. In
a big model, that saving is what makes adapters cheap to train and store.

**Adapter epochs** sets how long the adapter trains, while **Replay sentences** can mix some harbor
sentences back in, which the part called Replay, under Why it’s here, explains.

When training ends, the correction is added into the table for good, which makes it a **merged
adapter** and gives the row called **After adapting**. Merging means the shipped model is still one
table and costs nothing extra to run, but the adapter can no longer be lifted off or swapped for
another.

#### Stage 3: align

Next the model is tuned toward answers people prefer, using a method called **DPO**, which is short
for direct preference optimization. DPO works from three pairs, and each pair gives two endings for
the same opening, one preferred and one rejected. For the opening “stir the”, for example, the
preferred ending is “onions until they turn soft.” and the rejected one is “onions onions onions
onions.”

DPO nudges the table so that each preferred ending gains on its rejected partner, which means the
gap between how likely the two endings are grows wider. To see how far each ending has moved, DPO
compares the model with a **frozen copy**, a saved copy of the model from just before this stage
that never changes. **DPO steps** sets how long this runs, with 0 switching it off, and the result
is the row called **After aligning**.

**DPO beta** sets how big a gap counts as good enough. Once training has run long enough, a higher
beta settles for a smaller gap and keeps the model nearer the frozen copy. A lower beta pushes the
pairs further apart and lets the model wander further, which the Preference optimization in
practice lab explores.

**Preference pairs ordered**, on the **Stage by stage** card, gives the share of pairs where the
preferred ending gained on the rejected one, compared with the frozen copy. **Reward margin** shows
how far ahead the preferred endings have pulled on average. DPO beta scales that readout too, so
compare it only between runs that use the same beta.

#### Stage 4: compress

The computer stores each weight as a string of **bits**, where a bit is a single 0 or 1, and 8 bits
make one **byte**. Storing each weight in fewer bits is called **quantization**, and the **Weight
format** control picks how many bits each weight gets.

With 16 bits a number can be stored very precisely, but 4 bits can only be set in 16 different ways.
So in a 4-bit format, every weight has to be rounded to the nearest value the format allows, which
loses a little detail. The formats run from `F16`, which uses 16 bits per weight, down to `Q4_0`,
and the lab starts at `Q4_K_M`. Apart from `F16`, each format uses roughly the number of bits after
its Q, plus a little extra for bookkeeping.

**Pruning** is a second kind of squeezing, which sets the smallest weights, the ones closest to
zero, to exactly zero. The **Prune** slider picks what share of all the weights to remove, anywhere
from 0% up to 90%. The hope is that a table full of zeros can be stored in less space or run faster,
on software and chips built to skip over the zeros. In this lab it does not make the file any
smaller, as Where it breaks explains, so here you only see what it costs.

The result of both steps is the row called **As shipped**, which is the version that would actually
go out. That finished version, after every stage has changed it, is called the **artifact**, and the
first line of the **Release report** describes it. At the start it reads
`Q4_K_M, not pruned, merged adapter of rank 4`, which names its format, its pruning and its adapter.

#### Stage 5: serve

The last stage asks whether the model fits on a machine at the load you expect, and here the lab
switches to an imagined model. A table of 900 numbers fits anywhere, so the card imagines a model of
about 8 billion weights instead, which the lab calls **8B-class**. It runs on one **accelerator**, a
chip built to run models quickly, which has 24 GiB of memory. A **GiB**, short for gibibyte, is a
little over a billion bytes, so 24 GiB is room for about 26 billion bytes.

A full-size model writes one small piece of text at a time, called a **token**, which is often a
short word or part of a longer one. For each token it saves notes about the text so far, so it does
not have to redo that work every time it writes a new token. A store of saved work like this is
called a cache. These notes are the **KV cache**, named after the two kinds of note it keeps, which
are known as keys and values.

Every conversation needs its own notes, and the lab calls each conversation it serves a **stream**.
So the cache grows with **Concurrent users**, the number of people served at once, and with
**Context length**, the number of tokens each conversation can hold. The weights, every stream’s
notes and 1 GiB of working space all have to fit inside the machine’s 24 GiB. Adding those three up
and checking the total against the machine’s memory is called the **serving budget**.

### The release gate

Once the stages are done, the **Eval gate** card runs two pass-or-fail checks, always on the **As
shipped** row and never on an earlier one:

- **Gains on the recipe text** passes only if recipe loss has fallen below the base model’s by at
  least **Required recipe-loss gain**, which starts at 0.030 nats.
- **Keeps the harbor text** passes only if harbor loss has not risen above the base model’s by more
  than **Allowed harbor-loss rise**, which starts at 0.100 nats.

The **Release report** then adds the serving budget as a third condition, so the verdict depends on
quality and memory together. Its badge reads `Verdict: ship` only when both checks pass and the
budget fits, and in every other case it reads `Verdict: hold`. A set of checks like this, with pass
marks fixed before the run and applied to the version you would actually release, is called a
**release gate**.

## Why it’s here

Chaining the steps together creates problems that no single lab could show, and each part of the
release gate is there to catch one of them.

### Getting better at one job can mean getting worse at another

Picture a friend on a soccer team who spends a whole month practising nothing but penalty kicks. Her
penalties get sharp, but at the first real match her passing has gone rusty, and nobody saw it
coming because nobody watched her pass all month.

The adapter in Stage 2 can do something similar, because with **Replay sentences** at `none` it
trains on recipe sentences only. Yet harbor text and recipe text lean on the very same table, since
both use letter pairs such as t followed by h. When training bends those shared numbers toward
recipes, the harbor guesses move with them, and at the starting settings harbor loss rises by 0.150
nats during adapting.

Losing an old skill when a model trains on new data alone is called **catastrophic forgetting**. The
name sounds dramatic, but what matters is whether the old skill got worse and by how much, and the
lab shows you that number.

The soccer story stops matching in one way, because her passing fades from lack of use. The model’s
harbor guesses, by contrast, are pushed away by the very same changes that teach it recipes.

### Replay: practising the old job alongside the new one

In the soccer story, the fix would be to mix a few passing drills into every practice session. The
model’s version is called **replay**, which mixes some of the old harbor sentences back into the
adapter’s training. Every change then has to keep working for harbor text as well as recipe text,
and **Replay sentences** sets how many of the six harbor training sentences join in.

In general, replay has a price, because one small update now has to serve two texts at once. The
push that training gives toward the new job is called the training signal. It is now shared with
the old job, so the update cannot bend as far toward the new one. In bigger jobs that cost is real, and
it is worth watching for.

In this lab, though, you will not see it. With four replayed sentences, recipe loss ends at 2.367,
lower than the 2.497 it reaches with no replay at all. One likely reason is that the adapter trains
for a set number of epochs, so adding harbor sentences also gives it more practice steps in total.
Here, replay is the cheapest defence against forgetting, and it leaves the recipe text better off.

### You only see what you measure: the control set

If the gate checked recipe text alone, the starting model would pass, because its recipe loss falls
by more than the bar. The damage to harbor text would go out with the release, and nobody would find
out until people started using it.

So the gate also reads a **control set**, which is held-out text from a job that the adapting was
not meant to change. Here the control set is the two held-out harbor sentences, and the check called
**Keeps the harbor text** reads them. In the game picture, this is the item on the checklist that
says to re-test the old levels before the update goes out.

### Test the version you ship, not the one you trained

Compression comes after training, and it changes the numbers, so a gate that read the **After
aligning** row would be grading a model that never goes out. With four replayed sentences and
**Prune** at 90%, for example, **After aligning** would pass both checks with room to spare, while
**As shipped** fails both. That is why the gate always reads the last row, the artifact itself, and
never the model as it left training.

### A six-question quiz cannot settle a close call

The lab also runs a short quiz, called a **benchmark**, with six questions for each of the two
texts. Each question gives the start of a sentence and two possible next words, one right and one
wrong, where the wrong one is still a normal English word. The model passes a question if it scores the right word higher than the wrong
one. One recipe question, for example, asks whether “season the soup with salt and a pinch of”
should go on with “pepper” or with “rain”. The **Recipe items** and **Harbor items** columns count
how many of the six questions the model passes at each stage.

Picture a friend who makes 1 of 6 free throws on Monday and 4 of 6 on Tuesday. She may really have
improved overnight, or Monday may have been an unlucky day, and six shots cannot tell you which.

The quiz has the same weakness, so the lab puts a range around each count, called a 95% interval. If
you built ranges this way for many different tests, about 95 in every 100 of them would contain the
model’s true pass rate. That true pass rate is the share it would get right on a huge number of
similar questions, which is what you really want to know. For 1 of 6 the range runs from 3% to 56%,
while for 4 of 6 it runs from 30% to 90%. Both ranges include everything from 30% to 56%, so they
overlap.

When two ranges overlap, the quiz cannot say whether the model really changed, so the lab calls the
difference **not decisive**. Held-out loss suffers much less from that problem, because it scores
every character, which comes to about a hundred in each held-out text. It can also move by a
thousandth of a nat at a time, instead of jumping a whole question. That is why both gate checks
read held-out loss, while the quiz appears beside them only as extra evidence. Held-out loss is
still a small test, though, since it reads only two sentences per text. A gap of a few hundredths
of a nat can be luck too, as Where it breaks explains.

### A model that does not fit is not ready

Passing on quality says nothing about memory, and in the game picture, a great update that crashes
the servers on launch day has still failed. The model’s weights take the same space however many
people use it, but every conversation adds its own notes, and longer conversations add more. A
release has to fit at the load you actually expect, which is why the **Release report** adds the
serving budget to the two quality checks.

### Pass marks have to come first

Picture a class where the pass mark is chosen after the tests are marked, and it keeps sliding down
until everyone passes. Everyone does pass, but the result now tells you nothing about who actually
learned the material.

A gate works the same way, because if you loosen the pass marks after seeing the results, any model
can pass. A real release gate writes its marks down before the run and then leaves them alone,
whatever the results turn out to be.

## How to play with it

The lab’s five steps walk you through one full release, and each part below matches one of them.
Begin at the starting settings, and read each card in turn before you move anything.

### 1. Run the pipeline

Read **Stage by stage** from the top row down, comparing the recipe and harbor columns at each step.
Going from **Base model** to **After adapting**, recipe loss improves from 2.540 to 2.461, which the
table marks `−0.079 better`. Harbor loss gets worse, from 2.098 to 2.248, marked `+0.150 worse`, and
that rise is catastrophic forgetting caught in the act.

The next row, **After aligning**, nudges both losses up a little, by 0.035 on recipe text and 0.042
on harbor text. **As shipped** then barely moves, because rounding the weights to `Q4_K_M`
costs almost nothing. Under the table, **Recipe loss, base to shipped** reads `−0.043` and **Harbor
loss, base to shipped** reads `+0.194`.

The box under those numbers lists every rise bigger than 0.02 nats from one stage to the next, and
each one is called a **regression**. A regression is any stage that made a loss more than 0.02 nats
worse than it was after the stage before, even if the model ends up better overall.

### 2. Read the gate

Open **Eval gate**, where the badge reads `Gate: fail` and the two checks sit underneath it. **Gains
on the recipe text** passes, because recipe loss fell by 0.043 against a bar of 0.030. **Keeps the
harbor text** fails, because the shipped model is 0.194 worse than the base on harbor text, while
0.100 is the most allowed, so the release is on hold.

Now read the benchmark line under the two checks, which gives the quiz results for the same
comparison. The recipe count went from 1 to 4 correct and the harbor count fell from 5 to 1, yet
both are marked “overlapping, so not decisive”. Even a drop from 5 correct to 1 cannot be called
decisive with only six questions, while the loss check shows a 0.194 rise, nearly twice the
allowance.

### 3. Fix the regression

**Try this:** raise **Replay sentences** one step at a time, starting from `none`, and watch **Keeps
the harbor text** in **Eval gate**. At `1 of 6 harbor sentences` it already passes, because harbor
loss now rises by only 0.057, which is under the 0.100 limit. The badge turns to `Gate: pass`, and
since the budget already fits, the **Release report** now reads `Verdict: ship`.

Keep going until the slider reads `4 of 6 harbor sentences`, and watch both losses as you go. Harbor
loss now ends at 2.086, a hair better than the base model’s 2.098, so the forgetting is gone. Recipe
loss ends at 2.367, so the recipe text actually gained more with replay than it did without it.

### 4. Compress

Leave **Replay sentences** at 4, then step **Weight format** through every option from `F16` down to
`Q4_0`. While you do, watch **Recipe loss, as shipped** and **Harbor loss, as shipped** in the first
row of numbers on **Compress and serve**. Across all five formats, neither one moves by as much as
0.02 nats, while **Toy bits per weight** falls from 16.04 to about 5.

Then set **Weight format** back to `Q4_K_M`, the starting format, and drag **Prune** to 50%.
**Recipe loss, as shipped** moves only from 2.367 to 2.372, so cutting half the weights costs almost
nothing here, and the model still ships. Now drag **Prune** all the way to 90%, and recipe loss
jumps back to 2.530, nearly the base model’s 2.540. Almost all of the recipe training is undone, and
the gate fails on both of its checks.

### 5. Budget and decide

Set **Prune** back to 0%, and look at the second row of numbers on **Compress and serve**. At the
starting load of 8 users and 4,096 tokens, **8B-class weights** reads 4.58 GiB and **KV cache for
all streams** reads 4.00 GiB. Add the 1 GiB of working space and you get 9.58 GiB, and the box below
confirms that this fits in 24 GiB with 14.42 GiB to spare.

Now set **Concurrent users** to 64 and **Context length** to 32,768, then read the **Release
report**. The model still passes both quality checks, but the verdict is hold, because the budget
has grown to 261.58 GiB, more than ten times the machine’s memory. Last, bring the two sliders down
until the budget fits again, and read the verdict and its reasons once more.

### More comparisons worth running

- With **Replay sentences** at `none`, slide **Adapter epochs** from 8 up to 40 and watch the harbor
  column. Harbor loss ends worse than the base at every setting. Past about 25 epochs, recipe loss
  also ends worse than the base at most settings, including 40, and it jumps up and down from one
  setting to the next. Long training on six recipe sentences pushes the shared table hard, and the
  lab shows the result without pinning down the exact cause. Repeat with replay at 4, and harbor
  never rises more than about 0.03 above the base. Recipe loss stays below the base at every
  setting, by more than 0.1 nats from 14 epochs on.
- Set **DPO steps** to 0 to switch the preference step off, and **After aligning** becomes identical
  to **After adapting**. The cost of aligning vanishes, and **Preference pairs ordered** reads
  `DPO off`, because no preference training ran.
- With replay at 4, set **LoRA rank** to 1, which gives the adapter only one pattern to build its
  correction from. The adapter is now too weak to move the model much, so the recipe check fails,
  which is a different kind of failure from forgetting.

## What to notice

- **Replay buys back the old job:** at the starting settings, the shipped model is 0.194 worse on
  harbor text and 0.043 better on recipe text. With four replayed sentences it is 0.173 better on
  recipe and no worse on harbor, so in this lab replay helped both texts.
- **Aligning has a price:** with four replayed sentences, going from **After adapting** to **After
  aligning** raises recipe loss by 0.030 and harbor loss by 0.040. Meanwhile **Preference pairs
  ordered** reads 100%, so in all three pairs the preferred ending has gained on the rejected one.
  Ability lost to preference training like this is called the **alignment tax**, and it shows up
  here because DPO edits the same 900 numbers both held-out texts rely on. DPO only cares about the
  gap inside each pair, not about making real recipe text likelier. The rejected endings, such as
  “onions onions onions onions.”, are built from everyday letter pairs, so pushing them down can
  make ordinary text a little less likely too.
- **The report keeps every cost on the record:** those two rises are regressions, and the **Release
  report** lists them under **Cost of getting here** even though the model ships. The recipe text
  still improved overall, so listing each stage stops a loss from hiding inside an overall gain.
- **The quiz barely notices:** with four replayed sentences, the recipe quiz reads 1 of 6 before and
  1 of 6 after, though recipe loss fell by 0.173. The ranges overlap, so the quiz is not decisive,
  while held-out loss shows a clear gain because it counts every character.
- **Shrinking is nearly free, but pruning is not:** these numbers use four replayed sentences. The
  shipped model is 0.022 worse on recipe text than **After aligning** at 50% pruning, and 0.180 worse
  at 90%. Rounding to `Q4_K_M` causes
  0.017 of that first rise on its own, so pruning half the weights adds only about 0.005. Pruning
  90% adds about 0.163, so removing a little under twice as much does about thirty times the
  damage. The harm grows much faster than the amount removed.
- **Memory fails in a different place:** at the heaviest load the sliders allow, the imagined
  8B-class budget is 261.58 GiB at `Q4_K_M` and 271.96 GiB at `F16`. Switching formats saves only
  about 10 GiB, because the notes alone take 256 GiB, so the KV cache, not the weights, is what runs
  out of room.

**A common mix-up:** “The recipe count went from 1 to 4, so the model clearly got better at
recipes.” At the starting settings the count did jump from 1 to 4, while recipe loss fell by only
0.043. With four replayed sentences the count stayed at 1, while recipe loss fell about four times
as far. Six questions move in jumps that can point the wrong way, which is exactly what “not
decisive” warns you about.

### Working out the memory

At 4,096 tokens, each conversation’s notes take half a GiB, which is why 8 users need 4.00 GiB of
cache. To find **Streams that fit**, start from the 24 GiB and subtract the 4.58 GiB of weights and
the 1 GiB of working space. Then divide the 18.42 GiB that is left by half a GiB per conversation,
which gives 36.84. A conversation cannot be split into pieces, so the lab rounds down and shows 36.

Raising **Context length** to 32,768 makes each conversation eight times as long, so its notes grow
eight times bigger, to 4 GiB each. With 64 users that comes to 64 × 4 = 256 GiB of notes, before the
weights are even counted. Adding users also slows each person down, because every step of writing
has to read through more notes. You can see this in **Per user**, the number of tokens each person
gets per second, which reads 43.4 tok/s at the starting load.

### The bill, stage by stage

Putting it all together, here is what each stage of the journey costs the model in this lab:

- **Adapting** can cost the old job through forgetting, unless replay mixes some of the old text
  back in.
- **Replay** asks one update to serve both texts. In general this costs some of the training signal
  for the new job, though in this lab four replayed sentences left the recipe text better off.
- **Aligning** costs a little loss on both texts at once, which is the alignment tax.
- **Quantizing** costs almost nothing here, while **pruning** costs more and more the harder you
  push it.
- **Serving** costs memory, mostly in notes that grow with every user and every token of context.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab fakes or simplifies

- **A tiny model makes the costs look bigger:** the model has only 900 weights, and the adapter and
  the preference step both edit that one table. So forgetting and the alignment tax are bigger here
  than they would be in a large model.
- **The tests are tiny:** twelve quiz questions and two held-out sentences per text cannot tell
  close models apart. The toy also looks back only one character, so for a quiz word it sees only
  the space in front and the word’s own letters. The start of the sentence, which is what a reader
  would use to choose, barely matters to it at all.
- **The pass marks are sliders:** that lets you do exactly what the pass-mark story warned against,
  so try it once to see. Drag **Allowed harbor-loss rise** to the top at the starting settings, and
  the forgetful model ships, while a real gate fixes its marks before the run.
- **The memory numbers are arithmetic, not measurements:** the quality numbers come from the toy,
  while the budget describes an imagined 8B-class model that is not in the lab. That arithmetic
  treats every conversation’s notes as full length from the start and adds a flat 1 GiB of working
  space. It also works out speed only from how fast memory can be read.
- **Pruning saves nothing here:** pruning that removes weights wherever they sit, called
  unstructured pruning, keeps the table the same size. So in this lab **Prune** changes neither
  **Toy file at this format**, the toy’s file size, nor the memory budget, and it only adds damage.
- **The toy is not a chatbot model:** it reads one character at a time rather than words or pieces
  of words, and it looks back only one character. It was also never trained to follow instructions,
  and eight sentences per text stand in for what would really be a huge collection of training data.

Nothing in this lab is measured on a real model or on real hardware, even where the numbers look
precise.

### Where the real mechanism fails

- **Passing is evidence, not proof:** a gate shows that a model cleared the tests you chose, and it
  cannot speak for the tests you never ran.
- **Real tests are far bigger:** releases of real models use thousands of test questions across many
  topics, and they check that those questions never slipped into the training data. They also test
  the whole product, such as search, tools and safety, and re-run a set of old cases after every
  change.
- **A test can be worn out:** if you keep adjusting a model until the same held-out text scores
  well, the model ends up tuned to that one text. That is called overfitting, and a gate tuned
  against its own test text can be overfit like any other test.
- **Compression depends on the model:** how much quality a format loses depends on the model and on
  the sample text used to tune the rounding. Unstructured pruning saves time only on hardware that
  can skip over the zeros it leaves behind.
- **Real speed depends on more than memory:** the software that does the math, how requests are
  grouped and how chips are linked all matter, and this arithmetic skips them.
- **People make the final call:** real release decisions weigh quality, safety, cost, speed and
  risk, usually with people who answer for the choice. A verdict from a script like this one is only
  one input to that decision.

**A common mix-up:** “It passed the gate, so it is safe to ship.” The gate here is two held-out
texts and one imagined machine, which is far too little to vouch for a real release. A pass says
nothing about a real model’s safety, its accuracy on real tasks, or how it behaves under real
traffic.

## Going deeper

### What a real release adds

- **A bigger, fresher test:** hundreds to thousands of questions for each skill, kept away from the
  model, with the uncertainty written down beside every score.
- **Tests for the whole product:** these check whether search finds the right passage and whether
  the answer sticks to what it found. For a model that carries out tasks on its own, called an
  agent, they check whether it finishes and what each task costs. Safety tests count how often
  attacks succeed and how often harmless requests get refused by mistake.
- **A careful rollout:** a small share of users goes first, with an automatic way back if a watched
  number moves. The team also keeps a record of which model, data and pass marks produced the
  release.
- **Watching after launch:** people type things nobody tested, and the world changes, so the gate is
  the start of the evidence, not the end.

### Behind the numbers

- The ranges in the benchmark line are Wilson intervals, the same kind the Evaluation lab draws, and
  they stay sensible even at 0 of 6 or 6 of 6.
- The weight sizes in the memory check come from the file sizes llama.cpp reports for a real model
  of this size, and everything built on them is arithmetic.
- For the imagined model, every token of every conversation needs 128 KiB of notes, where one KiB is
  1,024 bytes. The Deployment & serving lab shows step by step where that figure comes from.

### Where to go next

Every piece of this lab has a full-size version that you can download and run on your own computer:

- **Training and adapting:** Hugging Face Transformers, with PEFT for LoRA adapters and TRL for
  preference training such as DPO.
- **Squeezing and running a model on your own computer:** llama.cpp, the same engine this app
  downloads for its guide.
- **Serving many people at once:** vLLM, which groups many people’s requests together so they share
  the work.
- **Testing:** EleutherAI’s lm-evaluation-harness for standard tests, plus your own held-out set for
  the job you care about.

Four research papers cover most of what the labs touched, and two of them describe stages in this
lab directly:

- *Attention Is All You Need*, by Vaswani and colleagues in 2017, which introduced the design behind
  most of today’s chatbots;
- *LoRA*, by Hu and colleagues in 2021, which describes the adapter method used in Stage 2;
- *Direct Preference Optimization*, by Rafailov and colleagues in 2023, which describes the
  preference method used in Stage 3;
- *Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models*, by Hoffmann and colleagues in 2022, which works
  out how big a model should be for a given amount of training.

A good first project is to repeat this lab’s loop on a small real model of your own choosing. Pick
one job, hold out a control set, adapt the model, and write your gate down before you run anything.
