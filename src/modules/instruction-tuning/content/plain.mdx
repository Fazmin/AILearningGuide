## The big picture

When you type a question into a chatbot, it writes an answer, stops, and waits for your next message.
That feels as natural as texting a friend, but the model underneath never sees chat bubbles or a send
button. It receives one long line of text, and the one thing it has been trained to do is guess which
piece of text comes next.

So something has to turn your conversation into that single line, and the line has to show whose words
are whose and where each reply ends. This module is about that layout, which is called a **chat
template**, and about the extra round of training that teaches a model to use it. That training is what
the module's title calls instruction tuning: practice on hand-picked examples of requests, each paired
with a good reply.

### Where you have met it

Each chat model is trained with its own template, which ships with the model so that the software
running it can lay out your messages the same way. When a chatbot finishes its answer and stops instead
of rambling on, that is because training taught it to write an end marker at that point. A template can
also hold a block of instructions that comes before your message, such as "answer briefly and plainly",
which is called a **system prompt**.

### Why it matters

A model that has only learned from web pages is good at continuing text but poor at answering it. Ask it
"how long should it simmer" and it may reply with another question, because on web pages one question is
often followed by more questions. It also has no signal for when to stop, so it keeps writing and often
makes up your next message itself.

The template also has to match between training and use, and a mismatch is easy to miss. If an app lays
out your messages differently from the way the model was trained, nothing crashes or shows an error,
while the model quietly does a worse job. The markers matter for safety as well: a system prompt
stays trusted only while ordinary typed text cannot copy the special labels around it.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- what a chat template adds to a plain question and answer, and why a model needs it;
- which part of the long line the model is trained to write, and which parts it only reads;
- what happens to where a model's replies stop if it trains without an end marker;
- what a trained model does next when it is given a layout it never trained on.

### What the lab lets you try

The lab trains two tiny models on the same four questions and answers, about fog, a sauce, the weather
by noon and how long to simmer something. One model sees them laid out with the chat template, while the
other sees each question and answer run together as plain text. You can switch parts of the template on
and off, choose which characters the model is graded on during training, and write your own examples. Then you can give
both trained models a layout they never saw and watch what each one does next.

The models are small enough to train instantly, so every number you see is really measured rather than
made up. The four starting examples, the system sentence and one test reply are fixed text written for
the lab. The markers are spelled out in letters rather than being special symbols. The models are also far simpler than a real chatbot, and the lesson points out where that changes
what the numbers mean.

## What it is

### One long line of text

A chat model never receives a conversation, only one long run of characters, which programmers call a
**string**. The **chat template** is the rule that decides exactly what that string looks like. Here is
the first example in the lab with every part of the template switched on, split over three lines so you
can read it:

```
<system>answer briefly and plainly.<end>
<user>when does the fog arrive<end>
<assistant>the fog settles over the harbor in the morning.<end>
```

The model gets all of this as one unbroken line of 138 characters. Each of the three switches in **Chat
template editor** adds or removes one kind of piece, and each switch shows `in template` or `off`:

- The **System block** switch puts `<system>` and the sentence "answer briefly and plainly." at the
  front. That sentence is the **system prompt**, a block of instructions that sits outside the user's
  turn.
- The **Role markers** switch adds labels called **role markers**, which say whose words each turn
  holds: `<user>` goes in front of the question and `<assistant>` in front of the answer.
- The **End-of-turn marker** switch closes every turn with `<end>`, including the system block when that
  is on. The question gets its `<end>` only while **Role markers** is on too, as step 1 below shows.

### A radio picture

Picture two people talking on walkie-talkies, where only one of them can talk at a time, so they follow
a routine. Each turn starts by saying who is talking, such as "this is base", and ends with the word
"over" to hand the turn back. Those words carry no news, since their only job is to mark where a turn
starts and ends. The markers do the same job, with `<user>` and `<assistant>` as the "this is base" part
and `<end>` as the "over".

The picture stops matching in two places. First, a person can say "over" in an ordinary sentence, such
as "the game is over", and the listener has to work out which meaning was intended. In this lab that
cannot happen, because `<` and `>` never appear in the lab's example text and are removed from anything you type,
so a bracketed marker can only ever mean structure. Second, a radio operator already knows what "over"
means, while a model gets nothing from `<end>` until training has shown it many examples of the marker
in use.

### The tiny models in this lab

Real chat models are huge, so the lab uses the smallest kind of model that can still show the idea. It
works with only 30 characters: the space, the 26 letters, the full stop, the opening bracket `<` and the
closing bracket `>`. Each model is a table with one row for each of those characters, and every row
holds the odds for which character comes next.

To make a guess, the model looks only at the last character and reads that character's row. If the last
character is `t`, it reads the `t` row, which after training gives `h` by far the best odds because
"the" is so common in the replies. The whole model is 30 rows of 30 numbers, which makes 900 numbers in
all, while a real chat model looks back over the whole conversation before every guess.

Both models start blank, with every row giving all 30 characters the same chance: 1 in 30, which is
about 3.3%. Training changes a row only when the model is graded on a guess made from that row. As you
will see below, the lab can choose to grade only some guesses. A row that is never used for a graded
guess stays at an even 3.3% for every character. Keep that in mind, because it explains several of the
most surprising results in the lab.

The lab trains two of these tables side by side on the same four examples. The templated model trains on
the chat template, while the plain model trains on each question and answer run together as plain text.

### Same guessing game, new layout

Big language models are first trained on enormous amounts of text by guessing the next piece at every
position, which is called **pretraining**. After each guess, training checks what really came next and
gives a penalty that grows with how surprised the model was. That penalty is called the **loss**, and
training works by nudging the model's numbers so that the loss gets smaller next time.

The lab measures surprise in a unit called **nats**, and a perfect guess, which gave the right character
a 100% chance, scores 0. Every time that chance is cut in half, the surprise goes up by about 0.69, so
the lower the chance the model gave the right character, the higher its surprise. A model that spreads
its odds evenly over all 30 characters scores about 3.40, which is the score of a model that has learned
nothing at all.

**Supervised fine-tuning** plays the same guessing game with the same penalty, but on a much smaller,
hand-picked set of requests paired with good replies. It is called supervised because every example
comes with the reply it should get, and fine-tuning because in real life it adjusts a model that
pretraining has already trained. Two things change from pretraining: the text is laid out as turns with
a chat template, and usually only the reply is graded.

### Graded, or only read

The switch labelled **Loss on** decides which characters count toward the loss, and with `Assistant
reply only`, the only graded ones are the reply and the `<end>` right after it. Everything before them,
from the system prompt to the question and its markers, is still read, because each next guess is based
on it. The model is never graded on guessing those characters, though.

This is called **loss masking**, because a mask covers part of the line so that those guesses do not
count. `Every character` removes the mask and grades every guess, which is exactly the rule pretraining
uses.

The same switch applies to the plain model, even though its text has no markers. The lab still knows
which part of each plain line is the reply, so with `Assistant reply only` it grades the reply's
characters and only reads the question. While the end marker is on, the templated model is also graded
on the `<end>` after each reply, so the two models are not graded on quite the same characters.

Picture a worksheet where you copy out each question and then write your answer underneath. You need the
question in order to answer it, but only your answer earns marks, and nobody gets points for copying the
question neatly. Loss masking grades a model the same way, and for the same reason: the goal is to get
better at answering, not at writing questions.

The strip under the switches shows the first example with one cell per character. Grey, hatched cells
are "context only: read, never graded", while plain cells with a blue underline are "graded: in the
loss". A line along the top of each cell shows whose turn the character belongs to. A bar inside the
cell shows how surprised the trained model is by that character, on a scale from 0 to 5 nats.

At the default settings, **Graded characters** reads 52 of 137. The total is 137 rather than 138 because
the very first character has nothing in front of it to guess from, so it is never predicted. The 52
graded characters are the 47 characters of the reply "the fog settles over the harbor in the morning."
plus the 5 characters of `<end>`.

### Markers as special symbols

Real models do not read one character at a time; instead, they cut text into chunks called **tokens**,
which are often whole words or pieces of words. Each token has its own id number, and a real template
gives every marker a reserved id of its own, called a **special token**. That makes `<end>` one symbol
rather than five characters.

In this lab, by contrast, the markers are spelled out in characters, so `<end>` uses the same `e`, `n`
and `d` rows as ordinary words like "the" and "and". Because `<end>` is graded, the marker and the
words compete for those rows. For instance, the `d` row has to point at a space after "and" but at `>`
inside `<end>`, so it cannot give either one the full odds. That sharing has a small cost, which you
will see in the lab's reply scores.

## Why it’s here

Look at what the plain model trains on for the first example: "when does the fog arrive the fog settles
over the harbor in the morning." Nothing in that line says where the question stops and the answer
starts, and nothing comes after the final full stop. Text taken from web pages has the same gap, because
it never says whose turn it is.

The template adds two things that plain text cannot, and together they make **instruction following**
learnable. Instruction following means writing a reply to a request instead of carrying on with the
request's own text. A model picks this up from formatted examples like the ones in this lab, not from
more pretraining.

### A starting line for the reply

The first thing the template adds is a clear starting line: after `<assistant>`, everything is the
model's own output, and that is exactly the part the mask grades. When you chat, the app sends your
question followed by `<assistant>`. That puts the model in the same spot where training made it practise
writing replies, so a reply is what comes next.

### A signal to stop

The second thing is an **end-of-turn token**, a marker the model learns to write when a reply is
finished. The part of the software that generates a reply, called the **decoder**, picks one piece of
text at a time and keeps going until it reaches a stop signal. If the model never learned to write that signal, the decoder
has nothing to stop on, so the model keeps writing and often invents the user's next question itself.

Picture two campers on walkie-talkies, and one of them has never learned to say "over". They finish
their news, keep holding the button, and then start guessing what their friend might say back, because
nothing in their routine tells them to hand the turn over. A model trained without `<end>` runs on in
the same way, because nothing in its training marks the point where a reply ends.

The picture stops matching in one way. The camper is guessing on purpose, while a model is not trying to
do anything. A real model writes a fake user turn only because, in conversation text, a user's message
is what usually comes next. The lab's tiny model cannot show even that: with no `<end>` and the mask on, its row
for the full stop is untrained. So the character it writes right after a reply is a pure guess, with
every character equally likely.

### Why only the reply is graded

The mask exists because the model is meant to write replies, not user messages. If every character were
graded, the model would also practise writing the questions and markers that the user and the app
supply. That practice can then show up in the wrong place, as the lab shows.

When every character is graded, the templated model also practises guessing what comes after each `>`.
In its training text, `<end>` is followed straight away by `<user>` or `<assistant>`. So it learns that
the closing bracket is often followed by an opening one. A reply starts right after the `>` of
`<assistant>`, so the model may open its reply with a marker instead of a word.

## How to play with it

The lab follows five steps, and each one below says what to change and what to watch.

### 1. Edit the template

Turn **System block**, **Role markers** and **End-of-turn marker** on and off one at a time, and watch
the strip gain and lose structure. For example, turn off only **Role markers**, and the line becomes
this:

```
<system>answer briefly and plainly.<end>when does the fog arrive the fog settles over the harbor in the morning.<end>
```

Now the question runs straight into the answer with a single space between them, so nothing shows where
one ends and the other begins. Turn all three switches off, and the strip shows exactly the plain text
that the plain model trains on.

Watch the count as well: with **End-of-turn marker** off, **Graded characters** drops from 52 to 47,
since the reply no longer has an `<end>` after it to grade.

### 2. Read the loss mask

With every switch back on, find `<assistant>` in the strip, and you will see the graded cells begin
right after it, at the `t` of "the".

**Try this:** switch **Loss on** between `Assistant reply only` and `Every character`, and read the
three numbers under the strip. With the mask on, **Graded characters** reads 52 of 137. Beside it,
**Mean −log p, graded** reads 2.11 nats, while **Mean −log p, context** reads 2.92 nats.

The label "−log p" is the lab's short way of writing the surprise score from earlier, and "mean" means
average. Here p is the chance the model gave the right character. Later you will see **log-prob**,
written log p, which is the same number with the sign flipped, so it is negative.

A surprise of 2.11 is what you would get if the model gave each right character about a 1 in 8 chance.
You can check this with the halving rule, since getting from 100% down to 1 in 8 takes three halvings.
Three steps of 0.69 add up to about 2.1. A surprise of 2.92 is closer to 1 in 18, so the context
characters are much harder for the model to predict. That makes sense, because the model is never
graded on them, so only the letter pairs they share with the replies get any practice.

Switch to `Every character`, and all 137 characters are graded, so **Mean −log p, context** reads "none"
because no character is left ungraded. The graded average becomes 1.99 nats, which is close to a 1 in 7
chance. Set **Loss on** back to `Assistant reply only` before you move on, because the numbers in the
next steps assume the mask is on.

### 3. Fine-tune on your pairs

In **Supervised dataset editor**, each line is one example, written as `instruction | response`. The
request goes to the left of the upright line `|`, and the reply it should get goes to the right.
Everything you type is made lowercase. Digits, commas and other symbols turn into spaces, and brackets
are removed, because the models only use 30 characters. The editor keeps up to 16 lines of 200 characters each, and a line
with no `|` drops out, which you can spot in the **Examples** count.

The **SFT epochs** slider (SFT is short for supervised fine-tuning) sets how many times training works
through all the examples, and one full pass through them is called an **epoch**. You can choose anywhere from 1 to 80 passes. Move it and watch the
readout `“<” after the full stop` in **Two models, two formats**, which gives each model's chance of
starting `<end>` after the reply's last full stop.

With the four starting pairs, the templated model gives that marker about a 6% chance at 4 epochs. At
the default of 30 epochs that rises to 54%, and at 80 epochs it reaches 88%. The end of a turn is
learned gradually, like any other pattern in the examples.

The same card shows **Reply log-prob per char, templated**, the log-prob from step 2. So −2.228 at the
defaults means an average surprise of 2.228 per character of the reply, and closer to zero is better. It
moves towards zero as the passes rise. At 4 epochs it is about −3.02, close to a 1 in 20 chance for
each right character. At 80 epochs it reaches about −1.87, which is nearer to 1 in 6.

The chart above it shows each model's loss falling as training goes on. Its bottom axis counts
**optimizer steps**, where each step is a single nudge to the table based on a small batch of graded
guesses. Each point on the chart averages a few steps.

The two training curves grade different characters, so the chart's own note warns that they are not a
ranking of which model is better. The same note gives the counts for one pass over the examples: 191
graded guesses for the templated model against 171 for the plain one. The extra 20 are the five
characters of `<end>` at the end of each of the four replies. **Graded targets**, in **Supervised
dataset editor**, shows the templated count, and it jumps to 528 when **Loss on** is set to `Every
character`.

Before you move on, delete any line you added and set **SFT epochs** back to 30. The numbers in the
next steps assume the four starting pairs at 30 passes.

### 4. Break the boundary

Turn **End-of-turn marker** off, keep the mask on, and compare the two lists under **What follows the
end of a turn**. Each list shows the five characters a model gives the best odds right after the full
stop that ends the first reply. The templated model is on top, and the plain model is below.

With the marker on, the templated model's top bar is `<` at 54.4%, the first character of `<end>`. With
it off, the upper list goes flat like the lower one. Every character, `<` included, gets 3.3%, so the
list shows only the first five characters in order: space, a, b, c and d. The readout `“<” after the
full stop` drops to 3% for both models. Turn the
marker back on before the last step, since its numbers assume the full template.

### 5. Serve a different format

Using a trained model to answer prompts is called **serving** it, and the card **Serve the trained
models** does that with both of the lab's models. It sends them the first example, cut off right where
the reply should begin. The box shows the exact string sent, with its last character highlighted. Each
model reads only that last character, so that one character decides what each model does next.

**Try this:** switch **Serve with** between `Chat template`, `No template` and `Wrong role marker`, and
watch the readout **First reply character “t”, templated vs plain**. Under `Chat template`, the reply's
first letter `t` gets 18% from the templated model and only 3% from the plain one. The plain model never
saw a `>` in front of anything during training, so its `>` row was never trained and spreads its odds
evenly.

`No template` sends only the question and a space, and then the templated model reads 22% while the
plain one reads 25%. The plain model does well here because in its training a single space sat right
before every reply. `Wrong role marker` puts `<user>` where `<assistant>` belongs, and you will find it
gives exactly the same results as `Chat template`; "What to notice" explains why. Serving a model with a
layout that differs from the one it trained on is called a **template mismatch**.

Above the bars, each model also writes a short piece of text, called a **sample**, by picking one
character at a time, with likelier characters picked more often. A note under each sample says where a decoder watching for
markers would stop.

Last, set **Loss on** to `Every character` and serve `Chat template` again. The templated model now puts
37% on `<` right after the closing `>`, and its sample opens with a marker instead of a reply. A decoder
watching for markers would stop at once, leaving an empty reply.

This happens because, with every switch on, each training example has two places where a closing bracket
is followed by `<`, inside `<end><user>` and `<end><assistant>`. Grading those characters taught the
model that a bracket is often followed by another marker.

## What to notice

### Where a reply ends

- With the full template and the mask, the templated model puts 54.4% on `<` right after a reply's full
  stop, so its likeliest next step is to start `<end>`.
- The plain model gives every character 3.3% at that same spot, because its row for the full stop was
  never trained. In its training, nothing ever came after a reply's last full stop, so nothing in that
  row says to stop.
- Turn **End-of-turn marker** off with the mask still on, and the templated model drops to 3.3% too. The
  graded `<end>` was the only thing teaching it where a reply ends. The role markers cannot do that job,
  because they sit before the reply, where nothing is graded.
- Switch to `Every character` with the end marker still off, and the templated model is back to 55% on
  `<`, even with no `<end>` in its data. It learned that from the system prompt, where "plainly." is
  followed by `<user>`, which is a boundary it was never meant to be graded on. The masked result is
  cleaner, because there the model learns where a reply ends from replies alone.
- A tall bar for the opening bracket means the model will probably start a marker, not that it will
  spell `<end>` correctly. The rest of the marker is still predicted one character at a time.

### What the mask changes

- Under the mask, the context characters (the system prompt, the markers and the question) average 2.92
  nats, against 2.11 for the graded reply. Nothing trains the model to predict them directly.
- The question still improves as a side effect. Its characters, spaces included, average about 2.50
  nats, well below the 3.40 of a blank model. That is because letter pairs like "th" and "he" in "the
  fog" also appear in the replies, so the question uses rows that the replies trained.
- The point of the mask is where the training effort goes: into writing replies, not into writing the
  user's side of the chat.

### What the template costs and buys

- The template costs the reply a little: **Reply log-prob per char, templated** reads −2.228, while
  **Reply log-prob per char, plain** reads −2.168, slightly closer to zero.
- Both of those scores work out to roughly a 1 in 9 chance per character, so the gap is small. It has
  two causes in this tiny model.
- First, the graded `<end>` uses the same `e`, `n` and `d` rows as the reply's letters, so those rows
  split their odds between the marker and ordinary words.
- Second, the reply's first letter is guessed from the `>` row, which has had far less training than the
  space row the plain model uses. The `<assistant>` letters themselves are not graded under the mask, so
  they train nothing.
- With `Every character`, the gap grows to −2.425 against −2.129, because grading the prompt makes the
  templated model spend more of those shared rows on the markers.
- What the template buys in return is a known place where every turn starts and ends. The readout `“<”
  after the full stop` shows it: 54% for the templated model, against 3% for the plain one, which has
  never seen `<end>`.

### Serving a different format

- A plain-trained model served the chat template does not crash or show an error. It answers from a row
  that nobody trained, so all of its next-character bars sit flat at 3.3%.
- Taking the template away even raises the templated model's score for `t`, from 18% with the template
  to 22% with `No template`. In this toy that is an accident of reading one character, because its
  space row was trained on the spaces between the replies' own words. It does not mean templates are
  optional.
- `Chat template` and `Wrong role marker` give identical bars and samples. Both strings end in `>`, and
  a model that reads one character cannot tell `<user>` from `<assistant>`, while a model that reads the
  whole marker can.
- The samples look like scraps of words rather than sentences, because a model that sees one character
  can only learn which letters tend to follow which. At the defaults, the templated sample writes a few
  scraps, reaches a full stop, and starts a marker right after it.
- **Held-out reply, log-prob per char, templated vs plain** scores a reply that appears in none of the
  training pairs, "the rain stops before noon." It reads −2.86 for the templated model and −2.87 for the
  plain one. Compare the templated −2.86 with −2.23, which **Reply log-prob per char, templated** gives
  the first trained reply.
- That gap is roughly the difference between a 1 in 17 chance and a 1 in 9 chance for each character.
  Scores on training data flatter a model, the same way that grading yourself on the exact questions you
  practised would flatter you.

**A common mix-up:** the mask does not hide the question from the model. Masked characters are still
read, and they are exactly what each next guess is based on; the mask only takes them out of the grade.
In the worksheet picture, you still read each question in order to answer it, and the teacher does not
mark your copy of it.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Every number in this lab is really trained and measured, so none of it is made up. What is missing is
size, and that limits what the numbers can mean.

- Each model looks at one character to guess the next. So "it knows the turn ended" means only that the
  row for `.` learned to point at `<`, the first character of `<end>`. A real model predicts a single
  stop symbol from the whole conversation.
- Four examples is not a dataset, while real fine-tuning uses thousands to millions of pairs.
- Both models start from blank tables, instead of from a model that pretraining has already taught.
- The markers are spelled in letters instead of being single reserved ids, so `<end>` shares rows with
  ordinary text.
- The system prompt is one fixed sentence, the strip shows only the first example, and only the end of
  the first reply is probed.
- Serving is tested through one character only, because each model reads the last character of the
  served string. A template mismatch therefore shows up as one different row of the table.
- That means the real cost of a mismatch cannot appear here: a real model reads the whole unfamiliar
  prompt again at every step of its reply.
- Each sample picks characters at random, with likelier characters picked more often, but the lab
  reuses the same random numbers every time. A sample therefore changes only when the trained table or the
  served string changes. Either way, you see one possible reply rather than a typical one, and the
  held-out reply is one sentence, so neither tells you much on its own.
- The two reply scores cover the same characters and are divided by the same count, which makes them
  fair to compare. They still come from two differently laid-out datasets, so treat the gap between them
  as a hint rather than proof.

### Where real systems fail

Serving a model with the wrong template is a common failure, and it is silent. Nothing errors;
the model receives a string unlike anything it trained on, and the quality of its answers sags.

Picture a student who practised for weeks with quizzes where every question started with "Q:" and every
answer space started with "A:". On test day the paper uses a new layout, so the student still writes
answers but makes more slips, and nothing on the paper says the layout caused them. A model served the
wrong template is in the same spot, except that it cannot notice the change at all.

A serving system that builds prompts by gluing text together can also turn `<end>` into five ordinary
characters instead of the reserved id. The two look identical in the printed record of what was sent, so the bug is hard to spot, but
the model never receives the marker it was trained on.

Stopping also depends on the serving software, which keeps a list of stop signals to watch for. If the
stop id is missing from that list, nothing halts the reply when the marker appears, and the model runs
on into an invented next turn.

There is a security side as well, since a **system prompt** only counts as instructions because the
template and the training data agreed to treat it that way. If the software turns a marker that a user
typed into the real marker id, the user's text can pretend to be a system turn.

Picture a group chat where messages from the admin appear in a special gold box. If anyone could type a
short code that put their own message in the gold box, the box would stop meaning anything. Reserved ids
only protect a system prompt when the serving software keeps them out of user text.

## Going deeper

### How real templates ship

Real models ship their template as a small piece of code, stored in the settings of the **tokenizer**,
which is the part that cuts text into tokens. The software that runs the model uses it to lay out every
conversation. When it is time to write a reply, the template also adds the opening assistant marker
after your message, so the model starts writing in the right place.

Adding a brand-new marker needs a new token id and a new row of numbers inside the model for that id, so
it changes the size of the model itself.

### Masking is a choice

Most fine-tuning recipes grade only the assistant's turns, but some research finds that grading the
instructions too can help when replies are short and examples are scarce. So the mask is a setting to
test, not a law.

### What the examples teach

The **superficial alignment hypothesis** is an idea about what this kind of training really does. Here
"alignment" means getting a model to behave the way people want when it talks with users, and a
hypothesis is an idea that researchers propose and then test. This one says that a model learns nearly
all its knowledge and skills during pretraining, and that later tuning mostly teaches it which format
and style of reply to use.

The idea comes from a 2023 paper by Zhou and colleagues, called LIMA, short for "Less Is More for
Alignment". They fine-tuned a large model called LLaMA, with about 65 billion adjustable numbers, on
only 1,000 carefully chosen pairs of prompts and replies. For comparison, each model in this lab has 900
adjustable numbers.

Smaller tests on a model with 7 billion numbers pointed the same way. The researchers trained it on 2,000
examples from the Stack Exchange question-and-answer sites, picked only from good-quality posts. That
raised a helpfulness score by about half a point on a six-point scale, compared with 2,000 examples
picked without that quality check. Doubling the amount of data, on the other hand, did not make the replies better.

The scores in those tests were given by another AI model, ChatGPT, rather than by people. So treat the
results as evidence for the idea about format and style, not as proof that fine-tuning adds no
knowledge.

### Conversations with many turns

The lab's examples each have one question and one answer, but real chats go back and forth. In a real
chat the template repeats: a user block, an assistant block, then a user block again. The masking rule
stretches to fit, because the whole conversation is trained as one string, every assistant turn is
graded, and every user turn is read as context.

**Multi-turn data**, meaning training conversations with several turns each, matters because a model
tuned only on single questions can lose the thread. In the LIMA paper, one model was tuned on 1,000
examples that each had a single turn. That model stopped following the prompt within three exchanges in
6 of the 10 test chats.

Adding only 30 multi-turn conversations raised the share of excellent replies from 45.2% to 76.1%. That
is a jump from fewer than half of the replies to about three in every four.

### Packing

Real models read text in windows of a fixed length, called the **context window**. A short example
leaves most of the window empty, and that space is filled with blank filler called padding, which the
computer still has to work through. **Packing** glues several short examples into one sequence to fill
the window instead.

That can save a lot of work, because Krell and colleagues found that up to half of the tokens in common
training datasets can be padding. Packing needs care, though, because each example still needs its own
end marker. The model must also be blocked from looking back across the join, or one example can read
the one before it as if they were one conversation.

In this lab every example is its own sequence, so nothing is packed and no guess ever crosses from one
example into the next.
