## The big picture

When you type a question into a chatbot, it writes an answer, stops, and waits for your next message. That feels as natural as texting a friend. But the model underneath never sees chat bubbles or a send button. It gets one long line of text. Its training taught it one skill, which is guessing the next piece.

So something has to turn your conversation into that single line. The line must show whose words are whose and where each reply ends. This module is about that layout, which is called a **chat template**. It is also about the extra round of training that teaches a model to use the layout. That training is instruction tuning: practice on hand-picked examples of requests, each paired with a good reply.

### Where you have met it

Each chat model is trained with its own template. The template ships with the model, so the software running it can lay out your messages the same way. When a chatbot stops instead of rambling on, that is training at work. It learned to write an end marker at that point. A template can also hold a block of instructions before your message, such as "answer briefly and plainly". That block is called a **system prompt**.

### Why it matters

A model that has only learned from web pages is good at continuing text but poor at answering it. Ask it "how long should it simmer" and it may reply with another question. Web pages often follow one question with more questions. It also has no signal for when to stop. So it keeps writing, and it often makes up your next message itself.

The template also has to match between training and use, and a mismatch is hard to spot. Say an app lays out your messages differently from the way the model trained. Nothing crashes and no error appears, but the model quietly does a worse job. The markers matter for safety too. A system prompt stays trusted only while ordinary typed text cannot copy the special labels around it.

### What you will be able to explain

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words:

- what a chat template adds to a plain question and answer, and why a model needs it;
- which part of the long line the model is trained to write, and which parts it only reads;
- what happens to where a model's replies stop if it trains without an end marker;
- what a trained model does next when it gets a layout it never trained on.

### What the lab lets you try

The lab trains two tiny models on the same four questions and answers. They are about fog, a sauce, the weather by noon and how long to simmer something. One model sees them laid out with the chat template. The other sees each question and answer run together as plain text.

You can switch parts of the template on and off and pick which characters get graded during training. You can also write your own examples. Then you can give both trained models a layout they never saw and watch what each one does next.

The models are small enough to train instantly, so every number you see is really measured. None of it is made up. The four starting examples, the system sentence and one test reply are fixed text written for the lab. The markers are spelled out in letters instead of being special symbols. The models are also far simpler than a real chatbot. The lesson points out where that changes what the numbers mean.

## What it is

### One long line of text

A chat model never receives a conversation. It receives one long run of characters, which programmers call a **string**. The **chat template** is the rule that decides exactly what that string looks like. Here is the lab's first example with every part of the template switched on. It is split over three lines so you can read it:

```
<system>answer briefly and plainly.<end>
<user>when does the fog arrive<end>
<assistant>the fog settles over the harbor in the morning.<end>
```

The model gets all of this as one unbroken line of 138 characters. Each of the three switches in **Chat template editor** adds or removes one kind of piece. Each switch shows `in template` or `off`.

- The **System block** switch puts `<system>` and the sentence "answer briefly and plainly." at the front. That sentence is the **system prompt**, a block of instructions that sits outside the user's turn.
- The **Role markers** switch adds labels called **role markers**, which say whose words each turn holds. The label `<user>` goes in front of the question and `<assistant>` in front of the answer.
- The **End-of-turn marker** switch closes every turn with `<end>`, including the system block when that is on. The question gets its `<end>` only while **Role markers** is on too, as step 1 below shows.

### A radio picture

Picture two people talking on walkie-talkies. Only one of them can talk at a time, so they follow a routine. Each turn starts by saying who is talking, such as "this is base". It ends with the word "over" to hand the turn back. Those words carry no news, because their only job is to mark where a turn starts and ends. The markers do the same job. The labels `<user>` and `<assistant>` are the "this is base" part, and `<end>` is the "over".

The picture stops matching in two places. First, a person can say "over" in an ordinary sentence, such as "the game is over". The listener then has to work out which meaning was meant. In this lab that cannot happen. The signs `<` and `>` never appear in the lab's example text, and they are removed from anything you type. So a bracketed marker can only ever mean structure.

Second, a radio operator already knows what "over" means. A model gets nothing from `<end>` until training has shown it many examples of the marker in use.

### The tiny models in this lab

Real chat models are huge, so the lab uses the smallest model that can still show the idea. It works with only 30 characters: the space, the 26 letters, the full stop, the opening bracket `<` and the closing bracket `>`. Each model is a table with one row for each of those characters. Every row holds the odds for which character comes next.

To make a guess, the model looks only at the last character and reads that character's row. Say the last character is `t`. The model reads the `t` row. After training, that row gives `h` the best odds by far, since "the" is so common in the replies. The whole model is 30 rows of 30 numbers, which makes 900 numbers in all. A real chat model looks back over the whole conversation before every guess.

Both models start blank. Every row gives all 30 characters the same chance, which is 1 in 30, or about 3.3%. Training changes a row only when the model is graded on a guess made from that row. As you will see below, the lab can choose to grade only some guesses. A row that is never used for a graded guess stays at an even 3.3% for every character. Keep that in mind, because it explains several of the most surprising results in the lab.

The lab trains two of these tables side by side on the same four examples. The templated model trains on the chat template. The plain model trains on each question and answer run together as plain text.

### Same guessing game, new layout

Big language models are first trained on enormous amounts of text by guessing the next piece at every position. This stage is called **pretraining**. After each guess, training checks what really came next. It gives a penalty that grows with how surprised the model was. That penalty is called the **loss**. Training works by nudging the model's numbers so that the loss gets smaller next time.

The lab measures surprise in a unit called **nats**. A perfect guess, one that gave the right character a 100% chance, scores 0. Every time that chance is cut in half, the surprise goes up by about 0.69. So the lower the chance the model gave the right character, the higher its surprise. A model that spreads its odds evenly over all 30 characters scores about 3.40. That is the score of a model that has learned nothing at all.

**Supervised fine-tuning** plays the same guessing game with the same penalty. But it uses a much smaller, hand-picked set of requests paired with good replies. It is called supervised because every example comes with the reply it should get. It is called fine-tuning because, in real life, it adjusts a model that pretraining has already trained. Two things change from pretraining. The text is laid out as turns with a chat template, and usually only the reply is graded.

### Graded, or only read

The switch labelled **Loss on** decides which characters count toward the loss. With `Assistant reply only`, the only graded ones are the reply and the `<end>` right after it. Everything before them is still read, from the system prompt to the question and its markers. Each next guess is based on it. But the model is never graded on guessing those characters.

This is called **loss masking**. A mask covers part of the line, so those guesses do not count. The setting `Every character` removes the mask and grades every guess. That is exactly the rule pretraining uses.

The same switch applies to the plain model, even though its text has no markers. The lab still knows which part of each plain line is the reply. So with `Assistant reply only`, it grades the reply's characters and only reads the question. While the end marker is on, the templated model is also graded on the `<end>` after each reply. That means the two models are not graded on quite the same characters.

Picture a worksheet where you copy out each question and then write your answer underneath. You need the question in order to answer it, but only your answer earns marks. Nobody gets points for copying the question neatly. Loss masking grades a model the same way, and for the same reason. The goal is to get better at answering, not at writing questions.

The strip under the switches shows the first example with one cell per character. Grey, hatched cells are "context only: read, never graded". Plain cells with a blue underline are "graded: in the loss". A line along the top of each cell shows whose turn the character belongs to. A bar inside the cell shows how surprised the trained model is by that character. The scale runs from 0 to 5 nats.

At the default settings, **Graded characters** reads 52 of 137. The total is 137 rather than 138. The very first character has nothing in front of it to guess from, so the model never predicts it. The 52 graded characters are the 47 characters of the reply, plus the 5 characters of `<end>`. The reply reads "the fog settles over the harbor in the morning."

### Markers as special symbols

Real models do not read one character at a time. They cut text into chunks called **tokens**, which are often whole words or pieces of words. Each token has its own id number. A real template gives every marker a reserved id of its own, called a **special token**. That makes `<end>` one symbol instead of five characters.

In this lab, by contrast, the markers are spelled out in characters. So `<end>` uses the same `e`, `n` and `d` rows as ordinary words like "the" and "and". Because `<end>` is graded, the marker and the words compete for those rows. For instance, the `d` row must point at a space after "and" but at `>` inside `<end>`. So it cannot give either one the full odds. That sharing has a small cost, which you will see in the lab's reply scores.

## Why it’s here

Look at what the plain model trains on for the first example:

```
when does the fog arrive the fog settles over the harbor in the morning.
```

Nothing in that line says where the question stops and the answer starts. Nothing comes after the final full stop either. Text taken from web pages has the same gap, because it never says whose turn it is.

The template adds two things that plain text cannot, and together they make **instruction following** learnable. Instruction following means writing a reply to a request instead of carrying on with the request's own text. A model picks this up from formatted examples like the ones in this lab, not from more pretraining.

### A starting line for the reply

The first thing the template adds is a clear starting line. After `<assistant>`, everything is the model's own output, and that is exactly the part the mask grades. When you chat, the app sends your question followed by `<assistant>`. That puts the model in the same spot where it practised writing replies in training. So a reply is what comes next.

### A signal to stop

The second thing is an **end-of-turn token**, a marker the model learns to write when a reply is finished. The part of the software that writes a reply is called the **decoder**. It picks one piece of text at a time and keeps going until it reaches a stop signal. If the model never learned to write that signal, the decoder has nothing to stop on. The model keeps writing, and it often invents the user's next question itself.

Picture two campers on walkie-talkies, and one of them has never learned to say "over". They finish their news and keep holding the button. Then they start guessing what their friend might say back. Nothing in their routine tells them to hand the turn over. A model trained without `<end>` runs on in the same way. Nothing in its training marks the point where a reply ends.

The picture stops matching in one way. The camper guesses on purpose, while a model is not trying to do anything. A real model writes a fake user turn because a user's message usually comes next in conversation text.

The lab's tiny model cannot show even that. With no `<end>` and the mask on, its row for the full stop is untrained. So the character it writes right after a reply is a pure guess, with every character equally likely.

### Why only the reply is graded

The mask exists because the model is meant to write replies, not user messages. If every character were graded, the model would also practise writing the questions and markers. The user and the app supply those. That practice can then show up in the wrong place, as the lab shows.

When every character is graded, the templated model also practises guessing what comes after each `>`. In its training text, `<end>` is followed straight away by `<user>` or `<assistant>`. So it learns that the closing bracket is often followed by an opening one. A reply starts right after the `>` of `<assistant>`. So the model may open its reply with a marker instead of a word.

## How to play with it

The lab follows five steps. Each one below says what to change and what to watch.

### 1. Edit the template

Turn **System block**, **Role markers** and **End-of-turn marker** on and off one at a time. Watch the strip gain and lose structure. For example, turn off only **Role markers**, and the line becomes this:

```
<system>answer briefly and plainly.<end>when does the fog arrive the fog settles over the harbor in the morning.<end>
```

Now the question runs straight into the answer with a single space between them. Nothing shows where one ends and the other begins. Turn all three switches off, and the strip shows exactly the plain text that the plain model trains on.

Watch the count as well. With **End-of-turn marker** off, **Graded characters** drops from 52 to 47. The reply no longer has an `<end>` after it to grade.

### 2. Read the loss mask

With every switch back on, find `<assistant>` in the strip. You will see the graded cells begin right after it, at the `t` of "the".

**Try this:** Switch **Loss on** between `Assistant reply only` and `Every character`. Then read the three numbers under the strip. With the mask on, **Graded characters** reads 52 of 137. Beside it, **Mean −log p, graded** reads 2.11 nats, while **Mean −log p, context** reads 2.92 nats.

The label "−log p" is the lab's short way of writing the surprise score from earlier. The word "mean" means average. Here p is the chance the model gave the right character. Later you will see **log-prob**, written log p. It is the same number with the sign flipped, so it is negative.

A surprise of 2.11 matches a 1 in 8 chance for each right character. You can check this with the halving rule. Getting from 100% down to 1 in 8 takes three halvings. Three steps of 0.69 add up to about 2.1. A surprise of 2.92 is closer to 1 in 18. So the context characters are much harder for the model to predict. That makes sense. The model is never graded on them. So only the letter pairs they share with the replies get any practice.

Switch to `Every character`, and all 137 characters are graded. So **Mean −log p, context** reads "none", because no character is left ungraded. The graded average becomes 1.99 nats, which is close to a 1 in 7 chance. Set **Loss on** back to `Assistant reply only` before you move on. The numbers in the next steps assume the mask is on.

### 3. Fine-tune on your pairs

In **Supervised dataset editor**, each line is one example, written as `instruction | response`. The request goes to the left of the upright line `|`. The reply it should get goes to the right. Everything you type is made lowercase. Digits, commas and other symbols turn into spaces, and brackets are removed, because the models only use 30 characters.

The editor keeps up to 16 lines of 200 characters each. A line with no `|` drops out, which you can spot in the **Examples** count.

The **SFT epochs** slider sets how many times training works through all the examples. SFT is short for supervised fine-tuning, and one full pass through the examples is called an **epoch**. You can choose anywhere from 1 to 80 passes. Move the slider and watch the readout `“<” after the full stop` in **Two models, two formats**. It gives each model's chance of starting `<end>` after the reply's last full stop.

With the four starting pairs, the templated model gives that marker about a 6% chance at 4 epochs. At the default of 30 epochs, that rises to 54%, and at 80 epochs it reaches 88%. The end of a turn is learned gradually, like any other pattern in the examples.

The same card shows **Reply log-prob per char, templated**, the log-prob from step 2. So −2.228 at the defaults means an average surprise of 2.228 per reply character. Closer to zero is better. The score moves towards zero as the passes rise. At 4 epochs it is about −3.02, close to a 1 in 20 chance for each right character. At 80 epochs it reaches about −1.87, which is nearer to 1 in 6.

The chart above it shows each model's loss falling as training goes on. Its bottom axis counts **optimizer steps**. Each step is a single nudge to the table, based on a small batch of graded guesses. Each point on the chart averages a few steps.

The two training curves grade different characters. So the chart's own note warns that they do not rank the models. The same note gives the counts for one pass over the examples. The templated model gets 191 graded guesses, against 171 for the plain one. The extra 20 are the five characters of `<end>` at the end of each of the four replies.

**Graded targets**, in **Supervised dataset editor**, shows the templated count. It jumps to 528 when **Loss on** is set to `Every character`.

Before you move on, delete any line you added and set **SFT epochs** back to 30. The numbers in the next steps assume the four starting pairs at 30 passes.

### 4. Break the boundary

Turn **End-of-turn marker** off and keep the mask on. Then compare the two lists under **What follows the end of a turn**. Each list shows the model's five likeliest characters right after the full stop that ends the first reply. The templated model is on top, and the plain model is below.

With the marker on, the templated model's top bar is `<` at 54.4%, the first character of `<end>`. With it off, the upper list goes flat like the lower one. Every character, `<` included, gets 3.3%. So the list shows only the first five characters in order: space, a, b, c and d. The readout `“<” after the full stop` drops to 3% for both models. Turn the marker back on before the last step, since its numbers assume the full template.

### 5. Serve a different format

Using a trained model to answer prompts is called **serving** it. The card **Serve the trained models** does that with both of the lab's models. It sends them the first example, cut off right where the reply should begin. The box shows the exact string sent, with its last character highlighted. Each model reads only that last character, so that one character decides what each model does next.

**Try this:** Switch **Serve with** between `Chat template`, `No template` and `Wrong role marker`, and watch the readout **First reply character “t”, templated vs plain**. Under `Chat template`, the reply's first letter `t` gets 18% from the templated model and only 3% from the plain one. The plain model never saw a `>` in front of anything during training. So its `>` row was never trained and spreads its odds evenly.

The setting `No template` sends only the question and a space. The templated model then reads 22%, while the plain one reads 25%. The plain model does well here because, in its training, a single space sat right before every reply.

The setting `Wrong role marker` puts `<user>` where `<assistant>` belongs. You will find it gives exactly the same results as `Chat template`, and "What to notice" explains why. Serving a model with a layout that differs from the one it trained on is called a **template mismatch**.

Above the bars, each model also writes a short piece of text, called a **sample**. It picks one character at a time, and likelier characters get picked more often. A note under each sample says where a decoder watching for markers would stop.

Last, set **Loss on** to `Every character` and serve `Chat template` again. The templated model now puts 37% on `<` right after the closing `>`. Its sample opens with a marker instead of a reply. A decoder watching for markers would stop at once and leave an empty reply.

This happens because of the full template. Each training example has two places where a closing bracket is followed by `<`. They are inside `<end><user>` and `<end><assistant>`. Grading those characters taught the model that a bracket is often followed by another marker.

## What to notice

### Where a reply ends

- With the full template and the mask, the templated model puts 54.4% on `<` right after a reply's full stop. So its likeliest next step is to start `<end>`.
- The plain model gives every character 3.3% at that same spot. Its row for the full stop was never trained. In its training, nothing ever came after a reply's last full stop. So nothing in that row says to stop.
- Turn **End-of-turn marker** off with the mask still on, and the templated model drops to 3.3% too. The graded `<end>` was the only thing teaching it where a reply ends. The role markers cannot do that job, because they sit before the reply, where nothing is graded.
- Switch to `Every character` with the end marker still off. The templated model is back to 55% on `<`, even with no `<end>` in its data. It learned that from the system prompt, where "plainly." is followed by `<user>`. That boundary is one it was never meant to be graded on. The masked result is cleaner, because there the model learns where a reply ends from replies alone.
- A tall bar for the opening bracket means the model will probably start a marker. It does not mean the model will spell `<end>` correctly. The model still predicts the rest of the marker one character at a time.

### What the mask changes

- Under the mask, the context characters average 2.92 nats, against 2.11 for the graded reply. The context is the system prompt, the markers and the question. Nothing trains the model to predict them directly.
- The question still improves as a side effect. Its characters, spaces included, average about 2.50 nats, well below the 3.40 of a blank model. Letter pairs like "th" and "he" in "the fog" also appear in the replies. So the question uses rows that the replies trained.
- The point of the mask is where the training effort goes. It goes into writing replies, not into writing the user's side of the chat.

### What the template costs and buys

- The template costs the reply a little. **Reply log-prob per char, templated** reads −2.228. **Reply log-prob per char, plain** reads −2.168, which is slightly closer to zero.
- Both scores work out to roughly a 1 in 9 chance per character, so the gap is small. It has two causes in this tiny model.
- First, the graded `<end>` uses the same `e`, `n` and `d` rows as the reply's letters. Those rows split their odds between the marker and ordinary words.
- Second, the model guesses the reply's first letter from the `>` row. That row has had far less training than the space row the plain model uses. The `<assistant>` letters themselves are not graded under the mask, so they train nothing.
- With `Every character`, the gap grows to −2.425 against −2.129. Grading the prompt makes the templated model spend more of those shared rows on the markers.
- What the template buys in return is a known place where every turn starts and ends. The readout `“<” after the full stop` shows it: 54% for the templated model, against 3% for the plain one, which has never seen `<end>`.

### Serving a different format

- A plain-trained model served the chat template does not crash or show an error. It answers from a row that nobody trained, so all of its next-character bars sit flat at 3.3%.
- Taking the template away even raises the templated model's score for `t`. It goes from 18% to 22% with `No template`. In this toy, that is an accident of reading one character. Its space row was trained on the spaces between the replies' own words. It does not mean templates are optional.
- `Chat template` and `Wrong role marker` give identical bars and samples. Both strings end in `>`. A model that reads one character cannot tell `<user>` from `<assistant>`, while a model that reads the whole marker can.
- The samples look like scraps of words rather than sentences. A model that sees one character can only learn which letters tend to follow which. At the defaults, the templated sample writes a few scraps and reaches a full stop. Then it starts a marker.
- This readout, **Held-out reply, log-prob per char, templated vs plain**, scores a reply outside the training pairs. That reply is "the rain stops before noon." It reads −2.86 for the templated model and −2.87 for the plain one. Compare the templated −2.86 with −2.23, which **Reply log-prob per char, templated** gives the first trained reply.
- That gap is roughly the difference between 1 in 17 and 1 in 9 for each character. Scores on training data flatter a model. Grading yourself on the exact questions you practised would flatter you in the same way.

**A common mix-up:** The mask does not hide the question from the model. Masked characters are still read, and they are exactly what each next guess is based on. The mask only takes them out of the grade. In the worksheet picture, you still read each question to answer it. The teacher does not mark your copy of it.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Every number in this lab is really trained and measured, so none of it is made up. What is missing is size, and that limits what the numbers can mean.

- Each model looks at one character to guess the next. So "it knows the turn ended" only means that the row for `.` learned to point at `<`, the first character of `<end>`. A real model predicts a single stop symbol from the whole conversation.
- Four examples is not a dataset. Real fine-tuning uses thousands to millions of pairs.
- Both models start from blank tables, instead of from a model that pretraining has already taught.
- The markers are spelled in letters instead of being single reserved ids, so `<end>` shares rows with ordinary text.
- The system prompt is one fixed sentence, and the strip shows only the first example. Only the end of the first reply is probed.
- Serving is tested through one character only, because each model reads the last character of the served string. A template mismatch therefore shows up as one different row of the table.
- That means the real cost of a mismatch cannot appear here. A real model reads the whole unfamiliar prompt again at every step of its reply.
- Each sample picks characters at random, with likelier characters picked more often. But the lab reuses the same random numbers every time. So a sample changes only when the trained table or the served string changes. Either way, you see one possible reply rather than a typical one.
- The held-out reply is one sentence, so it does not tell you much on its own.
- The two reply scores cover the same characters and use the same count. That makes them fair to compare. They still come from two differently laid-out datasets. So treat the gap between them as a hint, not proof.

### Where real systems fail

Serving a model with the wrong template is a common failure, and it is silent. Nothing errors. The model receives a string unlike anything it trained on, and the quality of its answers sags.

Picture a student who practised for weeks with quizzes. Every question started with "Q:" and every answer space started with "A:". On test day the paper uses a new layout. The student still writes answers but makes more slips, and nothing on the paper says the layout caused them. A model served the wrong template is in the same spot. The difference is that it cannot notice the change at all.

A serving system that glues text together to build prompts can make a mistake. It can turn `<end>` into five ordinary characters instead of the reserved id. The two look identical in the printed record of what was sent, so the bug is hard to spot. But the model never receives the marker it was trained on.

Stopping also depends on the serving software, which keeps a list of stop signals to watch for. Say the stop id is missing from that list. Then nothing halts the reply when the marker appears, and the model runs on into an invented next turn.

There is a security side as well. A **system prompt** counts as instructions only because the template and training data agree to treat it that way. Say the software turns a marker that a user typed into the real marker id. Then the user's text can pretend to be a system turn.

Picture a group chat where messages from the admin appear in a special gold box. Say anyone could type a short code that puts their message in the gold box. Then the box would stop meaning anything. Reserved ids only protect a system prompt when the serving software keeps them out of user text.

## Going deeper

### How real templates ship

Real models ship their template as a small piece of code, stored in the settings of the **tokenizer**. The tokenizer is the part that cuts text into tokens. The software that runs the model uses the template to lay out every conversation. To start a reply, the template also adds the opening assistant marker after your message. That way the model starts writing in the right place.

A brand-new marker needs a new token id. It also needs a new row of numbers inside the model for that id. So it changes the size of the model itself.

### Masking is a choice

Most fine-tuning recipes grade only the assistant's turns. But some research finds that grading the instructions too can help when replies are short and examples are scarce. So the mask is a setting to test, not a law.

### What the examples teach

The **superficial alignment hypothesis** is an idea about what this kind of training really does. Here "alignment" means getting a model to behave the way people want when it talks with users. A hypothesis is an idea that researchers propose and then test. This one says that a model learns nearly all its knowledge and skills during pretraining. Later tuning mostly teaches it which format and style of reply to use.

The idea comes from a 2023 paper by Zhou and colleagues. It is called LIMA, short for "Less Is More for Alignment". They fine-tuned a large model called LLaMA, which has about 65 billion adjustable numbers. They used only 1,000 carefully chosen pairs of prompts and replies. For comparison, each model in this lab has 900 adjustable numbers.

Smaller tests on a model with 7 billion numbers pointed the same way. The researchers trained it on 2,000 examples from the Stack Exchange question-and-answer sites, picked only from good-quality posts. That raised a helpfulness score by about half a point on a six-point scale. The comparison was 2,000 examples picked without that quality check. Doubling the amount of data did not make the replies better.

The scores in those tests were given by another AI model, ChatGPT, rather than by people. So treat the results as evidence for the idea about format and style. They are not proof that fine-tuning adds no knowledge.

### Conversations with many turns

The lab's examples each have one question and one answer, but real chats go back and forth. In a real chat the template repeats: a user block, an assistant block, then a user block again. The masking rule stretches to fit. Training treats the whole conversation as one string. Every assistant turn is graded, and every user turn is read as context.

**Multi-turn data** means training conversations with several turns each. It matters because a model tuned only on single questions can lose the thread. In the LIMA paper, one model was tuned on 1,000 examples that each had a single turn. That model stopped following the prompt within three exchanges in 6 of the 10 test chats.

Adding only 30 multi-turn conversations raised the share of excellent replies from 45.2% to 76.1%. That is a jump from fewer than half of the replies to about three in every four.

### Packing

Real models read text in windows of a fixed length, called the **context window**. A short example leaves most of the window empty. That space is filled with blank filler called padding, which the computer still has to work through. **Packing** glues several short examples into one sequence to fill the window instead.

That can save a lot of work. Krell and colleagues found that up to half of the tokens in common training datasets can be padding. Packing needs care, though, because each example still needs its own end marker. The model must also be blocked from looking back across the join. Otherwise one example can read the one before it as if they were one conversation.

In this lab every example is its own sequence, so nothing is packed. No guess ever crosses from one example into the next.
