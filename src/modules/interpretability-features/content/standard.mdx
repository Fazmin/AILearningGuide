## The big picture

This module is about naming the parts of a transformer layer's activation, and how far such a name
can be trusted. Neurons are **polysemantic**, responding to unrelated things, because of
**superposition**: a layer packs more features than it has dimensions onto nearly orthogonal
directions. A **sparse autoencoder** (SAE), a separate model, rewrites an activation as a few active
features out of a much wider dictionary; each feature's label is a hypothesis to test, not a
finding.

**The transformer block** explained the role of the residual stream, a running sum that every
sublayer adds to; this lab's SAE reads it after the last block. Like the VAE in **Diffusion & VAEs**, an SAE encodes and reconstructs, but its code is wider
than its input and almost all zeros.

### Where it shows up

- Anthropic amplified a Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude 3 Sonnet, and its replies to most
  queries mentioned the bridge, even when irrelevant.
- **Auto-interpretability** has a language model read a feature's top examples, propose a label,
  and score it by how well it predicts activations on held-out text.
- On the AxBench benchmark, steering with SAE features was not competitive with prompting (Wu et
  al., 2025); a later paper reports that a supervised pipeline for choosing and labeling SAE
  features gets close to a fine-tuned reference there.

### Why it matters

Superposition makes the model's own units unreadable, so without a decomposition there is no part
with a single meaning to name. With one, each kind of evidence for a label can mislead in its own way:

- Top examples can come from the first characters of the model's window, where the residual
  depends on almost no context, and describe the window start rather than language.
- A feature whose label fits every top example can still fire on many characters the label does
  not describe.
- Any added vector shifts the output, so a steering effect counts only when it beats a **null
  control**: a random direction of the same length.

### What you will be able to explain

- Superposition: why neurons end up polysemantic, and how a sparse autoencoder extracts a few
  active features from a wider dictionary.
- Labels as hypotheses: how to check where a feature's examples sit in the model's window, and how
  to score its label on new text as precision and recall at a chosen threshold.
- Steering: why adding a feature's direction is a causal test of its label, and why the effect
  counts only when it beats a random direction of the same length.

### What the lab shows

The lab runs the shipped 1.4-million-parameter character transformer and its SAE through ONNX.
**Residual to sparse code** and **Sparsity and superposition** compute the residual, its 32 active
features of 1,024, the unexplained share, the **Keep top k features** curve and the decoder
overlaps from those weights and your **Probe text**; only the SAE's training curve is logged.

**Feature evidence** reads fixed top examples, cached for 128 features, each with its window
position. It scores author-written rules such as `Space` on your text at the **Activation
threshold**: precision is the share of firings that match, recall the share of matches that fire.

The key experiment is **Steer with a feature**, for five features whose labels measured cleanly.
**Steering strength** adds a feature's decoder direction at **Steering position**, and the change in
the label character's log probability is set against 20 seeded random directions, one of them
shown by **Random control direction**. **Continue** writes greedy text three ways: no push, the
feature, and that random direction.

## What it is

The obvious way to understand a network would be to read its neurons: inspect what activates unit
1,432, name it, repeat. This fails quickly, because individual units respond to collections of
apparently unrelated things. They are **polysemantic**, and that is the normal case rather than an
anomaly.

### Why that happens: superposition

A layer has a fixed number of dimensions, and there are far more distinguishable features than
that. A model could represent at most `d` features on orthogonal directions. Instead it represents
many more on *almost*-orthogonal directions that overlap slightly.

This works because features are sparse: almost none are present in any single input, so the
interference between overlapping directions rarely matters. **Superposition** is a rational use of
a dimension budget, and it is exactly what makes single units unreadable.

### The proposed fix: sparse autoencoders

A **sparse autoencoder** (SAE) unpacks the overlap. It is a separate model trained on recorded
activations, here the transformer's residual stream after its last block, which it rewrites as a
few active features out of many more, then reconstructs. The transformer itself is untouched.

The shipped SAE, from `train_sparse_autoencoder.py`:

- normalizes each 256-number residual with a corpus mean and one global scale;
- encodes it as `ReLU(W_enc x + b_enc)` over 1,024 features, then keeps only the 32 largest (TopK);
- decodes with `x̂ = W_dec f`, where each feature's direction has unit length;
- trains on **reconstruction loss** plus a small L1 **sparsity penalty** (×0.002), for 10,000
  steps on 1,600 windows of the corpus's training split.

This is **dictionary learning** applied to a model's internals.

### Three questions about one feature

The lab asks them in order, and each one can come back "no":

- **Where do its examples come from?** Every cached example records its position in the model's
  64-character window. The first positions are not running text.
- **Does its label survive new text?** Score the label as precision and recall at a threshold.
- **Does turning it up do what the label says?** Add its direction to the residual and compare with
  a random direction of the same length.

## Why it’s here

An SAE is one way to ask what a layer represents, and its parts are only as meaningful as their
answers to the three questions above. A good reconstruction answers none of them: it says the code rebuilds the
vector, not that its parts are the model's own.

## What to notice

### Reconstruction

- At the first space of the default text, 32 of 1,024 features are active and 1.0% of ‖x‖² is left
  unexplained. Keeping only the largest feature leaves 53.5%; keeping 8 leaves 4.6%.
- The share is uneven: from 0.0% at position 0 to 22.3% at the K of KING, 7.0% on average. On 200
  held-out windows the SAE leaves 5.1% unexplained.
- The curve is not strictly falling. At the first space the 29th feature nudges the share from
  1.00% to 1.03%, because the code was fitted as a whole and not to be rebuilt in order.

### Overlap

- The 1,024 decoder directions average |cos| 0.069: nearly, not exactly, orthogonal.
  Superposition, measured in the dictionary.
- The largest overlap is 0.992, between `#108` and `#499`, and its cosine is **negative**. They
  point almost exactly opposite ways. Seven pairs exceed 0.9, and all seven are negative.
- On 768 held-out characters, `#108` and `#499` are never active on the same character, and one of
  the two is active on 734 of them.
- They split the text between them. From position 4 on, `#499` is active on 101 of the 111 spaces
  and `#108` on 6; on the 181 lowercase vowels it reverses, `#108` on 132 and `#499` on 41.

That reads as a **sign split**. The encoder passes values through a ReLU, so an activation is never
negative. A direction in the residual that takes both signs needs one feature for each side. It is
not feature splitting, which needs a wider dictionary than this lab trains.

### Where examples come from

- The cache flags 21 of its 128 features as **window-start artefacts**, 18 of them position-0
  artefacts. None of the 1,024 features fires only at position 0, but 24 are dominated by it.
- The top-ranked feature, `#876` (maximum 56.9), is flagged. Its eight top examples sit at window
  positions 1 and 2, so `First 4 characters` matches 8 of 8 of them.
- It is not simply a first-character feature. `#876` is active on 31.6% of scanned characters, and
  its largest example from position 4 on is 36.3, at position 19. On held-out passages it averages
  13.3 at position 1 and 0.64 from position 8 on. The residual itself is large at the start: its
  length, in the SAE's normalized units, averages 32.5 at position 0 and 21.5 at position 1, against 15.0
  from position 4.
- At the first space, the second-largest feature, `#499`, carries the same flag.

### Scoring labels

- `#121` looks like "a space". On the default text, `Space` at 5.0 scores 100% precision and 70%
  recall; at 2.0 it scores 90% and 90%.
- On 768 held-out characters the trade is clear: 96% precision and 91% recall at 5.0, 63% and 97%
  at 2.0, 100% and 56% at 8.0. Lowering the threshold lets false alarms in.
- `#511`'s eight top examples are commas, at about 14. At 5.0 on held-out text it fires on 18 of
  19 commas and on 102 other characters: 15% precision. The label looked perfect from the top
  examples and is poor on typical text.
- An excerpt shows every line break as a space. `#53`'s eight examples all show a space, yet `Space`
  scores 0% precision and 0% recall at 5.0, while `Line break` scores 41% precision and 62% recall.

### Steering

- `#160` fires on hyphens. At position 43 and strength 10, a hyphen goes from 1.3% to 43.0% and
  becomes the top choice. Its log probability rises by 3.53; twenty random directions of the same
  length move it by −0.53 to +1.36. The continuation is a row of hyphens; the random control writes
  ` where is no`.
- At strength 5, `#160` raises a hyphen's log probability more than any of the 1,024 decoder
  directions: +2.09, against +1.68 for the next.
- `#683` fires on y, yet raising it lowers y (0.55% to 0.14% at strength 5) and raises o most. It
  ranks 1,011th of 1,024 directions for raising a y. The directions that raise y most are `#167`,
  `#902` and `#737`, whose cached examples are b, m and t.
- `#122`, which fires on m, changes log p(m) by +0.39, against a random maximum of +0.37: inside
  the spread, so it says nothing about the label.

## Where it breaks

### In this lab

- One SAE, trained once, on one layer of a 1.4-million-parameter character model. Labels were
  written by this module's author and measured on 768 held-out characters; that is a small test set.
- What the cache guarantees: every example carries its window position; flags are computed over
  1,800 scanned windows; and features with early examples get an in-context list. It does not
  guarantee that an unflagged feature is a good one: `#511` is unflagged and a poor comma label.
- Only 128 of 1,024 features have cached examples, picked for large activations, not at random.
  Excerpts show line breaks as spaces.
- The probe text is one window starting at position 0, so its first characters are window-start
  positions whatever you type.
- Steering here is a direct effect only. The SAE reads the stream after the last block, so the added
  vector acts through the final normalization and output matrix, at one position, and no later layer
  can transform it. Twenty random directions are a rough, easy control, and five features were
  picked because their labels measured cleanly.
- A feature's activation is a correlation with the text. Nothing here shows that the transformer
  uses a feature to decide anything; the steering card tests one direct effect at the last layer,
  not a mechanism.
- The top-k curve truncates a trained 32-feature code; an SAE trained with a smaller k would learn
  different features.

### In real systems

An SAE trained with a different random seed finds different features: in one large Llama 3 8B
setting only about 30% were shared across seeds (Paulo and Belrose, 2025).

**Feature splitting** means the number of features found depends on the dictionary width chosen.
**Dead features** never activate; this SAE has none among 1,800 scanned windows. **Absorption**
makes a feature fail to fire where it should because a more specific one has taken over (Chanin et
al., 2024).

More fundamentally, a good sparse reconstruction does not prove the SAE found the model's own basis.
On the AxBench benchmark, steering with SAE features was not competitive with prompting (Wu et al.,
2025). A later paper reports that a supervised pipeline for choosing and labeling features gets
close to a fine-tuned reference there (Jørgensen and Hansen, arXiv 2605.31183).

## Going deeper

### Choices of sparsity

An L1 penalty shrinks active values and biases reconstructions. TopK, as here, fixes the count
directly: exactly 3.125% of features were active at every logged step, and the L1 term added only
9.7 × 10⁻⁵ to a final loss of 0.0471. JumpReLU variants learn a threshold. Each gives a different
dictionary from identical activations.

### From labels to causal tests

**Auto-interpretability** has a language model read top examples and propose a label, then scores
the label by how well it predicts activations on held-out text. That is still correlational. The
causal test is a **steering vector**: add the feature's direction during generation and check
whether behavior changes as the label predicts.

Anthropic did this at scale. Amplifying a Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude 3 Sonnet made its
replies to most queries mention the bridge, even when irrelevant.

The check that matters is the **null control**: how much does a random direction of the same length
change the output? Without it, a change cannot be pinned on the feature. This lab's `#683` shows
the other trap: a label describes where a feature fires, and what adding it writes can be
something else.

### Beyond one layer

Transcoders approximate a dense MLP layer with a wider, sparsely active one (Dunefsky et al., 2024).
Crosscoders learn features shared across layers or across models (Anthropic, 2024). The next lab, on
circuits, asks what these components *do*. Every label, here and in the literature, is a hypothesis
with evidence, not a finding.
