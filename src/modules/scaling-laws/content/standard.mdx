## The big picture

This module is about scaling laws: empirical curve fits that predict a language model's loss from
its parameter count `N` and its number of training tokens `D`. They answer a budgeting question.
With a fixed amount of training compute, how large should the model be, how many tokens should it
see, and what loss should it reach?

**Training dynamics** followed loss within one run; a scaling law is fitted to the measured
losses of many runs. **The transformer block** counted one block's weights, which this module
scales up to a whole model's `N`, and **Inference & KV cache** showed the serving cost that a
training-only optimum leaves out.

Compute is counted in **FLOPs**, floating-point operations. An **isoFLOP curve** holds the compute
budget fixed and trades model size against tokens; its lowest point is the **compute-optimal**
split.

### Where it shows up

- **The model-to-data ratio.** Hoffmann et al. (2022), the Chinchilla paper, found that model size
  and data should grow roughly in proportion; their headline rule of thumb is about 20 training
  tokens per parameter. The earlier fits of Kaplan et al. (2020) had favored growing model size
  much faster than data.
- **Models built to be served.** Labs deliberately **overtrain** smaller models, training them on
  far more tokens than the compute-optimal count, because smaller models are cheaper to serve.
  Roughly 8B parameters on 15T tokens is Llama 3 8B's recipe.
- **Other axes.** Mixture-of-experts models use only some of their parameters for each token, so
  their fits separate active from total parameters. **Test-time compute**, spent at answer time on
  sampling, longer reasoning or verification, has its own scaling behavior. Scaling fits also
  track how the best learning rate and batch size shift with compute.

### Why it matters

Training compute is roughly proportional to parameters times tokens, so on a fixed budget a bigger
model sees fewer tokens. Lean too far either way and loss rises: a large model is starved of
tokens, and a small one is limited by its size.

The fit also makes returns predictable. Once the **irreducible loss**, a fitted floor that no
amount of scale removes, is subtracted, the compute-optimal frontier is a power law: each tenfold
budget removes the same fraction of the reducible loss, the loss above that floor. The fitted
constants hold only for the architecture, data mix, tokenizer, optimizer and schedule they were
fitted with, and a lower loss does not guarantee a better score on a given task.

### What you will be able to explain

- Read the compute-optimal frontier as a power law: a straight line on log-log axes once the floor
  is removed, with steady and slow returns.
- Describe the compute-optimal balance of model size and data, and why a bigger model is not
  automatically better at fixed compute.
- Estimate `N` from a transformer's layer count and width, and say what that estimate leaves out:
  embeddings, norm scales and the exact MLP shape.

### What the lab shows

Nothing is trained. Every readout is closed-form arithmetic: a published loss fit, `C = 6ND`, the
ratio `D/N`, or `12·L·d²`. **Fit** chooses `Hoffmann 2022`, the constants Hoffmann et al.
printed, or `Epoch 2024 refit`, Besiroglu et al.'s refit of them. The Chinchilla and Gopher crosses
are the fit's predictions for their sizes, not their reported results, and most of the (N, D)
plane the sliders reach is extrapolation beyond the fitted runs.

On **Scaling law controls**, **Parameters** and **Training tokens** set `N` and `D`. **IsoFLOP
curves** draws one thin U-shaped curve per budget, each with a small dot at its minimum. The thick
curve is your own budget: your run is the filled circle on it, a diamond marks the fit's optimum
and a square the 20:1 split. The card reads out **Predicted loss**, **Compute C = 6ND**, **Tokens
per parameter** and **Gap to optimum**.

The key experiment sets **Budget** to `Hold compute` and drags **Parameters**, so the filled
circle slides along the thick curve until **Gap to optimum** reads `0.000` at the diamond. There
the two doubling readouts on **Loss breakdown** nearly match: doubling either resource buys about
the same.

**Frontier power law** plots loss above the floor against compute on log-log axes and reads out
its **Slope**. **N from dimensions** turns **Layers L** and **Width d** into a parameter count,
which **Use as Parameters** puts on the **Parameters** slider, clamped to its 100M to 1T range.

## What it is

Scaling laws are empirical curve fits. Train many models at different sizes on different amounts
of data, measure their loss, and fit a formula. The fit then predicts the loss of runs you have
not done.

The lab uses the parametric form from Hoffmann et al. (2022), the Chinchilla paper:

```text
L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β
```

`N` is parameter count and `D` is training tokens, both as raw counts. `E` is the **irreducible
loss**, a floor no amount of scale removes. The two power-law terms shrink with size and with
data. In the printed fit, `E = 1.69`, `A = 406.4`, `B = 410.7`, `α = 0.34` and `β = 0.28`, with
loss in nats per token on the paper's own data.

### Why a budget must be split

Training compute is approximately `C ≈ 6ND` FLOPs for a dense transformer: about 2 operations per
parameter per token in the forward pass and 4 in the backward pass. Because the product `ND` is
what costs money, a fixed budget is one **isoFLOP curve**. Along it, a bigger model means fewer
tokens.

Each isoFLOP curve is U-shaped. Too small a model and the size term dominates. Too large and the
data term dominates. The bottom is the **compute-optimal** split, and at that point the marginal
returns match: `α·A/N^α = β·B/D^β`.

### The ratio that changed

The first influential fits (Kaplan et al., 2020) concluded that model size should grow much faster
than data. Hoffmann et al. used learning-rate schedules matched to each run's length and found the
two should grow roughly in proportion. Their headline recipe, about **20 tokens per parameter**,
implied that the largest models of the time were undertrained.

## Why it’s here

Scaling laws are why large training runs can be planned at all. You cannot afford to try a
frontier configuration twice, so the configuration is chosen by fitting curves on small runs and
extrapolating to the large one.

They also explain the shape of the industry. Steady, predictable, sublinear returns justify large
capital spending in a way that unpredictable returns would not.

## How to play with it

Start on **IsoFLOP curves**. The default run is 3.2B parameters on 28B tokens: **Predicted loss**
2.416 at 5.4 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, only 8.8 tokens per parameter. The diamond on your curve is the fit's
optimum at the same compute, 1.38B on 65B tokens at 2.393.

### Two failures on one curve

Set **Parameters** to `10B` and **Training tokens** to `2B`: loss 2.873. Then set `200M` and
`100B`: loss 2.643. Both runs cost 1.2 × 10²⁰ FLOPs, so they sit on the same curve, on opposite
sides of its minimum at 2.575.

### Walk the curve

Switch **Budget** to `Hold compute` and drag **Parameters**. Now the other slider moves with it,
the dot slides along one curve, and **Gap to optimum** falls to `0.000` at the diamond. **Loss
breakdown** shows why: `Doubling N lowers L by` and `Doubling D lowers L by` meet there.

### Build N from dimensions

Under **N from dimensions**, set **Layers L** and **Width d**. The defaults, 32 layers of width
4,096, give 12 × 32 × 4,096² = 6.44B weights. Press **Use as Parameters** to put the slider there.
Then double **Width d**: N becomes 25.8B, fourfold. Double **Layers L** instead and it is 12.9B,
twofold.

## What to notice

- The bottoms are flat. At the default budget the 20:1 square is 0.006 above the diamond while
  using a 54% larger model. Roughly right matters; exactly right barely does.
- The bottleneck moves rather than disappearing. Push one resource and the other term becomes the
  one worth buying.
- Loss has a floor. Even at 1T parameters and 30T tokens the printed fit predicts 1.793 against
  `E = 1.69`.
- N follows the square of the width but only the first power of the layer count. Doubling the width
  quadruples N; doubling the layers doubles it.
- On **Frontier power law**, `L − E` along the frontier is a straight line on log-log axes with
  slope −0.154. Each tenfold budget keeps 70% of the reducible loss.

## Where it breaks

**In this lab.** Nothing is trained. Every readout is the published formula evaluated in closed
form, and the crosses for Chinchilla and Gopher are the fit's predictions for their sizes, not
their reported results. The sliders reach 1T parameters and 30T tokens, far beyond the fitted runs
(about 70M to 16B parameters, 5B to 500B tokens), so most of the plane is extrapolation.

### The fit and the rule of thumb disagree

Under the printed constants, the optimum at Chinchilla's own budget (5.76 × 10²³ FLOPs) is 32B
parameters on 3.0T tokens, about 93 tokens per parameter, not 70B on 1.4T. Besiroglu et al. (2024)
found the printed estimates inconsistent with the paper's other two methods. Their refit, the
**Fit** option `Epoch 2024 refit`, puts that optimum at 72B on 1.33T, about 18 per parameter.

The exponents are fragile too. With the more precise α = 0.3392 and β = 0.2849 listed in that
replication, the same optimum moves to about 40B. Small changes in α and β move large-scale
predictions a lot.

### Limits of the mechanism

Every term is conditional on architecture, data mix, tokenizer, optimizer and schedule. Loss is not
capability: a smooth loss curve can sit under a metric that jumps, and much of the "emergent
ability" literature turns on whether the metric is continuous or thresholded. The token supply is
finite: Muennighoff et al. (2023) found about four epochs of repeated data nearly as good as fresh
data, with value decaying after that.

## Going deeper

### From dimensions to N

The fit's `N` is a parameter count, and for a dense transformer two numbers nearly set it: the layer
count `L` and the width `d`. **The transformer block** counts a block's weights as `4d²` for
attention (queries, keys, values, output) and `8d²` for a classic MLP, so `12d²` per block and
`12·L·d²` for the model, with embeddings left out. Its toy block has `d` = 8: 12 × 8² = 768
weights, or 840 with biases and norm parameters.

The same formula works at scale. LLaMA's 6.7B model has 32 layers of width 4,096 (Touvron et al.,
2023, Table 2), so 12 × 32 × 4,096² = 6.44B, about 4% under the paper's count. Its 65.2B model has
80 layers of width 8,192, giving 64.4B, about 1% under. The shortfall is what the rule leaves out:
embeddings, norm scales and the exact MLP shape.

Embeddings matter more for small models. GPT-2 small has 12 layers of width 768 and a 50,257-token
vocabulary (its Hugging Face configuration): the blocks hold 12 × 12 × 768² = 84.9M weights and the
token table alone holds 50,257 × 768 = 38.6M. The blocks grow with `d²` and the table only with `d`,
so the share falls as models widen.

Which count a fit uses is part of the fit. Kaplan et al. fitted non-embedding parameters and
Hoffmann et al. total parameters, and Pearce and Song (2024) attribute much of the gap between the
two fits to that choice. Both fits in this lab are of Hoffmann et al.'s form.

**Inference & KV cache** uses the same two numbers from the serving side. Its cache holds `2 ×
layers × kv_heads × head_dim` values per token, which is `2·L·d` when every query head has its own
key and value head. So layers and width set both what a model costs to train and what it costs to
serve.

### Overtraining and serving

Compute-optimal is optimal for training only. A model served to millions of users spends more on
inference than on training, so labs deliberately **overtrain** smaller models. Set 8B parameters on
15T tokens, roughly Llama 3 8B's recipe: the printed fit charges 0.026 extra loss against a 36B
optimum, and the refit 0.054 against an 81B one, for a model several times cheaper to serve.

**Inference & KV cache** showed where that serving cost comes from: every generated token re-reads
the weights and the cache, and that bill recurs on every answer.

Mixture-of-experts models scale along a third axis: total parameters can grow while per-token
compute stays fixed, so fits separate active parameters, total parameters and expert count.

**Test-time compute** — sampling several candidates, longer reasoning, or verification — has its
own scaling behavior and trades against training compute for a target accuracy. Scaling fits are
also used to set hyperparameters, by fitting how the best learning rate and batch size shift with
compute, or by using a parametrization such as μP that keeps them stable as width grows.
