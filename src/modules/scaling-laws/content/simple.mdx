## The big picture

The AI models behind chatbots learn by reading huge amounts of text. Training a large one costs so much computing power that a team gets only one try. Before the run starts, someone must make two choices that cannot be undone. One is how big the model will be, and the other is how much text it will learn from. This module is about the patterns that guide those two choices. They also predict how good the model will be before anyone pays for it.

These patterns are called **scaling laws**. Researchers found them by training hundreds of models of different sizes on different amounts of text. They then scored how far off each model's guesses were. The scores follow smooth curves that one formula can describe. So a team can test ideas on small models and read the curve forward to plan a big one.

The chatbots you may have used were planned with the help of patterns like these. In 2022, a study that trained a model called Chinchilla suggested something. Many of the biggest models of the time had read too little text for their size. Today, many widely used models are deliberately made smaller than the formula's best choice. They learn from extra text instead, because a smaller model costs less every time it answers you.

Why does this matter? Without scaling laws, picking a model's size would be a very expensive guess. Nobody could tell in advance which way of splitting the budget would work best. Put too much into size and the model is starved of text. Put too much into text and the model is too small to use it all. Either mistake is locked in once the run ends, and the money spent on it is gone.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain:

- Why the best results form a straight line on a special graph once you remove a floor. The score never drops below that floor, and the line means slow, steady progress.
- How to split a fixed budget between model size and training text so that neither one is wasted.
- How to estimate a model's size from its layer count and width, and what that estimate leaves out.

The lab never trains anything, because it runs a published formula. So every number you see is a prediction. You set a model size and an amount of text on the **Scaling law controls** card. Three more cards then show what the formula predicts for that choice:

- **IsoFLOP curves** shows how good every possible split of one budget would be, and where the best split sits.
- **Loss breakdown** shows which part of the formula is holding the score up, the model's size or its text.
- **Frontier power law** shows the best result at every budget, lined up on that special graph.

The controls card also holds a small calculator called **N from dimensions**. It counts a model's size from its shape.

## What it is

### A library on a fixed budget

Picture a town that has saved up enough money to build one library, with no second chance. If it spends most of the money on shelves, it gets a huge building with almost nothing to read. If it spends most of the money on books, they end up stacked on the floor. Somewhere between those two mistakes sits the split that gives the best library for that budget.

Training a language model, the kind of AI behind chatbots, involves the same kind of choice. The model's size plays the part of the shelves, and its training text plays the part of the books. Both are paid from one bill for computing power. A **scaling law** is a formula that predicts how good the finished model will be. It works for any split you choose.

### Three words you need first

The lab's labels lean on three ideas, so let's pin them down before going further.

A model is a huge collection of adjustable numbers. Training nudges each one, over and over, so the model's guesses get better. Each of those numbers is a **parameter**, also called a weight. The letter N stands for how many a model has, which is its **parameter count**. The lab writes big counts with a letter on the end. M is for million, B is for billion and T is for trillion. So `3.2B` means 3.2 billion parameters.

Models read text in small pieces called **tokens**. A token is usually a whole word or part of one. The letter D counts the tokens a model reads in training. So `28B` means 28 billion pieces of text, far more than anyone could read in a lifetime.

During training, the model guesses each next token and gets a score for how far off its guess was. That score is called **loss**, and lower is better. The lab measures loss in **nats** per token. A nat is a unit of surprise. It grows when the model gave the real next token a low chance. For a sense of scale, imagine a model that gave the correct token a 1-in-11 chance every time. It would score about 2.4 nats per token.

A model that was sure of the right token, and correct, would score 0. A 1-in-2 chance scores about 0.7. Each time the chance the model gives the right token halves, the loss rises by about 0.7 nats. So the differences you will meet in this lab are small but real edges. Often they are only a few hundredths of a nat.

### The bill multiplies

Here the library picture needs a fix. A library's bill adds the cost of shelves to the cost of books. A model's bill multiplies size by text. It multiplies because every token of training text has to pass through every parameter. For each token, each parameter takes part in about 6 small calculations. Two happen while the model makes its guess, and 4 happen while training works out how to adjust it.

Each small calculation is one step of arithmetic on decimal numbers, called a floating-point operation. So training cost is counted in **FLOPs**, short for floating-point operations. The total cost of a run is called its compute, written C. It is about 6 × N × D, which the lab shows as **Compute C = 6ND**.

Here is the bill for the run the lab starts on, which has 3.2B parameters and 28B tokens. Multiply 6 × 3.2 billion × 28 billion and you get about 5.4 × 10²⁰ FLOPs. Here 10²⁰ means a 1 followed by 20 zeros. In other words, the run takes roughly 540 billion billion small calculations.

Because the bill multiplies, a fixed budget fixes the product N × D. A model twice as big can read only half as many tokens for the same money. That trade is the whole puzzle this lab is about.

### The formula: a floor plus two shrinking parts

The formula comes from those hundreds of training runs. Researchers measured each model's loss and then found the formula that best matched the results. It is a bit like drawing the line that best fits a scatter of dots on a graph. The lab prints that formula at the top of its **IsoFLOP curves** card:

```text
L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β
```

Here L is the predicted loss, and N and D are the parameter and token counts from before. The letters E, A, B, α and β stand for numbers the researchers chose so that the formula matches their results. Choosing numbers this way is called fitting, and one chosen set of numbers is called a **fit**. The sign `^` means "raised to the power", so `N^α` is N raised to the power α.

The lab offers two fits under **Fit**. The main one, `Hoffmann 2022`, is the set printed in the 2022 Chinchilla paper. Each of the formula's three parts has its own job:

- `E` is a floor that no amount of size or text gets below. It is called the **irreducible loss**, and in the lab's main fit it is 1.69.
- `A/N^α` is the **size term**. It shrinks as the model gets bigger, because a bigger model picks up more patterns from the same text.
- `B/D^β` is the **data term**. It shrinks as the model reads more tokens, because more text gives it more examples to learn from.

**Loss breakdown** works the formula out for your run. At the starting run of 3.2B parameters and 28B tokens, it shows this line:

```text
= 1.69 + 0.238 + 0.488 = 2.416
```

The size term adds 0.238 on top of the floor. The data term adds 0.488, about twice as much. Together they make the total, the **Predicted loss**. The lab also works out that doubling the text here would lower the loss by 0.086. Doubling the size would lower it by only 0.050. So this model is short of text more than it is short of size.

The Greek letters α (alpha) and β (beta) are powers, like the 2 in a square, but they are small. They are 0.34 and 0.28 in the main fit. A power of one half is the same as taking a square root. A power of 0.34 grows even more slowly than that.

Here is what that means for a model ten times bigger. N^α grows by 10 to the power 0.34, which is only about 2.2. Because N^α sits on the bottom of the fraction, the size term gets divided by 2.2. At the starting run, that takes the size term from 0.238 down to about 0.109. Another tenfold step takes it to about 0.050. So the second step removes less than half as much as the first, at ten times the price. Ten times more tokens works the same way, but it divides the data term by only about 1.9. That is why each improvement costs more than the one before.

The floor needs care, because people often describe it as the truly unpredictable part of text. An example is the next word in a sentence where several words fit equally well. But E is a number the fit chose to match the results, and nobody measured it. So it also includes whatever this design of model, trained this way, never learns. A different design might have a lower floor. Within this formula, though, no run ever reaches E. Take the far end of both sliders, 1T parameters on 30T tokens. The formula predicts a loss of 1.793, which is still above the floor of 1.69.

The library picture stops matching in two places. Books sit on shelves as whole copies, but a model does not keep copies of its text. Training only adjusts its numbers to pick up patterns. Also, a library's hundredth shelf holds as many books as its first. In the formula, each extra step of size or text lowers the loss less than the last.

### The best split for one budget

Now put the bill and the formula together by fixing the budget and trying every model size. Each bigger model must read fewer tokens, so its size term falls while its data term rises. Plot predicted loss against model size and you get a U-shaped curve, which the lab calls an **IsoFLOP curve**. The name comes from "iso", which means "same". Every point on the curve costs the same number of FLOPs.

The lowest point of the U is the best split for that budget. A run sitting there is called **compute-optimal**. To the left of the bottom, the model is too small to make full use of its text. To the right, it is so big that it reads too little text to fill it.

At the bottom, doubling the size or doubling the text would each double the bill. Each would also lower the loss by about the same amount. Neither is a better buy than the other, so moving money from one to the other cannot help. That means neither resource is being wasted.

Notice that the bottom is where the two doubling gains match, not where the two terms are equal. Doubling the size cuts the size term by about 21%. Doubling the text cuts the data term by only about 18%, because α is bigger than β. So at the best split the data term is still a little bigger. At the starting budget's best split, the data term is 0.385 and the size term is 0.317. The bigger term is only a rough guide to what a model is short of. The doubling gains are the real test.

Here is one budget worked out. Two runs that each cost 1.2 × 10²⁰ FLOPs sit on the same U:

- **10B parameters on only 2B tokens:** This is a giant library with almost no books. Its predicted loss is 2.873.
- **200M parameters on 100B tokens:** This is a tiny library buried in books, and its predicted loss is 2.643.

The two runs sit on opposite sides of their curve's bottom, which reaches 2.575 at about 700M parameters. So each one wastes a good part of the same budget.

Divide the number of training tokens by the number of parameters and you get the **token-to-parameter ratio**. The lab shows it as **Tokens per parameter**. A low ratio means a big model starved of text. A very high one means a small model fed more text than it can use. A popular rule of thumb, the **Chinchilla ratio**, says to aim for about 20 tokens per parameter. It is named after the Chinchilla model. Its makers trained it with 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, exactly 20 for every parameter.

### A straight line on a special graph

Find the bottom of the U for every budget and join them up. You get the **compute-optimal frontier**, which is the lowest loss the formula allows at each budget. To show its shape, the lab uses a graph whose axes grow by multiplying instead of adding. Each equal step along an axis means ten times more. These are called **log-log axes**, from logarithm, a piece of math you will not need here. The IsoFLOP chart's size axis works the same way, running through 100M, 1B, 10B, 100B and 1T.

Now take the frontier's loss and subtract the floor E. What is left, written L − E, is the part that more compute can still remove. On log-log axes it falls along a perfectly straight line, which is the sign of a **power law**. In a power law, each tenfold step in compute multiplies the loss above the floor by the same fraction. Under the main fit, that fraction is about 70%.

Here is that rule at work along the frontier, read from the formula:

- **Start:** At a budget of 10²⁰ FLOPs, the best possible run sits 0.910 above the floor.
- **Ten times the budget:** At 10²¹ FLOPs, the gap comes down to 0.639. That is 70% of what was there.
- **Ten times again:** At 10²² FLOPs, the gap comes down to 0.449. That is once more 70% of the step before.

Why does that draw a straight line? The loss axis also grows by multiplying, so keeping 70% is the same-sized step down wherever you start. On the lab's chart, dropping from 2 to 1 is the same step as dropping from 0.2 to 0.1. Each one is a halving. Each tenfold budget is also the same-sized step across. The same step down for every step across is exactly what a straight line is. So a power law always draws one on these axes.

This is what steady but slow means. Every tenfold budget buys the same share of progress, with no sudden jumps or stalls. Yet that share is small. Two tenfold steps mean 100 times the compute. They keep 70% of 70%, which is 49%, a little under half. So halving the loss above the floor takes a bit less than 100 times the compute, about 90 times.

The floor has to come off first. With it left in, the loss bends and flattens as it nears E, as if progress were dying out. With the floor left in, the frontier losses at those three budgets are 2.600, 2.329 and 2.139. Each keeps about 90%, then 92%, of the one before, so the drops seem to fade. Yet the part that can still shrink keeps the same 70% every time. Only once E is removed does the straight line appear. That is why the lab's chart plots L − E instead of the loss itself.

### Counting N from a model's shape

The formula needs N. For the most common kind of language model, called a **transformer**, you can estimate N from the model's shape. A transformer is built as a stack of repeated blocks called layers. Inside each layer, every token is carried as a list of numbers. The length of that list is the model's width, written d. The number of layers, sometimes called its depth, is written L. (The letter L does double duty in this lab. Next to **Layers L** it counts layers, but in the formula it means loss.)

Each layer keeps its weights in grids of numbers, called matrices. A grid d numbers by d numbers holds d × d numbers, written d². For example, a grid 8 by 8 holds 64. The attention part of a layer, where tokens gather information from one another, has four of these square grids. So it holds 4 × d².

The feed-forward part works on each token by itself. It has two wide grids, one d by 4 × d and one 4 × d by d. Each of them holds 4 × d² numbers, so together they hold 8 × d². Adding 4 × d² and 8 × d² gives 12 × d² weights per layer. So the whole stack holds about 12 × L × d².

The lab's calculator starts at 32 layers of width 4,096. That is the shape of one of the LLaMA models. Each of those layers holds 12 × 4,096² weights, about 201 million. So 32 layers hold about 6.44B in total.

Because the width is squared, it adds to N far faster than the number of layers does. Double the width and every grid gets four times as many entries, so N grows fourfold, to 25.8B. Double the layers instead and you only get twice as many grids, so N doubles, to 12.9B.

This count is an estimate that leaves some things out. The biggest is the **embeddings**, a lookup table that turns each token into its starting list of numbers. It has one row of d numbers for every token in the model's vocabulary. The vocabulary is the full list of tokens the model can read. The count also skips small extras, such as the numbers each layer uses to shift and rescale its values. It assumes the feed-forward part is exactly four times as wide, too, which not every model follows. So a real model's total usually comes out a little higher. For small models it can be a lot higher, as Going deeper shows.

## Why it’s here

Picture a baker asked to make one enormous cake for a wedding, with enough ingredients for exactly one attempt. She cannot practise on the real thing. So she bakes small cakes at several sizes and writes down how the baking time grows with size. Then she draws a curve through her notes and reads it forward to plan the big cake.

The biggest AI training runs are planned the same way. Nobody can afford to try one of them twice. A team trains many small models, fits the scaling-law formula to them, and reads it forward. That way the team picks the budget, size, text and expected loss before the costly run begins.

Using a formula beyond the range it was measured on is called **extrapolation**. It is both the main use of scaling laws and their biggest assumption. Like the baker's curve, the pattern might not hold at a size nobody has tried.

The formula also settles a question that people have gotten wrong before, which is how to split the budget. An earlier study, from 2020, concluded that model size should grow much faster than the amount of text. The 2022 Chinchilla study made several changes. One was to match each run's learning-rate plan to how long that run would last. The learning-rate plan sets how the size of each adjustment step shrinks over time. That way every run, short or long, could finish as well as it was able to.

Compared fairly like this, the runs showed that size and text should grow at roughly the same pace. Going deeper shows that the two studies also counted parameters differently, which explains much of the gap too.

The two crosses on **IsoFLOP curves** show what that change meant for the biggest models of the time. Gopher had 280B parameters and read 300B tokens, while Chinchilla had 70B parameters and read 1.4T tokens. The two used similar amounts of compute. The main fit predicts that the smaller model comes out ahead. It read more text, and it scores 1.937 against 1.993 for Gopher.

Scaling laws also help explain the AI industry. A company will spend huge sums on a training run only if it can predict what the money buys. Returns that are steady and predictable, even if slow, justify that spending in a way that surprises could not.

## How to play with it

The **Scaling law controls** card holds everything you can change. It starts with **Parameters**, which runs from 100M to 1T, and **Training tokens**, which runs from 1B to 30T. Both sliders move by multiplying, like the log axes. So each notch along them is the same percentage change rather than the same amount. **Fit** picks which published set of numbers the formula uses. **Budget** decides whether the two sliders move on their own (`Free`) or together (`Hold compute`). Under the sliders, **Fit optimum here** names the best split at your current budget. It gives parameters, tokens and tokens per parameter.

### Read the starting run

Before you touch anything, read the four readouts under **IsoFLOP curves**:

- **Predicted loss:** This is 2.416, the average score per token that the formula expects for your run.
- **Compute C = 6ND:** This is 5.4 × 10²⁰ FLOPs. It is the size of the bill for training this model on this much text.
- **Tokens per parameter:** This is 8.8, well below the rule of thumb of 20. This model reads little text for its size.
- **Gap to optimum:** This is 0.023 nats. It shows how far your loss sits above the best split at the same budget.

Your run is the filled dot on the thick curve. The diamond on that same curve marks the formula's best split for the same budget. The legend calls it **Fit optimum, same C**. It sits at 1.38B parameters on 65B tokens, with a loss of 2.393. The hollow square marks the 20-tokens-per-parameter split, called **20 tokens/param, same C**. It sits at 2.12B parameters on 42.3B tokens.

**Loss breakdown** tells you which way to move. **Doubling N lowers L by** shows 0.050, while **Doubling D lowers L by** shows 0.086. So at this run more text is the better buy. The sentence under the readouts says the data term dominates.

### Change the size, change the budget

With **Budget** on `Free`, drag **Parameters** and leave **Training tokens** alone. Your dot moves across the thin curves, and the thick curve moves with it. That happens because a bigger model on the same text costs more compute, which is a different budget. Each thin curve is one fixed budget, labelled from 10¹⁹ to 10²⁵ FLOPs where it leaves the chart. The thick curve is always the budget of your current run.

### Feed the model more text

Bring **Parameters** back near its starting 3.2B. Then drag **Training tokens** upward while you watch **Loss breakdown**. The hatched bar is the data term and the solid bar is the size term. The hatched bar shrinks as the text grows.

Keep an eye on the two doubling readouts as well. At around 190B tokens, **Doubling N lowers L by** catches up with **Doubling D lowers L by**. Past that point it is the bigger of the two. The bottleneck has moved, so now the model is short of size rather than text. The bars themselves only cross later, past 360B tokens, because the size term shrinks faster with each doubling.

### Two bad splits on one curve

**Try this:** Set **Parameters** to `10B` and **Training tokens** to `2B`. **Predicted loss** reads 2.873, a huge library with almost no books. Now set `200M` and `100B`, and the loss is 2.643, a tiny library buried in books. **Compute C = 6ND** reads 1.2 × 10²⁰ FLOPs both times. So both dots sit on the same curve, on opposite sides of its low point. **Fit optimum here** tells you where that low point is, at about 700M parameters.

### Walk one curve

Switch **Budget** to `Hold compute`, and the box under it now says the budget is held. Moving one slider now moves the other, so your dot slides along one curve instead of jumping between them. Drag **Parameters** toward the diamond and watch **Training tokens** move the opposite way, while **Gap to optimum** shrinks. Stop when it reads `0.000`, because then you are on the diamond, the best split this formula allows.

Keep dragging past the diamond and the gap grows again. On the left of the diamond, the model is too small for its text. On the right, the model is so big that it is starving for text. Drag across the whole curve and watch the gap grow on both sides of the bottom.

Near the bottom, the two doubling readouts in **Loss breakdown** nearly match. Its sentence changes to say that doubling either resource buys about the same. Each slider stops where the other would run off its range, so the budget never changes by accident.

### Read the frontier

On **Frontier power law**, the dashed line is the compute-optimal frontier. It is drawn on log-log axes with the floor already removed. The dotted line follows the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule. Your dot sits on or above the dashed line. It sits higher the further your run is from its best split.

Read **Slope**, which shows −0.154, and **10× compute keeps**, which shows 70% of L − E. The frontier has its own power, which comes from combining α and β. The lab shows it as the slope, so it says how steeply the line falls. A minus sign on a power means dividing. Ten to the power −0.154 is 1 divided by ten to the power 0.154. Ten to the power 0.154 is about 1.43, and 1 ÷ 1.43 is about 0.70. That is where the 70% comes from.

Then switch **Fit** to `Epoch 2024 refit`. The line stays straight, but the slope steepens to −0.178. Ten to the power 0.178 is about 1.51, and 1 ÷ 1.51 is about 0.66. So each tenfold budget now keeps 66% of the loss above the floor, a slightly faster pace than before.

### Build N from dimensions

Find **N from dimensions** at the bottom of the controls card. **Layers L** runs from 1 to 128, and **Width d** runs from 64 to 16,384 in steps of 64. The line below them works out 12 × L × d² for you. At the starting 32 layers of width 4,096 it reads 6.44B weights. The **Use as Parameters** button moves the **Parameters** slider to that count.

**Budget** may still be on `Hold compute` from walking the curve, so switch it back to `Free` first. Under `Hold compute`, the button would also move **Training tokens** to keep the bill the same. It could even stop short of the count, because the held budget runs out of room first.

Press it, then double **Width d** to 8,192 and watch N become 25.8B, four times as many. Put the width back and double **Layers L** to 64 instead, and N becomes 12.9B, only twice as many. If a shape gives a count outside the slider's 100M to 1T range, the line says so. The button stops at the nearest end.

## What to notice

### The bottom of the U is flat

At the starting budget, the square for 20 tokens per parameter sits only 0.006 above the diamond. Yet it uses a model 54% bigger. Getting the balance roughly right matters a lot, but getting it exactly right barely matters. That flatness is also why the dotted 20:1 line and the dashed frontier nearly overlap on **Frontier power law**.

### The bottleneck moves instead of vanishing

Push one resource far enough and the other becomes the one worth buying. **Loss breakdown** shows this when its doubling readout takes the lead. You never escape both terms by piling everything onto one slider, because the term you ignore stops shrinking.

### Loss has a floor

Take the largest run the sliders allow, with 1T parameters and 30T tokens. The formula still predicts 1.793 against a floor of 1.69. The size and data terms keep shrinking toward zero as you add more. But E stays put however much you spend.

### Width grows N faster than depth

N follows the square of the width but only the plain count of layers. So widening a model adds parameters much faster than adding layers does. Doubling the width multiplies N by four, while doubling the layers multiplies it by two. This is only about the count. It says nothing about whether a wide model or a deep one learns better.

### Straight, steady and slow

On **Frontier power law**, every tenfold budget keeps the same 70% of the loss above the floor. No point on the line speeds up or suddenly drops, so progress is predictable. But halving the loss above the floor costs about 90 times the compute.

### The two fits agree on shape, not on the bottom

Switch **Fit** at the starting run. Then **Fit optimum here** moves from 1.38B parameters on 65B tokens to 2.02B on 44.3B. Under `Hoffmann 2022`, the diamond pulls further from the square as budgets grow, toward smaller models fed more text. Under `Epoch 2024 refit`, the two stay close together, near 20 tokens per parameter.

### The crosses are predictions

The crosses marked Chinchilla and Gopher stand for real models. The legend lists their sizes as **Real runs (70B/1.4T, 280B/300B)**. But each cross sits at the loss this formula predicts for that size. It does not sit at the loss the real model reached.

## Where it breaks

### What this lab simplifies

Nothing in this lab is trained. Every readout is the published formula worked out with your numbers. So the lab shows a prediction, not what a real model would do.

The sliders also reach far past the runs the formula was fitted on. Those runs went from about 70M to 16B parameters, trained on 5B to 500B tokens. The sliders reach 1T parameters and 30T tokens, so most of the chart is extrapolation. It is like the baker reading her curve past the biggest cake she has baked.

The loss is in the formula's own units. It is measured on one particular collection of text, cut into tokens one particular way. So a loss from this lab cannot be compared with a loss reported for some other model.

The bill, 6 × N × D, is a shortcut too. It leaves out some of the work in attention, which adds up when a model reads very long passages. Real runs also lose time when machines wait on each other or restart, so the true cost is higher.

The frontier line is perfectly straight here because it is worked out from the formula itself. A real frontier is fitted to a few dozen training runs. Its uncertainty grows with each tenfold step past them.

Last, **N from dimensions** is a calculator, not a fit, and it skips the embeddings and small extras. For large models it lands a few percent under the real count. For small ones it lands much further under. The embedding table is a big share of a small model, as Going deeper shows.

### When the fit and the rule of thumb disagree

**A common mix-up:** "20 tokens per parameter" is a rule of thumb. It is not what the Chinchilla formula says at every budget. Use the formula's numbers as the paper printed them. The best split at Chinchilla's own budget is then 32B parameters on 3.0T tokens, about 93 tokens per parameter.

The Chinchilla paper worked out the best split in three ways. The printed formula was only one of them. A 2024 study by Epoch AI redid the paper's analysis. It found that the formula's numbers did not agree with the other two ways. So it fitted the data again. Switch **Fit** to `Epoch 2024 refit` and that same optimum moves to about 73B parameters on 1.34T tokens. That is about 18 tokens per parameter, which is close to 20.

The powers α and β are touchy as well. That study also lists the original powers with two more decimal places. So 0.34 becomes 0.3392 and 0.28 becomes 0.2849. That tiny change moves the same optimum from 32B to about 40B parameters. It shows how much large-scale predictions hang on small details of a fit.

### Where real scaling laws fail

Scaling laws are trends inside a tested range, not laws of nature. They only describe the kind of model and training they were measured on. Every part of the formula depends on the model's design and the mix of training text. It also depends on the way text is cut into tokens and on the training recipe. Change any of these and the whole curve shifts. So a fit made for one setup cannot be carried over to another.

Lower loss is also not the same as being more useful. Picture a spelling bee where you score a point only if you spell the whole word right. Being close earns nothing. A student who gets a few more letters right each week scores zero for weeks, then suddenly starts scoring. The student improved steadily all along.

Some AI tests work the same way and give credit only for a fully correct answer. So a smooth loss curve can sit under a test score that jumps. A skill that seems to appear suddenly at some size is called an **emergent ability**. Many reported emergent abilities depend on whether the test gives partial credit or counts only exact answers.

Good text also runs out, and how scaling behaves when it does is called **data-constrained scaling**. One study found that reading the same text four times works nearly as well as reading new text. Each pass after that is worth less.

## Going deeper

### From dimensions to N

The 12 × L × d² rule comes from counting one layer's grids, the way **The transformer block** lab does. There, the toy block has a width of 8. So it holds 12 × 8² = 768 such weights, or 840 once you count its small extras.

The rule holds up well at large scale, which you can check against the LLaMA paper. LLaMA's 6.7B model has 32 layers of width 4,096. The rule gives 6.44B, about 4% under the paper's count. Its 65.2B model has 80 layers of width 8,192, and the rule gives 64.4B, about 1% under. The missing part is what the rule leaves out. That means the embeddings, the small extras, and the exact shape of the feed-forward part.

Small models are where the rule falls furthest short, and GPT-2 small shows why. According to its published settings, it has 12 layers of width 768 and a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. Its layers hold 84.9M weights, yet its token table alone holds 38.6M. That is nearly half as much again on top.

The table grows only with the width, while the layers grow with the width squared. So the table's share falls as models get wider. Give a model the LLaMA shape above. A table of the same 50,257 tokens would add only about 3% to its count.

Papers even disagree about whether N should include that table. An early study from 2020 left it out, while the 2022 Chinchilla study counted it. A 2024 paper traced much of the gap between their two answers to that one choice. The answers were about how to split a budget. Both fits in this lab follow the 2022 study's form.

**Inference & KV cache** looks at the same two numbers when a model is answering people rather than training. While it writes an answer, the model saves a few notes for every token so far. Those notes grow with the number of layers and with the width. Some models share notes between parts of a layer to save space. For them the notes grow more slowly than the full width. So layers and width set both what a model costs to train and what it costs to run.

### Training cost is not the whole bill

Picture a bakery choosing between two ovens. One is cheap to buy but uses a lot of power on every loaf. The other costs more up front but uses far less per loaf. If you plan to bake millions of loaves, the second oven comes out cheaper in the end. It cost more on the first day, though.

Models face the same trade, because compute-optimal means best for the training bill only. A model served to millions of people runs up a new bill on every answer. Those bills can add up to more than training cost. So labs deliberately train a smaller model on far more text than the formula's best split. This is called **overtraining**.

For a model, the up-front price is paid in training, and here the oven picture stops matching. The oven's extra cost is only money, but the smaller model pays in quality too. At the same training budget it ends with a slightly higher loss. Or it needs extra training compute to reach the same loss.

Set **Budget** back to `Free`. Then set **Parameters** as close to 8B and **Training tokens** as close to 15T as the sliders allow. That means about 7.94B and 15.1T. That is roughly the recipe for Llama 3 8B. The printed fit charges about 0.026 of extra loss compared with its best split at that budget. That best split is a model of about 36B parameters. In return, the 8B model is several times cheaper to run on every answer it gives. Under `Epoch 2024 refit`, the extra loss is about 0.054, measured against a best split of about 81B.

**Inference & KV cache** showed where that running cost comes from. Every new token makes the model read through all its weights and its saved notes again. That bill comes back on every answer, for as long as the model is in use.

### Other ways to scale

Some models send each token to a few internal specialists, called experts, instead of through the whole model. Their total size can grow while the work per token stays the same. So fits for these models track three things separately. They are the total size, the part used for each token, and the number of experts.

Spending more compute at answer time has its own curves too. Sampling several answers, writing longer reasoning, and checking an answer before giving it are all kinds of **test-time compute**. To reach a given accuracy, a lab can spend less on training and more at answer time. It can also do the reverse.

The same curve-fitting trick also helps choose training settings for a big run. Labs fit how the best learning rate and the best batch size shift as compute grows. The batch size is the number of examples a model learns from in one step. Another approach sets up the model so those best settings stay the same as it gets wider.
