## How sure is a guess?

Ask what follows “the sky is ___” and the honest answer is not a word but a distribution: mostly `blue`, sometimes `grey`, occasionally something else. A language model's output is exactly that object, a probability for every candidate next token, and the loss it is trained on is a measurement of how surprised that distribution was by what actually came next. This lab builds both, from bars to bits.

### Where it shows up

Every stage of a language model's life uses the ideas here. Generation samples from a conditional distribution. Training minimizes cross-entropy between the data and the model. Evaluation reports perplexity and puts confidence intervals on benchmark scores. Distillation matches one model's distribution to another's with a KL term. Classifiers end in a softmax or a sigmoid.

### What you will be able to explain

- Why a distribution is one whole shared among outcomes, and why P(word | prompt) is one row of a count table divided by its own total.
- How an empirical tally converges on the true probabilities, and how the mean and variance of one draw set the bell curve that an average follows.
- How Bayes' rule turns a prior and a likelihood into a posterior, and why the prior cannot be skipped.
- Why surprise is −log₂ p, why its average is the entropy, and why cross-entropy is bounded below by it.
- How softmax maps logits to probabilities, what temperature rescales, and why two-class softmax is a sigmoid.

### What the lab shows

One running example, five candidate words, and a count table of 200 invented stories. Every readout is closed-form arithmetic on those authored numbers, and every “random” draw comes from a seeded generator, so the figures quoted below are what the lab prints at its defaults. Nothing is measured from a trained model; *Next-token prediction* does that.

## Build the idea, one card at a time

Each step shows one card, and each card adds one idea to the last. The lesson names the control; predict the result before you move it.

### Bars that add to one

A **probability distribution** assigns each outcome a non-negative share, and the shares sum to one. The first card draws P(blue) = 0.50, P(grey) = 0.25, P(dark) = 0.12, P(clear) = 0.08 and P(green) = 0.05 as columns, and again as a single strip that is always full: 0.50 + 0.25 + 0.12 + 0.08 + 0.05 = 1.00.

**Pause and predict:** drag `blue` to 80%. What happens to `grey`?

The remaining 20% is shared among the other four in their original proportions, so `grey`, which held half of the remainder, drops to 10%. Normalization is a constraint: raising one probability must lower others. **Show as** switches the labels to natural frequencies (50 of 100 stories), which are the same quantities and are usually easier to reason with.

### Draw from the bars

Drawing a **sample** picks an outcome with probability equal to its bar. With **Number of draws** at 1 the seeded draw is `grey`, the less likely word. At 10 draws the tally is 7, 2, 0, 1, 0, and **Biggest gap, tally to bars** reads 20.0 points. At 100 draws the gap is 9.0 points and at 1,000 it is 0.9 points, with `blue` at 507 of 1,000, or 50.7%.

That convergence is the **law of large numbers**: empirical frequencies approach the probabilities as the sample grows. It is what gives a probability its operational meaning, and it says nothing about any single draw. **New luck** reseeds the generator, which shows how much two samples of ten can differ.

### Averages pile up in a bell

Score a guesser that always says `blue` with 1 for a hit and 0 for a miss. The **expected value** (mean) of one answer is Σ p·x = 0.50, and its **variance**, the expected squared deviation from the mean, is 0.25, so the standard deviation is 0.5.

A test averages n such answers. The card simulates 2,000 tests of 25 questions: the scores pile up around 0.50 with a measured standard deviation of 0.101, against a predicted √(0.25 ÷ 25) = 0.100. The overlaid curve is the **Gaussian** (normal) with the pile's own mean and variance; about 68% of its area lies within one standard deviation of the mean.

**Pause and predict:** how many questions halve the spread?

One hundred: the variance of an average falls as 1/n, so its standard deviation falls as 1/√n. The card reads 0.050 at 100 questions and 0.025 at 400. This is the central limit theorem at work, and it is the reason a benchmark score needs an error bar. The fit is approximate: the pile is discrete, and it is skewed for small n or a mean near 0 or 1.

### Context changes the bars

Switch **Prompt** to “the grass is ___” and the distribution changes: `green` is now 70%. The table behind the bars counts 200 stories by prompt (rows) and next word (columns). Keeping one row and dividing by its total gives a **conditional probability**: P(green | the grass is) = 35 ÷ 50 = 0.70, and P(blue | the sea is) = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40.

A language model's output is this quantity for every candidate token, conditioned on the whole preceding context. It computes the distribution with a network rather than a lookup, but the object is the same.

### Read the table backwards

Now condition the other way: the next word was `blue`; which prompt preceded it? Reading down the `blue` column, 50 + 5 + 20 = 75 stories contain it and 50 of those began “the sky is”, so the answer is 50 ÷ 75 ≈ 67%, not the 50% that P(blue | the sky is) suggests.

**Bayes' rule** does the same arithmetic with shares. The **prior** is each prompt's share of all stories (0.50, 0.25, 0.25). The **likelihood** is P(blue | prompt) (0.50, 0.10, 0.40). Their products are 0.25, 0.025 and 0.10, the evidence is their sum, 0.375, and the **posterior** for the sky is 0.25 ÷ 0.375 = 0.667. The card draws the products as areas and the posterior as those areas rescaled to one.

**Pause and predict:** lower the **Prior** slider to 10%. Does the posterior for the sky change?

It falls to 18%, and “the sea is” leads with about 65%, although no likelihood changed. **A common mix-up:** reading a likelihood, P(clue | explanation), as if it were the posterior, P(explanation | clue). The two differ by the prior, which is why a rare explanation stays unlikely unless the evidence is strong.

### Surprise in bits

The **surprise** (self-information, or surprisal) of an outcome with probability p is −log₂ p, measured in **bits**. It counts halvings from certainty: 50% is 1 bit, 25% is 2 bits, and the card shows `dark` at 3.06 bits and `green` at 4.32 bits. Halving any probability adds exactly one bit.

The logarithm is there because it turns products into sums. For two independent draws, `blue` then `grey`, the probabilities multiply, 0.50 × 0.25 = 0.125, and the surprises add, 1.00 + 2.00 = 3.00 bits = −log₂ 0.125. Sums of log-probabilities stay in a usable numeric range where long products underflow, which is why losses are sums of logs.

**Try this:** drag `blue` from 50% to 25% and watch its hanging bar move from the 1-bit line to the 2-bit line, then press **Reset the bars**.

### Average surprise

**Entropy** is the expected surprise, H(p) = −Σ p log₂ p. The card draws each word as a block as wide as its probability and as tall as its surprise, so the entropy is the area, and the dashed line is the average height: 0.50 × 1.00 + 0.25 × 2.00 + 0.12 × 3.06 + 0.08 × 3.64 + 0.05 × 4.32 = 1.87 bits.

**Pause and predict:** which of a sure thing, the sky bars and five equal bars has the highest entropy?

The uniform distribution, at log₂ 5 = 2.32 bits; a sure thing has 0.00. The rare words of the sky bars have tall blocks, but those blocks are narrow. Two to the power of the entropy is the **perplexity**, the size of a fair choice that is equally uncertain: 3.67 for the sky bars, 5 for uniform, 1 for a sure thing.

### Your guess against what happens

Now outcomes follow the truth p while surprise is computed from a model q. The expected surprise is the **cross-entropy**, H(p, q) = −Σ p log₂ q, which the card calls the bill. The picture is the same blocks with widths from p and heights from q; the dashed outline shows the heights p itself would give.

With q uniform, every word costs 2.32 bits, so the bill is 2.32 against an entropy of 1.87, and 0.45 bits are wasted. That excess is the **KL divergence**, D(p‖q) = H(p, q) − H(p).

**Pause and predict:** can any q bring the bill below 1.87?

No. Sharpening q to 60% on `blue` makes `blue` cheap (0.74 bits) and the rest dear (3.32), for a bill of 2.03. The preset **confidently wrong: 96% green** costs 6.31 bits, 4.44 of them wasted. Only q = p reaches 1.87, where the divergence is 0.00. The KL divergence is non-negative and zero only at q = p (Gibbs' inequality), so minimizing cross-entropy over q is the same as pulling q toward p. That is the training objective of a language model.

### From scores to bars

A network emits unnormalized scores, **logits**, one per token. **Softmax** exponentiates each, sums, and divides: with scores 2.0, 1.3, 0.6, 0.2 and −0.3 the exponentials are 7.39, 3.67, 1.82, 1.22 and 0.74, their sum is 14.84, and P(blue) = 7.39 ÷ 14.84 = 0.498.

**Pause and predict:** press **Add 1 to every score**. Do the bars move?

They do not. The sum grows to 40.35, but every exponential is multiplied by e, so the ratios are unchanged: softmax depends only on differences between logits. **Temperature** divides every logit by T before the exponential. At T = 0.5 the gaps double and `blue` rises to 74%; at T = 2.0 they halve and `blue` falls to 34%. The ranking never changes. Temperature rescales the distribution the model already has; it adds no information.

With two classes, softmax reduces to the **sigmoid** of the logit gap, σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^−z). Press **Reset scores**, which also returns T to 1: for `blue` against `grey` the gap is 0.7 and both readouts give 66.8%. The gap is the log-odds, which is why a binary classifier can output a single logit.

## Try the challenge

The last card hides the true distribution for “the night is ___” and shows a tally of 40 seeded draws: 3, 4, 20, 11 and 2. Set q so that the KL divergence from the hidden truth is at most 0.05 bits.

The uniform starting guess wastes 0.583 bits. The winning move is the empirical distribution, counts divided by 40: bars of 7%, 10%, 50%, 28% and 5% waste 0.010 bits. That is maximum-likelihood estimation, and it is how the bigram table in *Next-token prediction* is built. The result is not zero because 40 draws estimate the truth with sampling error; **Reveal the true bars** shows `dark` at 55%, not 50%. Two further rounds use different hidden distributions.

What to notice across the cards: normalization appears three times (a row of counts, Bayes' rule, softmax), an expectation appears three times (mean score, entropy, cross-entropy), and every small sample (10 draws, 25 questions, 40 draws) misses the truth by a visible margin.

## What this picture leaves out

### What the lab simplifies

- **Authored numbers.** The count table, the hidden distributions and the logits were chosen so the arithmetic is clean. None is measured.
- **Five outcomes.** A real vocabulary has tens of thousands of tokens, most with tiny probability.
- **Seeded, independent draws.** Each draw ignores the last, unlike text. The challenge seeds were chosen so that counting meets the goal; for five outcomes and 40 draws the expected divergence of the empirical estimate is about 0.07 bits, so other seeds can miss it.
- **A 1% floor in whole percents.** No bar can reach zero, which keeps every surprise finite.
- **Idealized tests.** Simulated questions are independent with equal hit probability; real benchmark items vary in difficulty and cluster, which widens the true spread.
- **A known truth.** The lab can print H(p). For language, only H(p, q) is ever estimated, from held-out text.

### Where the real ideas run into trouble

- **Low loss is not truth.** Cross-entropy rewards assigning high probability to the evaluation text, whatever that text says.
- **Estimates have sampling error.** A loss or accuracy is a sample mean; differences smaller than its standard error are not evidence.
- **Zeros are unbounded.** An event given probability zero costs infinite surprise when it occurs, so count-based estimates need smoothing.
- **Priors are assumptions.** Outside a count table, a prior is often a judgement, and the posterior inherits it.
- **KL is asymmetric.** D(p‖q) and D(q‖p) penalize different errors, so the direction a method minimizes matters.
- **Temperature is not calibration by default.** A model's probabilities can be systematically too sharp or too flat relative to its accuracy; fitting one temperature on held-out data is a common correction.

## Where you will meet this again

- ***Next-token prediction*** shows a real model's conditional distribution, samples from it with temperature and truncation, reports surprisal and perplexity, and counts its bigram baseline as you did in the challenge.
- ***Loss & gradient descent*** and ***Train one from scratch*** minimize cross-entropy; some labs report it in nats (natural log), where 1 bit ≈ 0.69 nats.
- ***How we know a model works*** and ***Evaluation*** attach standard errors and confidence intervals to scores, for the reason the bell card shows.
- ***Classical machine learning*** uses Bayes' rule and per-class Gaussians in naive Bayes, and the sigmoid in logistic regression.
- ***Distillation & pruning*** and ***Post-training*** compare two models' distributions with a KL term, often at a raised temperature.
- ***Reinforcement learning foundations*** optimizes an expected value: the expected return of a policy.
